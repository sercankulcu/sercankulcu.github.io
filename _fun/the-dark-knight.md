I watched *The Dark Knight* in a crowded cinema a few weeks after it opened.

The room was completely full. People were still talking when the trailers ended, and there was the usual noise of a summer blockbuster audience: popcorn, late arrivals, seats folding, somebody whispering two rows behind me.

Then the Joker appeared.

The room changed.

That is one of my clearest memories of the film—not a particular explosion or chase, but the way a large audience gradually became quiet.

I had expected a Batman film.

I left thinking about systems under pressure.

The Joker is frightening not because he has a complicated political philosophy. He is frightening because he treats rules as hypotheses.

People say they are civilized.

Test it.

People say they will not hurt an innocent person.

Change the incentives.

People say institutions are stable.

Introduce enough fear.

He keeps asking the same question in different forms: how much pressure does it take before a principle becomes negotiable?

That is a disturbing question because most of us prefer to judge morality under comfortable conditions.

It is easy to believe you are generous when nothing important has to be sacrificed.

It is easy to value rules when the rules are protecting you.

It is easy to oppose violence when you are not afraid.

The film keeps removing comfort.

Batman himself becomes part of this problem. He wants to defend a system while repeatedly stepping outside that system to do it. That contradiction is what makes the character more interesting than simple heroism.

If the law cannot stop someone, how far outside the law may you go?

If surveillance can prevent an attack, does that make surveillance acceptable?

If telling the truth will destroy something socially useful, is a lie justified?

The film does not make those questions easy, and I am glad it does not.

Harvey Dent may be the most important character for this reason.

At the beginning, he represents the reassuring idea that institutions can produce legitimate heroes. Gotham does not need a masked vigilante forever if someone can fight corruption publicly and lawfully.

Then the film tests him too.

His transformation is not interesting because a good man suddenly becomes evil. It is interesting because pain gives him a new theory of fairness.

Chance.

A coin.

No responsibility.

There is something seductive about blaming randomness after the world has hurt you. If everything is chance, then nobody has to make a moral decision.

The two-ferry sequence is where the film's argument became clearest to me.

The Joker constructs an experiment and expects human beings to confirm his view of them.

For once, they refuse.

Not elegantly. Not heroically in the conventional sense.

They hesitate.

They argue.

They become afraid.

And then they do not do the terrible thing.

I remember the cinema being completely silent during that sequence.

A superhero film had somehow turned a room full of strangers into people watching other strangers decide whether strangers were worth saving.

When the credits started, the audience did not immediately return to its earlier noise.

Outside, people were discussing the Joker.

I was thinking about the ferries.

Chaos is dramatic. Destruction is easy to notice.

Restraint is quieter.

Sometimes the most important thing people do is the thing they decide not to do.
