Some people make a house feel warmer just by being there. The table is the same, the tea is the same, and the rooms are the same, but everything feels different when that person is around. Then one day they are gone, and we understand that a home is not made only of walls and furniture.

*Gülpembe* always makes me think about this kind of absence. It is not simply about missing someone. It is about discovering how deeply one person can become part of a family, a house, and even a period of our life.

## The Empty Chair

When we are children, we often believe that older people will always be there. Grandmothers stay in the kitchen, grandfathers sit in the same chair, and family gatherings seem to have the same faces every year.

Then life changes.

One chair becomes empty.

At first, everyone notices it. Later, people try not to look at it. New dinners are prepared, children grow up, walls are painted, and life continues. Yet that chair never completely disappears. It remains somewhere inside us.

Maybe this is what missing someone means: keeping a place for a person who is no longer there.

## Some People Are Like Seasons

Not everyone leaves the same mark on our lives. Some people are like rain. They come for a short time and move on. Others are like the wind. We hardly understand them before they disappear.

But some people are like spring.

They bring warmth, stories, familiar smells, food, laughter, and a feeling of safety. Losing such a person can feel like losing an entire season.

Spring comes again, of course. Trees bloom and birds return. But some springs belong only to the past.

## Ordinary Things

We rarely understand the value of ordinary moments while we are living them.

A Sunday breakfast. The sound of plates in the kitchen. A pair of slippers near the door. An older relative telling the same story again.

Sometimes we even become bored.

Years later, we may wish we could hear that same story one more time.

Memory is strange. We may forget important dates and long conversations, but remember how someone stirred their tea or the special word they used when they called us.

Memory is not a camera. It seems to save what the heart considers important.

## Do People Really Leave?

When I was younger, I thought death was simple. A person was here, and then they were not.

Later I understood that people leave in a much more complicated way.

Their habits remain. Their words remain. Their recipes remain. A son suddenly uses the same expression as his father. A granddaughter prepares the table exactly as her grandmother did.

Someone who left many years ago can still be present in a family.

Maybe this is one of the greatest things we can leave behind: not money or a name on a building, but a small part of ourselves living inside other people.

## Then We Become the Adults

One day, without noticing it, we move to the other side of the table.

We are no longer the children.

A child looks at us, and suddenly we hear ourselves saying something that an older person once said to us.

At that moment, the past and the future meet in the same room.

Perhaps this is why some people never completely disappear. We carry them in the way we speak, love, help, forgive, and take care of others.

## What Do We Leave Behind?

One day, our chair will also be empty.

Someone may remember a sentence we often used. Someone may laugh while telling a story about us. Someone may continue one of our small habits without even knowing where it came from.

The important question may not be how long we live.

Perhaps the real question is much simpler:

What do we leave inside other people?

Fear or kindness? Anger or laughter? A bad memory or a good lesson?

## Gülpembe

I cannot hear *Gülpembe* only as a song about loss. There is also gratitude inside it.

If you miss someone deeply, it means that person once existed in your life. There was a voice, a hand, a table, a season.

Now it is gone.

But it happened.

As the years pass, sadness slowly makes room for another feeling. We still think, “I wish they had not gone,” but another sentence begins to appear beside it:

“I am glad they were here.”

That sentence does not remove the pain, but it changes it.

We cannot bring people back. What we can do is protect the good things they left in us. We can call an older relative, prepare a bigger table, make peace with someone, help a child, or simply become a little kinder.

Life moves quickly. Children grow up, houses change, gardens become different, and people leave.

But love can remain for a very long time.

Sometimes many years pass, then one evening a song begins to play, and someone who has been gone for a long time suddenly feels close again.

Some people leave the house.

But they never completely leave us.
