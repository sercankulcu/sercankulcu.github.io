---
title: 'Pokemon Go And When Augmented Reality Left The Lab'
date: 2016-07-17
tags:

---

For years, augmented reality has been easy to demonstrate and surprisingly difficult to make ordinary.

Researchers can overlay digital objects on a camera image. Companies can build impressive prototypes. Technology presentations can make the future look only a product cycle away.

Most people, however, do not wake up thinking they need augmented reality.

Then Pokémon Go appeared.

Almost overnight, millions of people began using a system that combined smartphones, GPS, maps, cameras, mobile data, and a familiar fictional world. Players walked through real streets looking for virtual creatures, visited PokéStops tied to physical locations, and gathered around gyms that existed simultaneously as places on a map and destinations in a game.

The augmented-reality camera mode is only one part of the experience, but the more interesting idea is larger: the game makes software depend on where your body is in the physical world.

What interests me most is how little the terminology matters to the players.

They are not studying augmented reality. They are not discussing geographic information systems or location-aware computing.

They are trying to catch Pokémon.

This is often how technology becomes normal. The application arrives before the vocabulary.

Pokémon Go also demonstrates something programmers sometimes underestimate: important products are not always built from new components.

GPS was not new. Smartphones were not new. Cameras, maps, mobile internet, and game mechanics were not new. The novelty came from combining existing technologies around a strong idea and a fictional world that millions of people already cared about.

That is a useful engineering lesson. Innovation is sometimes less about inventing a new primitive than about arranging familiar primitives in a way that suddenly changes behavior.

And behavior changed very visibly.

People went outside because of software. Crowds appeared around locations that had never been designed to attract crowds. Players walked unfamiliar routes through their own cities. A map on a phone became a layer of motivation placed over ordinary geography.

There were problems as well. People staring at screens could forget traffic and physical surroundings. Large groups could overwhelm small public spaces. Location data created privacy questions. The servers struggled under demand.

Those problems were also evidence that the system had escaped the laboratory.

A prototype fails politely. A mass-market technology fails in public, around real streets, real crowds, and real human habits.

I suspect Pokémon Go will be remembered even if its first wave of excitement becomes much smaller. It gave a huge number of people an intuitive experience of software that responds not only to what they click, but to where they physically are.

Augmented reality may eventually move from phones to glasses or devices we have not yet adopted. It may become ordinary in education, tourism, maintenance, navigation, and work.

For now, one of its most visible ambassadors is a game.

That seems appropriate.

People often understand a new technology more quickly when they are playing with it.
