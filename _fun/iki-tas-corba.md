There are love songs that promise the world.

This one promises soup.

Maybe that is why I love it so much.

Mustafa Sandal's *İki Tas Çorba* does not imagine a grand house, expensive journeys, perfect bodies, success, or a life that other people would admire. It imagines mountains, a river, a small house, a wooden table, and two bowls of warm soup.

That is almost everything.

The older I get, the more I understand how radical such a small dream actually is.

We spend a large part of life learning to want more. A better house. A better car. A better position. More money. More recognition. Another achievement. Something larger than the thing we already have.

Then a song quietly asks a much more dangerous question:

What if enough is very small?

## A House That Has No Road

I love the fact that the imagined place does not even have a road.

It is almost unreachable.

Maybe it has to be.

The house is not really a house. It is a place outside ordinary life.

There are no meetings there. No deadlines. No people asking what you do for a living. No need to explain yourself. Nobody is measuring whether you are successful.

There is simply somebody you love.

A table.

Something warm to eat.

Perhaps this is why the fantasy feels so peaceful. It removes almost everything society has taught us to consider important.

No audience.

No competition.

No performance.

Only two people who have somehow escaped the noise.

I know real life cannot remain like this. Somebody has to buy the soup. The roof will eventually leak. People become ill. Money matters. Two people living together will disagree about ridiculous things.

Still, the fantasy says something true.

Sometimes what we want is not luxury.

It is relief.

## The Wooden Table

I keep thinking about the wooden table.

It could have been anything, but it is wooden.

Nothing polished, nothing elegant, nothing designed to impress anyone.

I imagine scratches on it.

Maybe one leg is slightly shorter than the others. Maybe somebody folded a piece of paper and put it underneath so the table would stop moving.

This is the kind of table real life happens around.

People eat there. Talk there. Become silent there. Argue there. Forgive each other there.

After enough years, the table begins to contain a history.

We usually think memories belong to people, but I think objects carry them too.

A chair can remember someone who always sat in the same place.

A cup can become impossible to throw away.

A kitchen can keep the shape of a person long after that person is gone.

Perhaps this is why the little house in the song feels real even though it never existed.

It already has memory inside it.

## Two Bowls

The most important number in the song is two.

Not one.

Not ten.

Two bowls of soup.

There is something deeply intimate about preparing food for two people. You do not need a celebration. You do not need guests. Nobody will photograph it.

It is just evening.

Maybe it is cold outside.

One person puts the bowls on the table.

The other person sits down.

That is all.

Yet I think many people spend their entire lives searching for exactly this scene while calling it by more complicated names.

Love.

Security.

Marriage.

Belonging.

Home.

Perhaps home is simply the place where somebody automatically puts out a second bowl.

## Even If You Do Not Drink It

There is a small detail in the song that I find strangely moving: the soup can be there even if the other person does not drink it.

That changes the whole image.

Love is often imagined as exchange. I do something for you, you do something for me. I give affection and expect affection back. I remember your birthday and hope you remember mine.

Fairness matters, of course.

But there are also moments when love is simply the pleasure of preparing the second bowl.

Whether it is finished or not.

You make tea because someone might want tea.

You buy something from the market because you remember they like it.

You leave the better piece for them.

These gestures are so small that nobody writes poems about them.

Maybe they should.

Grand declarations are easy to remember because they are rare.

Ordinary care is harder to notice because it happens every day.

Yet relationships probably live or die there.

Not in the flowers bought once a year.

In the second bowl.

## No One Has to Know

Another part of the song imagines a life almost outside the eyes of other people.

Nobody knows.

Nobody sees.

Nobody hears.

And even if they do, it does not matter.

This may be the part I understand more now than I would have when I was younger.

When we are young, we often want love to be visible.

We want other people to recognize that we are happy. We want photographs, stories, proof.

Later, privacy begins to feel luxurious.

There are beautiful things that become smaller when they are shown too much.

A private joke.

A quiet evening.

The way someone looks when they have just woken up.

A conversation that belongs to only two people.

Maybe love does not always need witnesses.

Perhaps some happiness becomes stronger when nobody is watching.

## One Moment or a Lifetime

The song also asks something I think about often: does it matter how long something lasts if it was real while it existed?

We naturally connect duration with value.

A relationship lasting twenty years seems more important than one lasting six months. A friendship that ends feels as if something failed.

But life is full of short things that become permanent inside us.

A summer.

A journey.

One evening.

A conversation.

Someone we knew only briefly.

Duration tells us how long something existed.

It does not tell us how deeply it entered us.

Maybe one moment and one lifetime are not as different as we imagine.

A lifetime is only many moments that happened not to separate.

## Maybe I Wanted This All Along

When I listen to *İki Tas Çorba*, I sometimes wonder whether many ambitions are substitutes for something simpler.

We say we want success.

Perhaps we want security.

We say we want money.

Perhaps we want freedom from fear.

We say we want a beautiful house.

Perhaps we want a table where we do not feel alone.

There is nothing wrong with ambition. I still want things. I still make plans. I still care about work, progress, and achievement.

But this song reminds me that none of them automatically creates the scene that matters.

You can have a large house and nobody you want to share soup with.

You can be admired by thousands and still eat alone.

You can win every competition and come home to silence.

Perhaps the difficult achievement is not becoming important.

It is building a life in which ordinary evenings feel worth living.

## What Is Wealth?

Philosophers have argued about the good life for thousands of years. Some placed happiness in virtue, some in pleasure, some in freedom, some in meaning.

I suspect the answer is embarrassingly ordinary.

Enough food.

Enough safety.

Enough time.

And somebody whose presence makes an ordinary table feel like a place worth returning to.

The problem is that “enough” is difficult to recognize.

Desire moves the finish line.

We get what we wanted and quickly discover something else to want. This ability keeps civilization moving, but it can make individual life permanently incomplete.

The little house in the song offers another possibility.

Not having everything.

Needing less.

There is freedom in that too.

## If I Could Choose the Picture

If someone asked me to choose between an image of extraordinary success and the image in this song, I do not know what I would say.

Perhaps I would still choose both.

Human beings are greedy even in philosophy.

But if I had to keep only one picture at the end, I think I understand why this song stays with me.

A small house.

Mountains somewhere around it.

A river moving without asking anything from us.

A wooden table in the garden.

Two bowls of soup.

And someone sitting across from me.

Nothing extraordinary happens.

Nobody knows we are there.

The world continues without us.

Maybe an hour passes.

Maybe a lifetime.

And for once, there is nowhere else I need to be.

Maybe happiness was never as complicated as I made it.

Maybe it was always two bowls.

One for me.

And one because you are there.
