There are love songs that promise the world.

This one promises soup.

Maybe that is why I love it so much.

Mustafa Sandal's *İki Tas Çorba* does not imagine a grand house, expensive journeys, perfect bodies, success, or a life that other people would admire. It imagines mountains, a river, a small house, a wooden table, and two bowls of warm soup.

That is almost everything.

The older I get, the more I understand how radical such a small dream actually is. We spend a large part of life learning to want more. A better house, a better car, a better position. More money, more recognition, another achievement. Something larger than the thing we already have. Then a song quietly asks a much more dangerous question: what if enough is very small?

I love the fact that the imagined place does not even have a road. It is almost unreachable. Maybe it has to be. The house is not really a house. It is a place outside ordinary life. There are no meetings there, no deadlines, no people asking what you do for a living, no need to explain yourself. There is simply somebody you love. A table. Something warm to eat. The fantasy removes almost everything society has taught me to consider important. No audience, no competition, no performance. Only two people who have somehow escaped the noise.

I know real life cannot remain like this. Somebody has to buy the soup. The roof will eventually leak. People become ill. Two people living together will disagree about ridiculous things. Still, the fantasy says something true. Sometimes what I want is not luxury. It is relief.

I keep thinking about the wooden table in the song. Nothing polished, nothing designed to impress anyone. I imagine scratches on it. Maybe one leg is slightly shorter and somebody folded a piece of paper and put it underneath. This is the kind of table real life happens around. People eat there, talk there, become silent there, argue there, forgive each other there. After enough years, the table begins to contain a history. Objects carry memory too. A cup can become impossible to throw away. A kitchen can keep the shape of a person long after that person is gone.

The most important number in the song is two. Not one, not ten. Two bowls of soup. There is something deeply intimate about preparing food for two people. No celebration. No guests. Nobody will photograph it. It is just evening. Maybe it is cold outside. One person puts the bowls on the table. The other person sits down. That is all.

Yet I think many people spend their entire lives searching for exactly this scene while calling it by more complicated names. Love. Security. Home.

Perhaps home is simply the place where somebody automatically puts out a second bowl.

There is a small detail in the song that I find strangely moving: the soup can be there even if the other person does not drink it. Love is often imagined as exchange. But there are also moments when love is simply the pleasure of preparing the second bowl, whether it is finished or not. You make tea because someone might want tea. You leave the better piece for them. These gestures are so small that nobody writes poems about them. Grand declarations are easy to remember because they are rare. Ordinary care is harder to notice because it happens every day. Yet relationships probably live or die there. Not in the flowers bought once a year. In the second bowl.

When I was younger, I wanted love to be visible. I wanted photographs, stories, proof. Later, privacy began to feel luxurious. There are beautiful things that become smaller when shown too much. A private joke. A quiet evening. The way someone looks when they have just woken up. A conversation that belongs to only two people. Maybe love does not always need witnesses.

When I listen to *İki Tas Çorba*, I sometimes wonder whether many ambitions are substitutes for something simpler. We say we want success. Perhaps we want security. We say we want a beautiful house. Perhaps we want a table where we do not feel alone. You can have a large house and nobody you want to share soup with. You can be admired by thousands and still eat alone.

If someone asked me to choose between an image of extraordinary success and the image in this song, I do not know what I would say. Perhaps I would still choose both. Human beings are greedy even in philosophy. But if I had to keep only one picture at the end:

A small house. Mountains somewhere around it. A river moving without asking anything from us. A wooden table in the garden. Two bowls of soup. And someone sitting across from me.

Nothing extraordinary happens. Nobody knows we are there. The world continues without us.

And for once, there is nowhere else I need to be.

Maybe happiness was never as complicated as I made it.

Maybe it was always two bowls.

One for me.

And one because you are there.
