I watched *Memento* with a notebook beside me.

I thought this was clever.

At first I wrote names, arrows and small notes about chronology. I intended to reconstruct the film faster than it wanted to reveal itself. After twenty minutes, the page looked like evidence prepared by a detective with poor handwriting.

Eventually I stopped.

That was when the film became more interesting.

Leonard cannot reliably form new long-term memories, so he externalizes memory into photographs, notes and tattoos. The premise immediately appealed to the technical part of my mind. If internal storage is unreliable, create persistence somewhere else.

Document.

Label.

Store.

The weakness is obvious only after the system begins operating.

A record is no more truthful than the information written into it.

This distinction matters far beyond Leonard's condition. We trust written things because they look stable: logs, messages, photographs, meeting minutes, databases. Stability feels objective.

But every record begins with selection.

What was worth writing down?

What was ignored?

What interpretation was already embedded in the label?

A photograph can be accurate and still mislead. A note can be permanent and wrong. A tattoo may look authoritative because it cannot easily be erased, but permanence is not truth.

The film's reverse structure forces the audience into Leonard's weakness. We enter scenes without the cause that would normally organize our judgment. A person seems trustworthy, then suspicious, then trustworthy again because new context keeps arriving from the direction in which ordinary stories would have already supplied it.

This made me aware of how much moral confidence depends on chronology.

Remove the previous five minutes and personality becomes harder to classify.

The most disturbing part is Leonard's ability to manipulate his own system. He depends on records precisely because he cannot check them against memory, which means past Leonard can give future Leonard instructions that will feel objective.

The person who most desperately needs reliable evidence can manufacture the evidence that will later control him.

That is a frightening loop.

After the film, I looked through the notebook I had used. The facts were mostly correct. The interpretations were not.

I had written “trustworthy?” beside one name and “liar?” beside another. The punctuation marks made me laugh. My notes had preserved not truth but my confidence at various stages of incomplete knowledge.

That is what memory often does too.

We remember not only events but previous interpretations of events. A story repeated enough times can become indistinguishable from the raw material that produced it.

Leonard's condition makes the philosophical question impossible to avoid: if memory changes, what makes a person continuous? The body? The purpose? The records? The story a person tells about who they have been?

All of us build identity partly from sentences beginning with “I have always” and “I am the kind of person who.”

Those are records too.

When *Memento* ended, the notebook beside me contained the plot.

The film had made the plot the least interesting thing on the page.
