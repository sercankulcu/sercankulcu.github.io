I watched *The Pianist* alone on a Sunday evening.

I had expected a film about music during war.

What I remember most is silence.

That surprised me.

The film follows Władysław Szpilman, a pianist trying to survive the destruction of Jewish life in Warsaw during the Second World War.

At the beginning, music belongs to his profession.

Later, survival reduces life to much more basic questions.

Where can I hide?

Is there food?

Who can be trusted?

Can I move without being seen?

The piano remains.

But often he cannot touch it.

That image stayed with me more than almost anything else.

A musician sitting near an instrument he cannot play.

Normally we think of art as expression.

In the film, survival sometimes requires the opposite.

Do not make a sound.

Do not attract attention.

Do not reveal yourself.

The thing that gives a person identity can become too dangerous to express.

That is a terrible kind of silence.

The film also changed the way I thought about survival stories.

We like survival to contain heroism.

A clever decision.

A brave action.

A person overcoming impossible odds through willpower.

*The Pianist* is less comfortable.

Szpilman survives through courage, certainly, but also through luck, help, timing, accident, and the decisions of other people.

That matters.

Survival is not always evidence that the survivor was stronger or better.

Sometimes one person survives because a door opens and another person does not because it remains closed.

That randomness is difficult to accept.

It feels unfair because it is unfair.

The ruins of Warsaw become another character in the film.

As the city is destroyed, human life seems to become smaller.

Rooms disappear.

Streets disappear.

Neighbors disappear.

Civilization does not vanish in one dramatic moment.

It is dismantled.

That gradual destruction frightened me.

We usually experience cities as stable.

Buildings feel permanent because they are older than our daily problems.

The film shows how quickly permanence can become rubble.

Then there is the scene with the German officer and the piano.

It would have been easy to make that moment sentimental.

Instead, it feels fragile.

Music does not erase the war.

It does not turn everyone into good people.

It creates a brief recognition between two human beings inside a system designed to destroy recognition.

That is smaller than redemption.

Perhaps that is why it works.

When the film ended, I noticed the sounds in my own room.

A computer fan.

Traffic outside.

Someone moving in another apartment.

Ordinary noise.

For a while it felt luxurious.

We rarely think of the ability to make noise as freedom.

The film made me understand that silence can mean many things.

Peace.

Attention.

Fear.

Hiding.

Absence.

And sometimes survival.
