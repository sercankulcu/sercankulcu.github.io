There are relationships in which I slowly discover that I am arguing with someone who is not in the room.

An old lover is there. A betrayal from years ago is there. A humiliation is there. Perhaps a childhood fear too.

I arrive later, knowing nothing about any of them, and somehow I am asked to pay the bill.

This is what Mustafa Sandal's *Adı İntikamdı* makes me think about. The narrator tries to understand why someone could not trust him despite his effort. Instead of ending with anger, he begins searching for an explanation. Perhaps she had a wound before him. Perhaps what looked like cruelty was really an old form of revenge. Perhaps she was trying to forget someone else.

That possibility is much more disturbing than simple betrayal. If someone hurts us because they hate us, at least we are part of the story. But what if we were only standing in the wrong place?

Relationships rarely begin from zero. Both people arrive carrying evidence from older lives. Someone who was betrayed may enter the next relationship looking for betrayal. Someone who was abandoned may become suspicious of distance. Someone who once loved too easily may later become proud of loving nobody. Then a new person arrives, having committed no crime. The trial begins.

This is one of the unfair things about intimacy: sometimes we are punished for what someone else did. I answer the message late, and suddenly the problem is not the message. I want a quiet evening, and somehow it becomes evidence that I am losing interest. I make an ordinary mistake, but the reaction belongs to a much larger history. At first I try to explain myself. Later I realize I am defending myself against accusations that were written before I arrived.

The song holds this uncertainty carefully. It does not say "I know why she did this." It says: maybe there was a wound. That *maybe* matters. We become very good at inventing explanations for people who hurt us. And sometimes the explanation becomes a reason to stay when we should not. A wound can explain cruelty. It cannot automatically justify it. This distinction took me a long time to understand. Empathy is not the obligation to remain where someone keeps hurting you.

Emotional revenge often loses its original address. The person who caused the wound disappears, yet the anger remains. Years later, somebody completely different receives it. A person abandoned once may later abandon first. A person deceived once may begin deceiving. Then the wound reproduces itself. Nobody planned this transformation. It simply happened slowly enough to feel reasonable.

There is a romantic idea I distrust more as I get older: that the right person can heal another person. Sometimes love certainly helps. A patient relationship can teach someone trust again. But a lover is not a hospital. Another person cannot perform all the work that someone refuses to do themselves. Damaged people can be extremely attractive. Their distance looks mysterious. I imagine that if they finally trust me, it will prove my love was exceptional. So I try harder. Then absurdly patient. Eventually I am proud of surviving things I should never have accepted.

The song uses the image of something going dark inside, like a falling star. Heartbreak often feels exactly like this — not because the entire world becomes dark but because one source of light suddenly disappears. The strange thing is that another light eventually appears. No person will be exactly the same. Life does not require them to be.

Perhaps one of the most important ethical responsibilities in life is this: do not make another person pay for a wound they did not create. We all inherit emotional habits from experience. We become careful because someone once punished openness. Protection can become aggression if we never examine it.

But there is a difficult truth on the other side. Loving someone does not give me responsibility for repairing their entire history. I can understand their wounds, respect them, and be patient with them. But there must eventually be reciprocity. Love cannot remain one person explaining and the other person injuring.

Perhaps maturity begins when I can say two things at the same time:

I understand why you became this way.

And I still cannot allow you to treat me this way.

Both sentences can be true.

That may be the most difficult lesson hidden inside *Adı İntikamdı*. Sometimes I am hurt by someone who was already hurting before we met. Their pain is real. So is mine. Understanding the first does not require denying the second. And maybe the only way to stop emotional revenge from moving from one person to another is for somebody, finally, to refuse to pass it on.
