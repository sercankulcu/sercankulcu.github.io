Some people leave your life quietly. Others have to be cut out.

*El Adamı* makes me think about the second kind.

There are relationships that become so mixed with your identity that ending them feels almost physical. You do not only lose the person. You lose routines, habits, places, versions of yourself, and even the way you used to look at the future. That is why sometimes a small action can feel bigger than it really is.

Cutting your hair. Changing a room. Throwing away something you kept for years.

These actions do not erase the past, but they tell the body what the mind has finally accepted: something is over.

I understand that need.

There have been times when I wanted a visible sign that a period of my life had ended. Something simple enough to do with my hands because the emotional part was much harder to control.

Maybe this is why the image of cutting hair feels so strong to me. Hair grows slowly, stays with us for years, carries time without asking questions. Cutting it can feel like taking control of something after spending too long feeling powerless.

But *El Adamı* is not only about pain.

There is pride in it too.

The loneliness here does not feel like a person sitting in an empty room and begging somebody to return. It feels almost chosen. There is sadness, of course, but also a decision not to belong to someone simply because they want you.

I think that difference matters.

Being alone and choosing solitude are not the same thing.

When somebody leaves us, loneliness can initially feel like punishment. The evenings become longer. The phone becomes quieter. Places that once belonged to two people suddenly seem unnecessarily large.

Then, slowly, something changes.

You begin to discover that silence also has space in it.

You can decide where to go. You can change your mind without explaining yourself. You can spend an evening doing absolutely nothing and nobody needs to approve of it.

Freedom is not always exciting.

Sometimes it is simply peaceful.

The song also makes love feel dangerous. There are cliffs and deep places in its emotional world. I like that, because love has never seemed completely safe to me.

To love someone seriously is to accept risk.

You give another person access to parts of yourself they could hurt. You trust them with insecurities they could use against you. You allow their presence to become important enough that their absence may one day cause pain.

Yet we still do it.

Maybe that is because a life without risk is not necessarily a full life.

The problem begins when danger becomes the relationship itself.

There is a difference between taking an emotional risk for love and constantly being damaged in the name of love. I think most of us learn this distinction later than we should.

Patience can also become dangerous.

We are taught that patience is a virtue, especially with people we love. Give them time. Try to understand. Forgive. Wait.

Sometimes that is exactly what love needs.

But patience has an end.

There comes a moment when something inside you finally breaks, and strangely, that breaking can feel like freedom.

You no longer need another explanation.

You no longer need to win.

You simply know.

I have come to respect that moment.

Not because endings are easy, but because there is dignity in recognizing when enough is enough.

Perhaps that is what stays with me most in *El Adamı*. The person in the song is wounded, but she is not powerless. She can love deeply and still leave. She can walk close to the edge without allowing somebody else to decide where she goes.

That is a kind of strength I admire.

Maybe we all need to learn it eventually.

To love without becoming owned.

To stay while staying is honest.

And when the time comes, to cut what needs to be cut and recognize ourselves in the mirror again.
