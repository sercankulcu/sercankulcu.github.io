I watched *The Incredibles* expecting a children's film.

I ended up thinking about work.

That was unexpected.

Bob Parr has a problem that looks ridiculous because he is a superhero.

He is good at something he is no longer allowed to do.

So he goes to an office.

The office is safe.

Stable.

Ordinary.

He hates it.

That situation is not limited to superheroes.

There is a particular frustration in having an ability that has no place to go.

You can be competent and still feel useless.

That is different from failure.

Failure says you cannot do the thing.

Frustration says you can, but the environment does not want it.

Bob becomes nostalgic for the period when his abilities mattered.

Nostalgia is dangerous because it edits.

He remembers excitement.

Purpose.

Recognition.

He forgets cost.

That is why returning to superhero work feels irresistible.

The film understands the temptation of becoming your old self again.

But the real story is not about Bob recovering his identity.

It is about discovering that identity has changed.

He is no longer only Mr. Incredible.

He is a husband.

A father.

Part of a system where other people's lives are connected to his choices.

That makes heroism more complicated.

When he acts alone, he is trying to return to the past.

When the family acts together, something new becomes possible.

I liked that difference.

The Parr family is not effective because everyone has the same power.

Their abilities are almost metaphors for personality.

Helen stretches because she is constantly holding the family together across impossible distances.

Violet disappears and creates shields.

Dash cannot stop moving.

Jack-Jack appears simple until he turns out to contain far more than anyone expected.

A family is a team whose members did not choose one another based on complementary skills.

That makes cooperation harder and more interesting.

The film also says something uncomfortable about exceptional ability.

Syndrome's resentment begins with exclusion.

He wants to be special too.

Later, his answer is to make specialness meaningless.

“When everyone's super, no one will be.”

That line can be read in many ways.

What interested me was the difference between equality and sameness.

Treating people fairly does not require pretending they have identical abilities.

Some people are better at some things.

That fact can be uncomfortable.

The challenge is building a society where difference does not become entitlement.

The film does not solve that problem.

It hides it inside capes and explosions.

Still, it is there.

When the movie ended, I realized why it had worked so well for adults.

Children could watch superheroes.

Adults could watch a marriage.

Two people trying to remember why they chose each other while work, disappointment, children, secrecy, and routine accumulate around them.

The superpowers make the family unusual.

The problems make them ordinary.

That combination may be the real trick of the film.
