title: 'Movie Suggestions For Software Engineers'
date: 2019-08-13

Software engineers already spend enough of their lives in front of screens, so recommending more screen time sounds slightly irresponsible.

Still, films can show something technical books rarely do well: the human mess around technology.

I am not especially interested in films because they teach programming accurately. Most do not. They compress timelines, invent conversations, simplify technical work, and turn long periods of frustration into scenes that fit inside two hours.

What they can show well is ambition, obsession, rivalry, failure, compromise, and the strange kinds of people who become attached to difficult problems.

*The Imitation Game* is a dramatized film built around Alan Turing and wartime codebreaking. I would not use it as a history lesson, but it is useful as a reminder that some of the most important technical work happens under conditions that have little to do with the clean, rational image we often attach to computing.

*Hidden Figures* looks at African-American women mathematicians who worked at NASA. What stayed with me is not only the mathematics, but the way technical achievement can remain invisible when institutions decide whose work is worth remembering.

*The Social Network* is barely about programming in the narrow sense. It is about ambition, friendship, ownership, status, and what happens when a technical project grows faster than the relationships around it can survive. That makes it relevant to software engineering in a way that a film full of code on screens might not be.

*Pirates of Silicon Valley* looks back at the early history and rivalry around Apple and Microsoft. It is dated in some ways, but the personalities, competition, opportunism, and mythology around the personal-computer industry remain interesting.

Documentaries often come closer to the culture of technical work. *Revolution OS* covers GNU, Linux, free software, and the open-source movement. It captures a period when ideas about software freedom were becoming part of a much larger public conversation.

*Indie Game: The Movie* is about games, but I think many programmers will recognize the emotional structure of the work immediately: long periods of effort, tiny teams, uncertainty, obsession with details, and the uncomfortable moment when something deeply personal has to be released into the world and judged by strangers.

None of these films will make anyone a better programmer by themselves.

That is not why I watch them.

Technical work is easy to describe as systems, algorithms, languages, architectures, and tools. All of those matter. But behind every system are people who become tired, competitive, proud, insecure, stubborn, inspired, and occasionally obsessed.

They build things for reasons that are not always rational.

They make mistakes.

They disagree.

They fail.

And sometimes the technology that survives is inseparable from the personalities that created it.

That human layer is what I look for when I watch films about technology.
