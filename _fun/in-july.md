title: "In July: Sometimes the Destination Is Beside You"
date: 2002-08-29
tags:

There are films we remember because they are great films, and there are others we remember because they entered our lives at the right moment. *In July* belongs to the second group for me. I do not remember watching it and thinking mainly about cinematography, structure, or whether every scene was perfectly written. What remained was something less technical and more difficult to explain: summer, movement, uncertainty, and the strange feeling of traveling toward something important without fully understanding what it is.

Fatih Akın's *In July* is a romantic road movie that begins in Germany and moves toward Istanbul. Daniel is a young teacher living a careful and predictable life. He meets Juli, who is already interested in him, but he does not really understand what she means to him. Instead, a series of coincidences leads him to believe that another woman, Melek, may be the person he is supposed to find. That belief pushes him onto the road, and Juli eventually becomes part of the journey.

On paper, the story sounds almost too simple. A man follows the wrong woman and slowly realizes that someone important was already near him. Yet this simplicity is one of the reasons the film works. It takes a familiar romantic idea and places it inside movement, borders, accidents, missed chances, bad decisions, and ordinary foolishness. The result feels less like a carefully designed romantic fantasy and more like life itself, where meaning often appears only after confusion.

What I like most is the idea that the wrong person can still lead us in the right direction. Daniel begins his journey because he thinks he knows what he wants, but he does not. He is wrong about the destination, yet the movement itself changes him. Without that mistake, he might have remained exactly where he was, living the same safe life and seeing the same people without really noticing them.

We usually divide decisions into correct and incorrect ones. A correct decision is supposed to take us directly toward a good result, while a wrong decision is treated as wasted time. Life is rarely that clean. A failed relationship can teach us what we actually need from love. A job we later regret can introduce us to people who change us. A city we dislike can help us understand where we belong. Sometimes the mistake is not outside the path. It is part of the path.

This is one of the quiet philosophical ideas inside *In July*. Daniel needs the illusion before he can reach the truth. He has to believe strongly in something that is not quite right because that belief gives him enough energy to leave his familiar world. The film does not present wisdom as knowing everything in advance. Wisdom comes later, after movement, confusion, embarrassment, risk, and experience.

Juli is already close to Daniel from the beginning, and this creates the central irony of the film. He can see her, but he cannot yet recognize what he sees. There is a large difference between seeing and recognizing. We see people every day without understanding their importance. Familiarity can make someone less visible because we assume that what is close will remain close.

Distance works differently. What is far away can easily become ideal because we know less about it. Imagination fills the empty spaces. A person in another city, a future we have not lived, or a relationship that has not yet begun can remain perfect because reality has not had time to damage the fantasy. What is close to us has to survive ordinary life, repetition, bad moods, silence, routine, and boredom. Strangely, this can make the distant person seem more meaningful than the person who is actually present.

This is why we sometimes understand value only after distance appears. A friend moves away, a relationship ends, a familiar place disappears, or someone who was always available becomes part of another life. Then attention arrives. The thing itself may not have changed at all; only our ability to see it has changed.

The sun symbol in the film is playful but also important. Juli gives Daniel a ring with a sun and suggests that it will lead him toward the woman meant for him. Daniel later connects the symbol with Melek and interprets the coincidence as a sign. It is almost childish, but people are naturally hungry for signs. We want the universe to speak to us because uncertainty is difficult to carry.

A coincidence feels more beautiful when we call it destiny. A random meeting becomes more meaningful if we believe there was a reason for it. A song playing at the right moment seems personal. A small object becomes a message. Rationally, we know that the mind is very good at creating patterns, but emotionally we still want those patterns to mean something.

Perhaps we sometimes search for signs because choosing is frightening. If the universe has already chosen, then we do not have to carry the full responsibility of the decision. The sign tells us to move, and movement becomes easier. The sign itself may be meaningless, but what happens because we follow it may not be meaningless at all.

This connects naturally with the road-movie structure. Every good road movie contains two journeys. One is geographical and can be followed on a map. The other happens inside the traveler. Daniel crosses countries and borders, but the more important distance is between the person he is at the beginning and the person he becomes by the end.

At first, Daniel does not seem made for adventure. He appears cautious, controlled, and attached to routine. This is exactly why the journey matters. A fearless person taking risks tells us very little, but when a careful person begins improvising, trusting strangers, crossing borders, getting lost, and accepting uncertainty, we can see change happening.

Travel has always had this philosophical quality. When we remain in the same place, the world helps us maintain our identity. We sleep in the same bed, walk through familiar streets, speak the same language, and understand the rules around us. Travel removes these supports. Suddenly we have to ask questions, misunderstand people, wait, improvise, and depend on strangers. We become slightly foolish again, and perhaps this is healthy.

We spend years constructing stable versions of ourselves. We learn what we like, what we avoid, how we behave, and what kind of person we believe ourselves to be. Then a journey places us somewhere where those habits do not work very well. For a while, identity becomes less certain. That uncertainty can be uncomfortable, but it can also create room for change.

Borders in *In July* are therefore more than geographical lines. There are borders between friendship and love, certainty and doubt, planning and accident, safety and risk. Daniel spends much of the film in a middle state. He is no longer the person who stayed safely at home, but he has not yet become the person who understands why he left.

Many important periods of life happen in exactly this kind of middle. We leave something before we know where we are going. A relationship ends before another life has begun. We move to a new city before it feels like home. We stop believing something before we know what will replace it. We usually want to escape this uncertainty as quickly as possible, but the middle is often where transformation actually happens.

The film also made me think about the relationship between love and attention. I used to think love was mainly about finding the right person. That now seems incomplete. The right person can stand in front of us and still remain invisible if our attention is somewhere else.

There are moments when somebody gives us exactly what we later realize we wanted, but at that time we are not capable of receiving it. Timing matters because people are constantly changing. The person we could not understand at twenty-five may be exactly the person we would understand at thirty-five. The reverse can also happen. Someone who once seemed perfect may later feel completely wrong because both people have changed.

We do not simply meet people. We meet them at particular moments in our lives. This makes love partly a question of timing, but not in the mystical sense that there is one perfect date written somewhere in the universe. Timing matters because perception changes. Juli does not only need Daniel to see her; Daniel needs to become capable of seeing her.

The road performs that work on him.

The title also matters: *In July*. Not *Destiny*, not *The Journey*, not *The Woman of My Dreams*. Just July. A month. There is something temporary about summer, and perhaps this is one reason summer stories remain in memory so easily. Their beauty already contains the knowledge that they will end.

Long evenings, open windows, warm streets, travel, unexpected meetings, and impulsive decisions all feel slightly outside ordinary life. Summer seems to create permission. People do things they might not do in November. They become more open to accidents, strangers, movement, and possibility.

Maybe this is why summer romances have such a strong place in memory. They do not always need to become permanent in order to matter. We often make the mistake of measuring experiences only by duration. A ten-year relationship seems more meaningful than one that lasted three months. A friendship that disappeared can feel like a failure. A beautiful period that ended becomes something we think we lost.

But duration and value are not the same thing. A single summer can change the direction of a life. A conversation lasting twenty minutes can remain in memory for decades. A person can be temporary and still be important.

*In July* understands this lightness. It does not treat every emotional mistake as tragedy. People misunderstand signs, make bad decisions, behave foolishly, become jealous, get lost, and continue moving. The film allows life to be ridiculous without making it meaningless. This balance is one of the things I love about it.

There is also the question of fate. Is *In July* really a film about destiny? In one sense, yes, but I prefer a less mystical interpretation. Perhaps fate is simply the name we give to coincidence after coincidence becomes meaningful.

Imagine two strangers meeting accidentally. At that moment, the event may mean almost nothing. If they never meet again, it disappears among thousands of other forgotten moments. But if they later spend their lives together, both of them will return to that first accidental meeting again and again. The event itself has not changed. Its meaning has.

The future rewrites the past. We begin saying things like, "If I had left five minutes earlier," or "If she had chosen another café," or "If I had not gone there that day." We slowly discover necessity inside accident. Maybe fate is created backwards. We do not first experience destiny and then live the story. We live the story, and later we look back and discover destiny inside it.

I think the film understands this very well. Its coincidences are exaggerated, but they belong to its worldview. Life is chaotic, and meaning often comes later.

Then there is Istanbul, the destination toward which the journey moves. For a story beginning in Germany, Istanbul is not just another city on the map. It feels almost mythical because so much expectation has been placed on it. Europe has been crossed, borders have been passed, and the ordinary world has become distant. Istanbul represents arrival, but arrival does not produce the answer Daniel expected.

This is another thing travel teaches very effectively. We imagine destinations before reaching them. Paris, Istanbul, Rome, New York, Tokyo, or even a small town where somebody we love lives can become much larger in imagination than in reality. The imagined place carries our expectations, but the real place knows nothing about them.

You arrive, and the city continues being a city. Traffic moves. People go to work. Someone argues. Someone drinks tea. Someone misses a bus. Nothing stops because your personal story has reached what feels like its most important point.

There is something humbling in that. We can travel thousands of kilometers toward what feels like the center of our existence and discover that the world has no center. Perhaps only then do we begin to notice the person who has been traveling beside us.

I have seen films that are more serious than *In July*, films that are more technically ambitious, more visually impressive, or more intellectually complex. Yet liking a film is not an academic competition. Some films simply find a permanent room inside us.

*In July* has one in me because it believes in adventure without becoming cynical about romance. It accepts coincidence without demanding that we take it too seriously. It understands how foolish people become when they think they are in love, but it never treats that foolishness with cruelty. Most importantly, it suggests that being lost is not always a disaster.

Daniel thinks he is traveling to find a woman. I think he is actually traveling to become the man who can recognize Juli. That is a much more interesting journey.

Maybe this is also why certain people enter our lives before we are ready for them. They are there, we see them, we talk to them, and perhaps we even like them, but recognition has not happened yet. Then life takes us somewhere. We fail, travel, lose things, become embarrassed, become braver, and return with a slightly different way of looking.

Suddenly, the familiar person seems different.

Perhaps they did not change at all.

The distance was inside us.

I do not think every film needs to teach a lesson, and I do not want to force *In July* into one. What it leaves me with is not a rule but an image: a road, a person moving toward something he believes is important, another person moving beside him, countries passing, plans failing, coincidences accumulating, and the destination slowly approaching while the real answer has been traveling next to him the entire time.

There is something comforting in that image because we are all probably traveling toward some wrong destinations. We want people who may be wrong for us. We chase ideas that may disappoint us. We believe in signs that may mean nothing. We take roads we later question.

But being wrong about the destination does not necessarily make the journey useless.

Sometimes we have to travel a very long way to see what was close. Sometimes we need a foreign country to understand home. Sometimes we need the wrong love to understand love.

And sometimes, at the end of a long road, the discovery is surprisingly simple:

the person you were searching for was already traveling beside you.
