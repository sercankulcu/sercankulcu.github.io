---
title: "In July: Sometimes the Destination Is Beside You"
date: 2002-08-29
tags:

There are films we remember because they are great films, and there are others we remember because they entered our lives at the right moment. *In July* belongs to the second group for me. I do not remember it mainly because of technique, structure, or cinematic perfection. What remained was a feeling: summer, movement, coincidence, uncertainty, and the strange experience of traveling toward something important without fully understanding what it is.

Fatih Akın's *In July* begins with Daniel, a young teacher whose life is safe, quiet, and predictable. He meets Juli, who is already interested in him, but he does not really see what she means. Instead, a chain of coincidences convinces him that another woman may be the person he is supposed to find. That belief sends him on a journey from Germany toward Istanbul, and Juli eventually becomes part of the road.

On paper, the story is almost too simple. A man follows the wrong woman and slowly realizes that someone important was already close to him. But this simplicity is exactly what gives the film its emotional power. The journey becomes less about finding someone and more about becoming capable of seeing what was there from the beginning.

I like the idea that the wrong person can still lead us in the right direction. Daniel begins the journey because he misunderstands his own desire. Yet without that mistake, he might have remained exactly where he was. His error creates movement, and movement creates change.

We usually think of decisions as correct or incorrect. A good decision is supposed to take us toward a good result, while a bad decision is treated as wasted time. Life is rarely that clean. A failed relationship may teach us what we need from love. A job we regret may introduce us to people who change us. A city we dislike may help us understand where we belong. Sometimes the mistake is not outside the path. It is part of the path.

This is one of the philosophical ideas I find in *In July*. Daniel needs the illusion before he can reach the truth. He has to believe strongly in something that is not quite right because that belief gives him enough courage to leave his ordinary life. Wisdom comes later, after confusion, embarrassment, risk, and experience.

Juli is already close to him from the beginning, and this creates the central irony of the film. He can see her, but he cannot yet recognize what he sees. There is a large difference between seeing and recognizing. Familiarity can make a person less visible because we assume that what is close will always remain close.

Distance has the opposite effect. What is far away can easily become ideal because imagination fills the empty spaces. A person we barely know can seem perfect. A city we have not visited can become magical. A future we have not lived can look complete because reality has not yet had the chance to damage it.

This is why we sometimes understand value only after distance appears. A friend moves away, a relationship ends, a familiar place disappears, and attention suddenly arrives. The thing itself may not have changed. Our ability to see it has.

The sun symbol in the film plays with this human need for signs. We want the universe to speak because uncertainty is difficult to carry. A coincidence feels more beautiful when we call it destiny. A random meeting becomes more meaningful if we believe it happened for a reason.

Maybe we search for signs because choosing is frightening. If the universe chooses for us, then we do not have to carry the full responsibility of the decision. The sign may be meaningless, but what happens because we follow it may not be.

This connects naturally with the road-movie structure. Every good road movie contains two journeys. One can be followed on a map. The other happens inside the traveler.

Daniel crosses countries and borders, but the important distance is between the person he is at the beginning and the person he becomes by the end. At first, he seems cautious and attached to routine. The road slowly removes the supports that keep that identity stable. He has to improvise, trust strangers, misunderstand situations, take risks, and accept uncertainty.

Travel often does this to us. When we stay in the same place, the world helps us remain the person we already know. We sleep in the same bed, walk through familiar streets, and understand the rules around us. Travel makes us slightly foolish again, and perhaps that is healthy. It reminds us that identity is not as fixed as we imagine.

The film is also full of borders that are not geographical. There are borders between friendship and love, certainty and doubt, planning and accident, safety and risk. Daniel spends much of the story in a middle state. He is no longer the man who stayed safely at home, but he has not yet become the person who understands why he left.

Many important periods of life happen in this middle. A relationship ends before another life has begun. We leave a city before the new one feels like home. We stop believing something before we know what will replace it. We usually want to escape uncertainty quickly, but transformation often happens there.

The film also made me think about the relationship between love and attention. I used to think love was mainly about finding the right person. That now feels incomplete. The right person can stand in front of us and still remain invisible if our attention is somewhere else.

We do not simply meet people. We meet them at particular moments in our lives. The person we could not understand at twenty-five may be exactly the person we would understand ten years later. Or the opposite may happen. Timing matters because people are always changing.

Juli does not only need Daniel to see her. Daniel needs to become capable of seeing her.

The road does that.

The title also matters: *In July*. Not *Destiny*, not *The Journey*, not *The Woman of My Dreams*. Just July. A month. There is something temporary about summer, and perhaps this is why summer stories remain in memory so easily. Their beauty already contains the knowledge that they will end.

Long evenings, open windows, warm streets, travel, unexpected meetings, and impulsive decisions all feel slightly outside ordinary life. Summer creates permission. People take risks they might avoid in another season.

Maybe this is why summer romances have such a strong place in memory. They do not always need to become permanent to matter. We often make the mistake of measuring experiences only by duration, but duration and value are not the same thing. A single summer can change the direction of a life.

*In July* understands this lightness. It does not treat every emotional mistake as tragedy. People misunderstand signs, make bad decisions, become jealous, get lost, and continue moving. The film allows life to be ridiculous without making it meaningless.

There is also the question of fate. I do not think the film needs a mystical idea of destiny. A simpler interpretation is enough: fate may be the name we give to coincidence after coincidence becomes meaningful.

Two people can meet accidentally, and at that moment the event means almost nothing. If they never meet again, it disappears. But if they later build a life together, that first meeting becomes important. The event has not changed. Its meaning has.

The future rewrites the past.

We begin saying, "If I had left five minutes earlier," or "If she had chosen another place." We discover necessity inside accident. Maybe fate is created backwards. We live the story first, and only later do we find destiny inside it.

Then there is Istanbul. For a journey beginning in Germany, Istanbul feels almost mythical because so much expectation has been placed on it. It represents arrival, but arrival does not produce the answer Daniel expected.

This is another thing travel teaches very well. We imagine destinations before reaching them. The imagined city carries our hopes, but the real city knows nothing about them. We arrive, and life continues normally. Traffic moves. People go to work. Someone drinks tea. Someone misses a bus.

There is something humbling in this. We can travel thousands of kilometers toward what feels like the center of our existence and discover that the world has no center.

Perhaps only then do we begin to notice the person who has been traveling beside us.

I have seen films that are more serious, more ambitious, or more technically impressive than *In July*. But liking a film is not an academic competition. Some films simply find a permanent room inside us.

This one has one in me because it believes in adventure without becoming cynical about romance. It accepts coincidence without demanding that we take it too seriously. It understands how foolish people become when they think they are in love, but it never treats that foolishness with cruelty.

Most importantly, it suggests that being lost is not always a disaster.

Daniel thinks he is traveling to find a woman. I think he is actually traveling to become the man who can recognize Juli. That is a much more interesting journey.

Maybe this is also why certain people enter our lives before we are ready for them. We see them, talk to them, perhaps even like them, but recognition has not happened yet. Then life takes us somewhere. We fail, travel, lose things, become embarrassed, become braver, and return with a slightly different way of looking.

Suddenly, the familiar person seems different.

Perhaps they did not change at all.

The distance was inside us.

I do not think every film needs to teach a lesson, and I do not want to force *In July* into one. What it leaves me with is an image: a road, a person moving toward something he believes is important, another person moving beside him, countries passing, plans failing, coincidences accumulating, and the destination slowly approaching while the real answer has been traveling next to him the entire time.

There is something comforting in that image because we are all probably traveling toward some wrong destinations. We want people who may be wrong for us. We chase ideas that may disappoint us. We believe in signs that may mean nothing. We take roads we later question.

But being wrong about the destination does not make the journey useless.

Sometimes we have to travel a very long way to see what was close. Sometimes we need a foreign country to understand home. Sometimes we need the wrong love to understand love.

And sometimes the person we were searching for was already traveling beside us.
