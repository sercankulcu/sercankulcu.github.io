Some songs tolerate almost any kind of listening. They survive kitchens, traffic, conversation, cheap speakers and the distracted half-attention we now give to enormous amounts of music.

*Space-Dye Vest* has never been one of those songs for me.

Years ago I wrote that every food has a way it should be eaten and this song has a way it should be heard. I would probably use a different word now.

Ritual.

There is a point in the song where my hand moves toward the volume control. I know the transition is coming. I have heard it many times. None of this weakens the reflex. The sound approaches a certain place and the body remembers what to do.

A little louder.

The interesting moment occurs when the volume is already at maximum.

The hand still moves.

Then there is nowhere for the gesture to go, so the music goes somewhere else.

Into the stomach, perhaps.

This is why I distrust purely intellectual accounts of listening. We can discuss harmony, lyrics, production, dynamics and structure, and all of those things matter. But before analysis, the body has habits. A familiar line changes posture. A transition makes you lean closer. Sometimes you know what the next ten seconds will do to you and choose them anyway.

Music used to belong more strongly to places for me. A particular computer, a room, a pair of speakers, a drive at night. Before streaming turned listening into an almost frictionless continuum, choosing a song required a small act of intention. You opened a folder, found the file and played it.

That tiny effort created a boundary.

I remember listening to *Space-Dye Vest* late at night when everyone else in the building seemed asleep. The screen was the main light in the room. I had work to finish and was doing none of it. The song ended and I immediately played it again, which is a poor productivity technique but an excellent way to attach a piece of music to a period of life.

Songs become contaminated by us in this way.

A recording is fixed. The listener is not.

At twenty, a lyric seems to mean one thing. Ten years later the same line may become less profound, more embarrassing, or unexpectedly precise. We return to old music carrying new experiences, and the music appears to have changed because the person hearing it has.

There are songs I avoided for years because they remembered too accurately. There are others whose emotional force disappeared once the life around them changed. A few somehow survive every version of the listener.

*Space-Dye Vest* has survived partly through the ritual.

The hand still moves.

That reflex contains older rooms, older speakers, older versions of myself who believed certain feelings would last forever because they had not yet lasted long enough to change.

People often ask what a song means.

I increasingly prefer another question.

What happened to the song after it entered your life?

Did it become attached to a person? A winter? A city? Did you use it to intensify a mood you probably should have escaped? Did you always turn the volume up at the same place?

The recording is shared.

The listening is private.

And when the control is already at maximum, the only thing left to adjust is yourself.
