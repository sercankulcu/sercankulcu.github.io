There are sentences we regret saying and sentences we regret withholding. *Brief Encounter* belongs almost entirely to the second category.

When I first wrote about the film, I reduced my reaction to one question: What if I had said, *don't go, stay*? Years later I still think that is the right entrance into the story, although the sentence now seems less romantic and more complicated.

Laura and Alec meet without ceremony. There is no grand sign that an important story has begun. A railway station, a small accident, tea, another meeting. Their intimacy grows inside ordinary spaces, and perhaps that is why it feels dangerous. Extraordinary settings permit extraordinary behavior. A station café belongs to the rest of life.

Both already have lives. Their affection does not arrive in an empty room; it arrives among spouses, children, obligations, habits and people who would have to absorb the consequences. A cup of tea therefore becomes heavy with what it might lead to. A train matters because it leaves whether the conversation is finished or not.

I once had a goodbye like that, though not a romantic one. A friend was leaving the city for a job abroad. We met for coffee before his bus. I had several things I intended to say: that I envied his courage, that I was angry he was leaving, that I suspected we would not remain as close as we promised. Instead we discussed luggage, rent and whether the bus would stop for dinner. When the announcement came, he stood, we hugged, and I said, “Send a message when you arrive.”

For years I remembered the conversation we did not have more clearly than the one we did.

That is what the film understands. Silence is not emptiness. It contains possible sentences, and each possible sentence seems to lead toward another life.

We often romanticize the things we did not choose because reality never had the opportunity to damage them. The job we declined never became boring. The city we did not move to never became lonely. The person we did not stay with never developed ordinary habits that irritated us. Unlived lives remain polished by absence.

Laura and Alec's restraint is painful partly because it keeps possibility intact. If they acted without restraint, the story might become less beautiful and more ordinary: logistics, consequences, resentment, compromise. That does not mean they should remain silent. It means speech would create a different kind of truth.

The film's spaces have stayed with me too. Stations, cafés, corridors and platforms are designed for temporary occupation. Nobody belongs there for long. They are places between destinations, which makes them natural settings for a relationship that cannot comfortably become a destination itself.

Real goodbyes are rarely arranged as carefully as films arrange them. Someone interrupts. A telephone rings. A train arrives. Another person enters the room. We assume a better moment will come later and discover, sometimes years afterward, that the imperfect moment was the final one.

But I no longer think the lesson of *Brief Encounter* is simply that we should say “stay.”

Wanting someone to remain does not prove that remaining would be kind. Love can be real and still fail to make a choice right.

That is the film's maturity. Nobody has to be a villain for a situation to be impossible.

The train leaves anyway.
