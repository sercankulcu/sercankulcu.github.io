title: "Brief Encounter"
date: 2008-12-31

There are sentences we say.

And there are sentences we spend years thinking we should have said.

*Brief Encounter* is a film for the second kind.

When I first wrote about it, I described it very simply:

A film for people who have thought,

**"What if I had said: don't go, stay?"**

That was what I saw in the film.

Not simply a love story.

Not simply two people meeting at the wrong time.

A story about the terrible distance between what we feel and what we manage to say.

Laura and Alec meet almost accidentally.

There is nothing spectacular about the beginning.

No grand entrance.

No destiny announcing itself.

Just an ordinary encounter in an ordinary place.

Then another encounter.

Then another.

And slowly, something that should have remained unimportant becomes important.

That is how many important things probably begin.

Quietly.

The difficulty is that both of them already have lives.

Responsibilities.

Families.

Habits.

People waiting for them.

They cannot simply behave as if nothing existed before they met.

And that makes every small moment between them heavier.

A cup of tea is no longer just a cup of tea.

A train is no longer just transportation.

A few minutes together become something that must be counted.

Time becomes visible when you know it is running out.

That feeling stayed with me.

We usually do not appreciate ordinary time while we have unlimited amounts of it.

You can see someone tomorrow.

You can call next week.

You can explain yourself later.

There is always another opportunity.

Until suddenly there isn't.

Then ten minutes becomes precious.

One sentence becomes important.

One goodbye can stay in your mind for years.

The film understands this very well.

It is full of restraint.

People do not constantly announce what they feel.

They hesitate.

They change the subject.

They control themselves.

They say one thing while meaning another.

Today we might call that poor communication.

But sometimes communication is not difficult because we do not know the words.

Sometimes we know exactly what we want to say.

We are simply afraid of what will happen after we say it.

"Stay."

It is such a small word.

But saying it can change the responsibility for everything that follows.

If the other person stays, what happens next?

What must be abandoned?

Who gets hurt?

What does this new life look like tomorrow morning?

Perhaps that is why people sometimes choose silence.

Silence does not solve the problem.

It postpones responsibility.

And for a little while, postponement can feel like safety.

The problem is that silence also becomes a decision.

If you do not say "stay," the other person may leave.

If you do not say "I love you," they may never know.

If you do not say "I'm sorry," there may eventually be nobody left to forgive you.

We often imagine regret as the result of doing something wrong.

But some of the strongest regrets come from doing nothing.

Not asking.

Not going.

Not calling.

Not stopping somebody at the door.

Not saying the sentence that was already waiting inside us.

That is what *Brief Encounter* made me think about.

The title itself becomes more painful the longer I think about it.

A brief encounter.

Something short.

Temporary.

Almost insignificant when measured in time.

Yet length and importance are not the same thing.

Someone can stay in our life for years and leave very little behind.

Another person can appear for a short time and change the way we remember an entire period of our life.

Time measures duration.

It does not measure meaning.

There is also something beautiful about the fact that the film does not need enormous events.

Its emotional world is built from stations, trains, cafés, glances, conversations, interruptions and departures.

Very ordinary things.

Maybe that is why it works.

Most of our own important memories are also attached to ordinary places.

A bus stop.

A corridor.

A café.

A street corner.

A car.

A table where we once sat with somebody.

The place itself may be completely unremarkable to everyone else.

But once something important happens there, geography becomes memory.

A railway station can become a whole relationship.

And then there is the goodbye.

Goodbyes in films usually get the space they deserve.

Music rises.

People say everything.

There are final embraces.

Perfect sentences.

Real life is often much worse organized.

Somebody interrupts.

The phone rings.

The bus arrives.

Another person walks into the room.

You assume there will be another opportunity.

And suddenly that was the goodbye.

You only recognize it afterwards.

I think that is one of the saddest truths in the film.

We rarely know which moment is the final one while we are living it.

If we did, perhaps we would behave differently.

Perhaps we would look longer.

Listen more carefully.

Say one more thing.

Or perhaps we would simply say:

Don't go.

Stay.

But there is another side to this.

Wanting somebody to stay does not necessarily mean that they should.

Love does not automatically make every decision correct.

Sometimes leaving is painful precisely because staying would damage too many lives.

That is what prevents *Brief Encounter* from becoming a simple romantic fantasy.

There is no easy villain to defeat.

The obstacle is life itself.

Duty.

Timing.

Existing choices.

Other people's feelings.

The consequences of desire.

Sometimes two people can love each other and still have no good decision available.

That is a much harder story than one where love simply wins.

Maybe that was what I meant years ago when I wrote that the film was for people who had thought:

**"If only I had said, don't go, stay."**

Because the sentence contains two different regrets.

The first is obvious:

I should have asked you to stay.

But underneath it is another question:

If I had asked, would you really have stayed?

And underneath that:

If you had stayed, would we have been happier?

We can never know.

That is why unsaid sentences are so powerful.

Reality never gets the opportunity to prove them wrong.

They remain perfect possibilities.

The life we did not live can always look better than the one we did.

Maybe that is why some brief encounters remain with us for so long.

They never had enough time to become ordinary.

They never had enough time to disappoint us completely.

They remain unfinished.

And unfinished stories invite us to keep writing endings for them in our heads.

Years later, we can still imagine another version.

The train does not leave.

The door does not close.

Someone turns around.

Someone finally says the sentence.

Don't go.

Stay.

Perhaps the painful lesson of *Brief Encounter* is not that we should always stop people from leaving.

It is simpler than that.

When something matters, say it while there is still someone there to hear it.
