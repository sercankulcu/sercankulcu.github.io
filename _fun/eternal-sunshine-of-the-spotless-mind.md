I watched *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* after a relationship had ended, which was not a neutral condition in which to encounter a film about deleting someone from memory.

At that point I had already performed the ordinary, low-technology version of Lacuna's procedure. Photographs had been moved into a folder I would not open. Messages had been deleted. Certain songs were skipped. I took a different route through one part of the city because the usual street seemed to contain too much unnecessary information.

We cannot erase a person, so we erase the triggers.

The film's premise is therefore less fantastic than it sounds. The technology is imaginary; the desire is common. Pain produces an editorial instinct. We want to remove the paragraph that makes the rest of the page difficult to read.

What moved me was Joel's change of mind inside the procedure. Once the memories begin to disappear, even the painful ones acquire value. A bad evening, an argument, an awkward silence—things he had wanted gone become evidence that a life was shared.

Memory behaves unfairly after a relationship ends. On one day it presents the prosecution's case. Every irritation returns with perfect clarity. On another day it becomes sentimental and edits out everything except tenderness. We call both versions memory, though each is already a form of storytelling.

The film makes this visible by turning memory into rooms that collapse. Faces disappear. Locations lose their edges. A familiar place empties while Joel is still inside it. The effect is beautiful because forgetting is usually too slow for us to see.

I found the ordinary memories the hardest to watch. The dramatic moments are easy to explain to other people: how you met, why you fought, what finally ended the relationship. But those events are only the signposts. A relationship mostly exists between them, in remarks nobody writes down, meals, walks, stupid jokes, shared boredom, small annoyances and the way another person's presence becomes part of a room.

When those things disappear, the loss cannot be summarized.

Clementine and Joel are not ideal partners, and the film would be much weaker if they were. They irritate one another. They misunderstand motives. They expect the other person to repair things that existed before the relationship began. Their love does not purify them.

Near the end, the recordings remove any remaining fantasy. Each hears what the other once disliked. There is no innocence left to protect. They know that attraction does not guarantee compatibility and that the future may contain the same failures.

Still, there is the possibility of beginning.

That bothered me when I first watched it. I wanted the film to tell me whether beginning again was brave or foolish.

Years later, I think the refusal to answer is the point.

After my own relationship ended, there were months when I would gladly have erased parts of it. Later I discovered something more complicated: I did not want the relationship back, but I did want the memories to remain mine.

Missing a period of life is not the same as wanting to return to it.

Some memories are valuable precisely because they are finished.

Pain changes shape.

Erasure would remove the evidence that something once mattered.
