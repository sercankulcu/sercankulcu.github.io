I first watched *The Social Network* alone, after midnight, on a computer.

Before starting the film, I closed Facebook.

For the next two hours I watched a dramatized version of the story behind the website I had just closed.

That circularity amused me, but what surprised me was how little the film made me think about programming.

Code is present. There are servers, traffic, product decisions, rapid growth and the pleasure of building something users immediately want.

The deeper subject is ownership.

Who owns an idea?

Who owns the work?

Who owns the story of how success began?

Software projects often start in rooms where those questions feel premature. Someone has an idea. Someone else writes code. A friend introduces another person. Roles remain informal because the project is too small to justify paperwork.

Then the project succeeds.

Success makes memory valuable.

Suddenly everyone remembers the beginning differently.

One person remembers the idea. Another remembers doing the difficult implementation. Another remembers providing access or money. Each version may contain truth.

The larger the outcome becomes, the more expensive the origin story becomes.

I once worked on a much smaller project with three people. It never became a company, never made significant money and would not interest a film director. Even so, when we later described how the project began, each of us naturally placed ourselves closer to the center.

That was enough to make *The Social Network* uncomfortable.

Failure usually ends arguments about ownership.

Success manufactures them.

The film also changes the usual romance of the student project. Technical culture loves the story of the small thing built quickly that becomes enormous: a weekend prototype, a dorm-room project, an idea launched before anyone gives permission.

The code can scale.

Relationships may not.

Engineers are trained to anticipate load. What happens when traffic doubles? When one server becomes ten? When storage grows?

We are less systematic about another question.

What happens to the people involved when the project succeeds beyond the scale of the original friendship?

A favor becomes equity.

An informal promise meets a lawyer.

A shared experiment becomes property.

The irony is almost too perfect: a film about a technology for social connection is filled with people who communicate instantly with millions while failing to communicate with the person across a table.

When the film ended, I reopened Facebook.

The interface looked unchanged.

For a few minutes I had difficulty seeing it only as software.

Every large platform once fit into a room small enough for everyone involved to know one another personally.

Then the software grows.

The room becomes history.

And history becomes valuable enough to argue about.
