I first watched *The Social Network* alone, after midnight, on a computer.

That seems like the appropriate way to watch a film about Facebook.

At the time, Facebook itself still felt strangely close to its university origins. It was already enormous, but it had not yet become the background infrastructure of everyday digital life. People still talked about “being on Facebook” as though it were a place one deliberately entered.

I remember closing the browser before starting the film.

For the next two hours, I watched a fictionalized version of the story behind the site I had just closed.

What surprised me was how little the film made me think about programming.

There is code in it, certainly. There are servers, traffic, rapid growth, technical decisions, and the excitement of building something that people immediately want to use.

But the real subject seemed to be ownership.

Who owns an idea?

Who owns the work?

Who owns the success after a small project becomes something none of the original participants could have fully predicted?

Software projects often begin casually. Someone has an idea. Another person helps. A third person knows someone useful. Roles are fluid because the thing itself is still small.

Then the project succeeds.

Success has a strange ability to rewrite memory.

Suddenly everybody remembers the beginning differently.

One person remembers inventing the idea. Another remembers doing the difficult work. Another remembers providing the opportunity. Someone else remembers being present before anybody knew the project would matter.

The larger the outcome becomes, the more valuable the origin story becomes.

That is what I found interesting in *The Social Network*.

Failure usually ends arguments about ownership.

Success creates them.

The film also made ambition look less heroic than technical culture sometimes prefers.

Programmers enjoy stories about building things quickly. A weekend project becomes a company. A small script solves a problem. A student creates something in a dorm room and millions of people use it.

The narrative is attractive because it suggests a clean relationship between intelligence, effort, and reward.

But growth changes the meaning of a project.

At ten users, a technical decision is a technical decision.

At ten million users, the same decision may have social, financial, legal, and personal consequences.

The code scales.

Relationships often do not.

That was the part I kept thinking about after the film.

Software engineering teaches us to anticipate load. We ask what happens when traffic doubles, when storage grows, when one server is no longer enough.

We are much worse at asking what success will do to the people involved.

What happens when friendship becomes equity?

When a favor becomes a contribution?

When a shared experiment becomes a company?

When informal promises meet lawyers?

The film is full of people who can communicate instantly with thousands of users and still fail to communicate with the person sitting across the table.

That contradiction feels almost too perfect for a film about social media.

When it ended, I reopened Facebook.

The interface looked exactly the same as before.

But for a few minutes I found it difficult to see it as only a website. Behind every successful piece of software there is a period when it is still small enough to fit inside a room.

There are conversations.

Assumptions.

Friendships.

Handshakes.

People who believe they understand what they are building together.

Then, if the software succeeds, the room becomes history.

And history is where everyone starts arguing about who was standing where.
