Important People in Algorithms

62 profiles of the people behind the algorithms and ideas of the course's fourteen chapters, from Euclid's GCD algorithm and the Turing machine to Dijkstra and Kruskal, KMP and Huffman coding, Cook's NP-completeness theorem, competitive analysis, Karatsuba multiplication and branch and bound.

EU

Euclid

c. 300 BC · Ancient Greece · Alexandria

GCD algorithm · Elements

In the Elements he described how to find the greatest common divisor of two numbers by repeated subtraction; today we write the same method with division and remainder. Based on the identity gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b), it is one of the oldest algorithms still in use. Because the larger number at least halves every two steps, the number of steps is bounded by O(log min(a, b)).

AK

Al-Khwarizmi

c. 780 – c. 850 · Baghdad · House of Wisdom

Hindu–Arabic arithmetic · algebra

A mathematician at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad; his book on calculating with Hindu–Arabic numerals spread through Europe in Latin translation (Algoritmi de numero Indorum). The word “algorithm” comes from the Latinized form of his name, and “algebra” from the title of his work al-Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wa-l-muqābala. He stands for the idea of solving problems with explicit, repeatable steps.

LF

Leonardo Fibonacci

c. 1170 – c. 1250 · Italian · Pisa

Liber Abaci · 1202

With Liber Abaci (1202) he introduced Hindu–Arabic numerals to Europe; the book's rabbit problem produces the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, … Solving F(n) = F(n − 1) + F(n − 2) by plain recursion takes exponential time because the same subproblems are computed again and again. This is the best-known starting example for dynamic programming and memoization.

LE

Leonhard Euler

1707–1783 · Swiss · St. Petersburg, Berlin

Bridges of Königsberg · 1736

In 1736 he proved that there is no walk crossing each of the seven bridges of Königsberg exactly once; treating land masses as vertices and bridges as edges is regarded as the beginning of graph theory. He showed that such a walk requires the number of odd-degree vertices to be 0 or 2. An Euler tour, which uses every edge once, is also a step of Christofides' algorithm.

CG

Carl Friedrich Gauss

1777–1855 · German · Göttingen

Arithmetic series · 1 + 2 + … + n

According to a well-known story from his school days, he summed the numbers 1 to 100 at once by pairing terms from both ends, giving n(n + 1)/2. This sum appears every time nested loops are counted as O(n²). The trick of multiplying two complex numbers with three real multiplications instead of four is also attributed to him; Karatsuba's method applies a similar idea to large numbers.

OB

Otakar Borůvka

1899–1995 · Czech · Brno

First MST algorithm · 1926

In 1926 he published the first minimum spanning tree algorithm, for building the electricity network of Moravia at minimum cost. In each round every component picks its lightest outgoing edge; since the number of components at least halves each round, O(log V) rounds suffice. Because the choices within a round are independent, the method also suits parallel and distributed settings.

AT

Alan Turing

1912–1954 · British · Cambridge, Manchester

Turing machine · 1936

In his 1936 paper “On Computable Numbers” he formalized the notion of an algorithm with the Turing machine and showed that no algorithm can solve the halting problem. Together with Alonzo Church's result in lambda calculus from the same year, this definition is accepted as the meaning of “effectively computable” (the Church–Turing thesis). The classes P and NP are defined with a time-bounded version of the same model.

Ronald A. Fisher & Frank Yates

1890–1962 · 1902–1994 · British · Rothamsted

Fisher–Yates shuffle · 1938

In 1938, in Statistical Tables for Biological, Agricultural and Medical Research, they described a pencil-and-paper method for producing a random permutation of a list with a table of random numbers. Richard Durstenfeld adapted it to computers in 1964 as the O(n) swapping version, which Knuth then popularized. Fisher is one of the founders of experimental design and modern statistics.

JN

John von Neumann

1903–1957 · Hungarian-American · Princeton IAS

Merge sort · 1945

In 1945 he described merge sort in programs written for the EDVAC; Knuth counts it among the first sorting programs written for a computer. A classic example of divide and conquer, the method runs in O(n log n) even in the worst case and is stable. He also helped develop the Monte Carlo method with Stanisław Ulam, and today's computer architecture bears his name.

Stanisław Ulam

1909–1984 · Polish-born · USA, Los Alamos

Monte Carlo method · 1946–1949

At Los Alamos in 1946, starting from the idea of estimating the chance of winning a game of solitaire by random trials, he proposed the Monte Carlo method; with von Neumann he applied it to neutron-diffusion calculations on the ENIAC. The first paper bearing the method's name appeared in 1949 with Nicholas Metropolis. Estimating π with random points is the simplest example of the idea used in the course.

George B. Dantzig

1914–2005 · American · RAND, Stanford

Simplex method · 1947

In 1947 he developed the simplex method for solving linear programs; in branch and bound the bound at each node is usually computed with such an LP relaxation. With Fulkerson and Johnson he solved a 49-city TSP instance to optimality with cutting planes in 1954. The story that as a student he solved two open statistics problems, mistaking them for homework, is also famous.

Claude Shannon

1916–2001 · American · Bell Labs, MIT

Information theory · 1948

His 1948 paper “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” founded information theory and defined entropy as the lower bound on the average number of bits per symbol in lossless compression. The average length of a Huffman code exceeds the entropy by at most 1 bit. His 1937 master's thesis, which applied Boolean algebra to switching circuits, is also a foundation of digital circuit design.

Derrick H. Lehmer

1905–1991 · American · UC Berkeley

Lehmer generator (1949) · the term “backtrack”

In 1949 he presented the multiplicative linear congruential generator x(k+1) = a · x(k) mod m, the pseudo-random number method now called the Lehmer generator. In the 1950s he used the term “backtrack” for search that extends a partial solution and steps back at a dead end. He also did pioneering work on primality testing and calculating machines in number theory.

Stephen C. Kleene

1909–1994 · American · University of Wisconsin

Regular expressions · 1951

In a 1951 RAND report (published 1956) he defined regular expressions and showed that they describe exactly the languages recognized by finite automata (Kleene's theorem). The * operation, meaning “zero or more repetitions”, is called the Kleene star after him. With the theory of recursive functions he is one of the founders of computability theory.

DH

David A. Huffman

1925–1999 · American · MIT, UC Santa Cruz

Huffman coding · 1952

As a graduate student at MIT he solved the problem of finding the shortest prefix code as a term paper that could replace the final exam in Robert Fano's course, and published it in 1952. The greedy method that repeatedly merges the two least frequent nodes minimizes the expected number of bits per character and beats his teacher's Shannon–Fano method. Huffman coding is a stage of formats such as DEFLATE and JPEG today.

Hans Peter Luhn

1896–1964 · German-American · IBM

Hashing into buckets · 1953

At IBM in 1953 he proposed speeding up search by distributing keys into “buckets” with a function; this is one of the earliest known examples of hash tables and chaining. The Luhn check-digit algorithm, still used to validate credit-card numbers, and key-word-in-context (KWIC) indexing are also his.

Richard Bellman

1920–1984 · American · RAND Corporation

Dynamic programming · 1950s

At the RAND Corporation in the 1950s he introduced the name “dynamic programming” and the principle of optimality for multi-stage decision problems: every part of an optimal solution is itself optimal for its subproblem. In 1958 he published a single-source shortest-path method that allows negative edge weights (Bellman-Ford). The phrase “curse of dimensionality” is also his.

GA

Gene Amdahl

1922–2015 · American · IBM

Linear probing (1954) · Amdahl's law (1967)

In 1954, with Elaine McGraw and Arthur Samuel, he used linear probing, which moves to the next free cell on a collision, in an assembler for the IBM 701. He was one of the chief architects of the IBM System/360. In a 1967 conference talk he argued that the sequential part of a program limits parallel speedup; this observation is known as Amdahl's law.

FF

L. R. Ford Jr. & D. R. Fulkerson

1927–2017 · 1924–1976 · American · RAND Corporation

Maximum flow · 1956

At the RAND Corporation in 1956 they published the Ford-Fulkerson method, which augments flow until no augmenting path remains in the residual network, and the max-flow min-cut theorem. In the same years Ford also gave the shortest-path method that relaxes edges repeatedly, which is why the algorithm is called Bellman-Ford. Their 1962 book Flows in Networks established network flow as a field of its own.

Joseph Kruskal

1928–2010 · American · Princeton, Bell Labs

Kruskal's algorithm · 1956

In a short 1956 paper he gave the MST algorithm that examines edges in order of weight and adds those that do not form a cycle; the same paper also described the reverse-delete method. Today the cycle check is done with union-find, and the algorithm runs in O(E log E). He also made important contributions to statistics, such as multidimensional scaling.

EM

Edward F. Moore

1925–2003 · American · Bell Labs

Shortest path in a maze (BFS) · 1957

At Bell Labs he described breadth-first search for finding the shortest path through a maze (“The Shortest Path Through a Maze”, presented 1957, published 1959). The work shows that shortest paths in unweighted graphs can be found layer by layer; because of the method he gave for the weighted case, Bellman-Ford is sometimes called “Bellman–Ford–Moore”. The Moore machine type of finite state machine is also named after him.

RP

Robert C. Prim

1921–2021 · American · Bell Labs

Prim's algorithm · 1957

At Bell Labs in 1957 he published the minimum spanning tree algorithm that starts from one vertex and grows the tree by the lightest outgoing edge at each step. Vojtěch Jarník had found the same method in 1930, and Dijkstra rediscovered it in 1959, so it is also called the Jarník–Prim algorithm. With a priority queue it runs in O(E log V), and its structure closely resembles Dijkstra's algorithm.

Donald L. Shell

1924–2015 · American · General Electric

Shell sort · 1959

In the method he published in 1959, elements are first insertion-sorted at large gaps and then at gradually smaller gaps. Distant inversions are thus fixed early, and the O(n²) behaviour of insertion sort improves greatly in practice. The complexity depends on the gap sequence and is still an open question for some sequences.

ED

Edsger W. Dijkstra

1930–2002 · Dutch · Amsterdam, Eindhoven; Austin, USA

Shortest paths · 1959

By his own account he designed his shortest-path algorithm in about twenty minutes in Amsterdam in 1956, and published it in 1959; the same paper also independently gave Prim's method for minimum spanning trees. Settling the vertex with the smallest tentative distance and relaxing its edges works for non-negative weights and takes O((V + E) log V) with a priority queue. He is also known for structured programming and semaphores, and received the Turing Award in 1972.

MR

Michael O. Rabin

b. 1931 · Israeli · Hebrew University of Jerusalem; Harvard

Miller–Rabin (1980) · Rabin-Karp (1987)

With Dana Scott he defined nondeterministic finite automata in 1959, work that earned the 1976 Turing Award. In 1980 he turned Gary Miller's primality test (1976) into an unconditional probabilistic test: in Miller–Rabin a composite number passes a round with probability at most 1/4. With Karp he published Rabin-Karp string matching with a rolling hash in 1987; he is a pioneer of using randomness deliberately in algorithm design.

TH

C. A. R. (Tony) Hoare

1934–2026 · British · Oxford

Quicksort · 1959–1961

He designed quicksort in 1959–1960 while a visiting student at Moscow State University; the algorithm was published in 1961. Partitioning around a pivot and sorting both sides recursively takes O(n log n) on average and O(n²) with bad pivots. The same year he published Find (today's QuickSelect) for the k-th smallest element; he founded Hoare logic for program correctness and received the Turing Award in 1980.

Edward Fredkin

1934–2023 · American · MIT, Carnegie Mellon

Trie · 1960

In 1960 he published the idea of storing strings in a tree that shares common prefixes and named it “trie” from the middle of the word “retrieval”; René de la Briandais had described the same structure a year earlier (1959). Fredkin is also known for reversible computing and the Fredkin gate, and he directed Project MAC at MIT.

KA

Anatoly Karatsuba

1937–2008 · Soviet · Moscow

Karatsuba multiplication · 1960/1962

In 1960, while Kolmogorov's seminar was discussing the conjecture that multiplying two n-digit numbers needs Ω(n²) operations, he found within a week the method with three sub-multiplications that runs in O(n^1.585); he was 23 at the time. Kolmogorov had the result published in 1962 under the names of Karatsuba and Ofman. It is one of the first examples showing that divide and conquer can beat a presumed lower bound.

LD

Ailsa Land & Alison Doig

1927–2021 · b. 1929 · British · London School of Economics

Branch and bound · 1960

In a 1960 Econometrica paper written at the London School of Economics they proposed branch and bound for solving integer linear programs: split the solution space into branches and compute a bound for each branch with an LP relaxation. Branches whose bound is worse than the best known solution are pruned. This idea is still at the core of today's integer programming solvers; Doig later worked at the University of Melbourne as Alison Harcourt.

Robert W. Floyd

1936–2001 · American · Carnegie Mellon, Stanford

Floyd-Warshall · 1962

In 1962 he published the algorithm that finds all-pairs shortest paths in O(V³) time; this dynamic program, which adds intermediate vertices one at a time, is now known as Floyd-Warshall. In 1964 he gave the method that builds a heap in O(n) time, and the “tortoise and hare” cycle-detection method for lists is also attributed to him. He received the Turing Award in 1978 for his pioneering work on program verification.

SW

Stephen Warshall

1935–2006 · American

Transitive closure · 1962

In his 1962 paper “A Theorem on Boolean Matrices” he published the method that computes the transitive closure of a relation on a Boolean matrix in O(V³) time: if there is a path from i to k and from k to j, there is a path from i to j. Because Floyd's shortest-path algorithm of the same year uses the same triple loop, the two are named together. Bernard Roy had published a similar result in 1959.

J. W. J. Williams

1930–2012 · British-Canadian

Heapsort · 1964

In 1964 he published heapsort, and the binary heap behind it, as “Algorithm 232: Heapsort” in Communications of the ACM. Turning the array into a max-heap and repeatedly moving the root to the end takes O(n log n) time in the worst case and O(1) extra memory. In the same year Robert Floyd gave the improvement that builds the heap in O(n) time.

Jack Edmonds

b. 1934 · American-born · Canada, Waterloo

Blossom algorithm (1965) · Edmonds-Karp (1972)

In his 1965 paper “Paths, Trees, and Flowers” he gave the blossom algorithm for maximum matching in general graphs in polynomial time and argued that “efficient algorithm” should mean polynomial-time algorithm. With Karp he showed in 1972 that Ford-Fulkerson with BFS-chosen augmenting paths runs in O(V·E²). In 1971 he showed that the greedy algorithm is optimal exactly on matroids.

Vladimir Levenshtein

1935–2017 · Soviet-Russian · Keldysh Institute of Applied Mathematics

Levenshtein distance · 1965

In 1965 he defined the minimum number of insertions, deletions and substitutions needed to turn one string into another; the measure bears his name. His goal was error-correcting codes; today it is used in spell checking, DNA sequence comparison and approximate search. Computing the distance by dynamic programming in O(m·n) time (Wagner–Fischer, 1974) is the table method used in the course.

Solomon W. Golomb

1932–2016 · American · University of Southern California

Backtrack Programming · 1965

Their 1965 Journal of the ACM paper “Backtrack Programming”, written with Leonard Baumert, is one of the first systematic accounts of backtracking as a general search method; they showed with examples how pruning shrinks the search tree. His description of polyominoes in 1953 inspired computer search for puzzle and tiling problems. Golomb coding is also his contribution.

RG

Ronald Graham

1935–2020 · American · Bell Labs, UC San Diego

List scheduling (1966) · Graham scan (1972)

In 1966 he proved that list scheduling, which gives each job to the first free machine, has a makespan at most (2 − 1/m) times the optimum; this is one of the first examples of an approximation ratio. In 1969 he showed that the LPT rule, which sorts jobs from longest to shortest, has ratio 4/3 − 1/(3m). In 1972 he published the Graham scan, which finds the convex hull of points in the plane in O(n log n) time.

László Bélády

1928–2021 · Hungarian-born · USA, IBM

OPT (MIN) · Bélády's anomaly

At IBM in 1966 he defined the offline algorithm (MIN, today OPT) that evicts the page used furthest in the future and gives the fewest page faults; online policies are compared against this ideal. Andrew Viterbi

Viterbi algorithm · 1967

In 1967 he proposed a dynamic programming algorithm for decoding convolutional codes that keeps, at each step, the best path reaching each state. Donald E. Knuth

b. 1938 · American · Stanford

The Art of Computer Programming · 1968–

With the multi-volume The Art of Computer Programming he turned the analysis of algorithms into a discipline, popularizing exact operation counts and average-case analysis. Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson & Bertram Raphael

Nilsson: 1933–2019 · American · Stanford Research Institute (SRI)

A* search · 1968

At SRI they published the A* algorithm in 1968 for the path planning of the Shakey robot. Raphael suggested adding the cost so far to Nilsson's heuristic search, and Hart introduced the notions of admissible and consistent heuristics. Proceeding by f = g + h, A* finds a shortest path with an admissible heuristic and often expands far fewer vertices than Dijkstra's algorithm.

Volker Strassen

b. 1936 · German · Zurich, Konstanz

Strassen's algorithm · 1969

In his 1969 paper “Gaussian Elimination is not Optimal” he split matrices into four blocks and multiplied them with seven block products instead of eight, giving O(n^2.81) matrix multiplication; it was the first result showing that matrices can be multiplied faster than n³. With Schönhage he developed fast multiplication of very large integers in 1971, and with Solovay a probabilistic primality test in 1977.

Dinic's algorithm · 1970

In Moscow in 1969 he designed the maximum-flow algorithm that proceeds by finding blocking flows in a level graph, and published it in 1970. Knuth-Morris-Pratt · 1970/1977

In 1970 Morris found a matching method that never moves backwards in the text by exploiting the pattern's own prefixes, and published it with Pratt in a technical report; a few weeks later Knuth reached the same algorithm independently from automata theory. Stephen Cook

b. 1939 · American-Canadian · University of Toronto

Cook's theorem · 1971

In his 1971 paper “The Complexity of Theorem-Proving Procedures” he showed that every problem in NP reduces to SAT in polynomial time, i.e. SAT is NP-complete. The result posed the P = NP question formally and started the theory of NP-completeness. He received the Turing Award in 1982.

Robert E. Tarjan

b. 1948 · American · Princeton

Strongly connected components · 1972

In 1972 he published the algorithm that finds the strongly connected components of a directed graph in linear time with a single DFS. In 1975 he proved that union-find with path compression and union by rank takes nearly constant amortized time, and he systematized amortized analysis. With Sleator he developed splay trees and the analysis of online list update (1985), and with Goldberg the push–relabel method (1988); he shared the 1986 Turing Award with Hopcroft.

Richard M. Karp

b. 1935 · American · Berkeley

21 NP-complete problems · 1972

In 1972, starting from SAT, he showed by polynomial reductions that 21 problems such as clique, vertex cover, Hamiltonian cycle and knapsack are NP-complete, revealing how widespread NP-completeness is. Hopcroft–Karp matching · 1973

Nicos Christofides

1942–2019 · Cypriot-British · Imperial College London

Christofides' algorithm · 1976

In 1976 he gave an algorithm for TSP with the triangle inequality (metric TSP) that finds a tour at most 3/2 times the optimum: build an MST, add a minimum perfect matching on the odd-degree vertices, and walk the Euler tour with shortcuts. The ratio remained the best known guarantee for metric TSP until 2020. Anatoliy Serdyukov reached the same result in the USSR at about the same time.

Robert S. Boyer & J Strother Moore

Boyer: b. 1946 · American · University of Texas at Austin

Boyer-Moore · 1977

In 1977 they published the algorithm that compares the pattern from right to left and makes large jumps in the text with the bad-character and good-suffix rules; with long patterns most text characters are never examined. Horspool gave a simpler version in 1980 that uses only the bad-character rule. The pair also built the Boyer–Moore theorem prover (the ancestor of ACL2) and the majority vote algorithm.

LZ

Abraham Lempel & Jacob Ziv

1936–2023 · 1931–2023 · Israeli · Technion

LZ77 · LZ78

At the Technion they published LZ77 in 1977, which refers back to earlier repeats inside a sliding window, and LZ78 in 1978, which collects seen phrases in a dictionary. Terry Welch simplified LZ78 into LZW in 1984; the GIF format uses LZW, while DEFLATE, used by ZIP and PNG, combines LZ77 with Huffman coding. These methods are the basis of “universal” compression that needs no probability table in advance.
