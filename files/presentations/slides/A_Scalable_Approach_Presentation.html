1 · Introduction

What Does Twitter Think about the News?

  • Decisions depend on "what other people think"; sentiment analysis finds out whether a text is positive or negative.
  • Two families: supervised machine learning (train on labelled data) and semantic orientation (unsupervised, lexicon-like).
  • Tweets arrive continuously, so the classifier must scale: the work is distributed with Hadoop (HDFS + MapReduce) and Mahout.
  • Most earlier work is on English; this study builds a Turkish dataset and applies the same methods to Turkish.
Why Turkish is hard

Agglutinative: one root takes many suffixes, e.g. gel-mi-yor-um "I am not coming". Words with the same root can mean different things.

Why tweets are hard

Short texts, misspellings, missing Turkish characters, URLs, user names and "RT" without sentiment.

3.4 · Figs. 2–3

Two MapReduce Jobs

Job 1: analyse a batch

Input: (news item, tweet) pairs from the last ten minutes.

Map: classify each tweet with the model trained in Mahout beforehand.

Reduce: per news item, count positive, neutral and negative tweets (popularity, supporters, opposers) and write the result to HDFS.

Job 2: merge with history

Input: the new batch result and all earlier results since system start.

Map: key each record by a hash id of its news item.

Reduce: add up the counts per key into one consolidated result.

Splitting the work this way keeps each ten-minute run small: old tweets are never re-classified, only their counts are merged.

4.2 · Classifiers · Interactive

Naive Bayes vs. Complement Naive Bayes

Try:

  • Naive Bayes: sum the evidence of each word, plus the class prior, which favours the larger class.
  • Complement NB (Rennie et al.) learns from the complement classes and drops the prior; with two classes that missing prior is the visible difference. On maç and yeni karar the two disagree.

5 · Conclusion and Future Work

Conclusion

  • A scalable framework links Turkish tweets to news items and rates each item by its positive, neutral and negative tweets.
  • On 6,000 manually labelled Turkish tweets, the Bayes classifiers (Complement NB 79.38%, NB 78.61%) clearly beat logistic regression (63.12%).
  • Unigrams are the better feature model, and two-class problems are easier than three-class ones.
  • Mapping by bag of words reaches 40.3%; better mapping, e.g. lexicon-based, is future work.

5 · Conclusion

Check Questions

  1. Why does Turkish need stemming before tweets and news can be compared word by word?
  2. What problem of Naive Bayes does Complement Naive Bayes address, and why does it matter for this dataset?
  3. Why can bigrams and trigrams do worse than unigrams on 6,000 tweets?
  4. Why is the second MapReduce job needed?
  5. Six nodes give a 3.4× speed-up, not 6×. Why?
Show answers
  1. Turkish is agglutinative: the same root appears with many suffixes, so surface forms rarely match exactly.
  2. Bias towards the class with more training examples; half of the tweets are negative.
  3. More, sparser features on a small dataset make overfitting easier.
  4. To merge each ten-minute batch result with all earlier results into one consolidated count per news item.
  5. Fixed overheads such as job start-up, data distribution and the shuffle between map and reduce do not shrink with more nodes.
