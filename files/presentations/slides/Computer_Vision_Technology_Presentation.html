1 · Introduction

Why Computer Vision in Food?

  • Producers must meet strict safety rules and customer demands; quality and safety control is critical.
  • Many quality factors are visible: size, colour, shape and texture change as food ages or spoils, and they can be measured without destroying the product.
  • The result can set the product's price or its best-before date.
  • Human inspection is repetitive, tiring, costly and subjective; machines can also see beyond the visible spectrum.

Computer vision

  • fast, objective, consistent
  • non-destructive, low running cost
  • works on the production line

Human inspector

  • slow and tiring
  • subjective, varies by person
  • limited to visible light

An image is a matrix

Each element is a pixel with a position and an intensity; analysis turns these numbers into measurements.

Classification imitates a judge

The object is assigned to one of a finite set of classes, automating human visual inspection and decision-making.

2.1 · Challenges

Three Practical Challenges

Lighting

One of the factors that most seriously affects the result. Poor or uneven lighting causes unexpected and wrong decisions.

Resolution

Accuracy needs enough pixels on the details that matter; high resolution costs storage and processing time.

Round objects

A camera sees only a projection of one side. Fruits must be turned or viewed by several cameras to inspect the whole surface.

Different products respond differently at different wavelengths, so multispectral, hyperspectral or ultrasound acquisition may be needed instead of an ordinary colour camera.

3 · Applications

What Each Food Group Is Judged On

Meat

Chemical, microbial and sensory quality; above all flavour, juiciness and tenderness.

Poultry

Appearance and texture: skin colour, visible defects, pinkness of cooked meat.

Seafood

The most perishable group: physical properties, microbial contamination, appearance.

Fruit

Physical and chemical attributes; shape and texture reveal quality.

Vegetables

Appearance first: colour, size, shape and gloss.

Grain

Bulk density, moisture, shape and diameter drive classification.

Bakery

Shape and size: variation in thickness, diameter and weight.

Common thread

Visible, measurable attributes that a camera can quantify without touching the food.

4 · Future Research Directions

Where the Field Is Heading

Better algorithms

Effective segmentation, robustness, real-time capability and standardization: good data alone is not enough.

Hyperspectral imaging

Extra information per pixel; needs cost-effective, efficient processing to meet industry demands.

Inside the food

Fast, low-cost thermal, X-ray and MR imaging for internal defects, especially in fruit.

From 2D to 3D

Inspection increasingly needs height and depth, not only a flat projection.

Robots

Reprogrammable automation for evaluation, handling and processing, cheaper and more flexible over time.

Cloud robotics

Large datasets and cloud computing to improve learning for quality evaluation.

5 · Conclusion

Conclusion

  • With good image processing, computer vision is a proven technology for food quality and safety information.
  • Colour, multispectral, hyperspectral, thermal, X-ray and MR imaging have been developed for fast, objective, consistent, non-destructive evaluation.
  • Most systems use reflectance to judge surface attributes: shape, size, colour and texture.
  • Computer vision helps machines locate and characterize raw materials before handling, and will remain a key research area for the food industry.

Check Questions

  1. Name the five steps of a computer vision system and what each produces.
  2. Why can poor lighting create false defects?
  3. Why might a single camera miss a defect on an apple?
  4. When is hyperspectral imaging preferred over an ordinary colour camera?
  5. Which technique in the tables can see inside the product, and for what?
Show answers
  1. Acquisition (image), preprocessing (cleaner image), segmentation (object mask), feature extraction (size, colour, shape, texture values), classification (class or grade).
  2. Shadows and uneven brightness change the measured colour, so healthy dark areas look like bruises.
  3. It sees only one projected side of a round object; the defect may be on the hidden side.
  4. When the property is chemical or hidden (tenderness, contamination, adulteration, early bruises) and shows up only at particular wavelengths.
  5. Computed tomography (carrots) and X-ray imaging (onions): internal tissue and structure.
