Summary

At a Glance

6TiSCH schedules radio resources in time and frequency. When the 2.4 GHz band is crowded and networks are dense, both run short. Smart and directional antennas add a third dimension: space.

1.2–1.3 · Problem and Motivation

Constrained Nodes, Shared Cells, Wasted Energy

  • Sensor nodes are tiny: e.g. TelosB has a 250 kbps 802.15.4 radio, an 8 MHz MSP430 and 10 kB RAM. They decide with local knowledge only.
  • Nodes in hard-to-reach places live as long as their battery: energy decides network lifetime.
  • New nodes join through shared cells: every node listens on the same slot and channel, so collisions and interference are frequent.
  • Omnidirectional antennas radiate everywhere; current radio duty-cycling ignores antenna direction, and standard MAC protocols do not fit directional antennas.
Three ways to save energy

(1) smart antennas reach further with fewer hops, (2) scheduling wakes radios only when needed, (3) smarter schedules cut delay and keep control traffic healthy.

Research gap

Using smart antennas to improve 6TiSCH had not been studied: resources stayed two-dimensional.

1.4 · Aim and Contributions

Contributions

Antenna models in Cooja and Contiki

Switched-beam and steerable antenna models, configurable from the scenario file (number of antennas, beamwidth).

Distributed scheduling with switched beams (SF-DA)

Direction-aware resource planning, a neighbour table that keeps the best antenna, tests with Minimal Configuration and Orchestra.

Faster discovery for steerable antennas

False Beacon packet, neighbour-aware beacon direction and an autonomous schedule for control packets.

RSSI-based best direction

The direction with the highest received power is stored per neighbour and refined over time.

Direction-aware TASA

The known centralized TASA algorithm adapted to antenna directions, then optimized for end-to-end delay.

Bidirectional centralized scheduler

Cells follow the packet's path to the sink and the reply's path back, minimizing round-trip delay.

2.1 · 6TiSCH Protocol Stack

6TiSCH in Brief

6top (6P) + Scheduling Functionwho gets which cell
IEEE 802.15.4 TSCHtime slots + channel hopping
Cell = (slot, channel offset)

A slotframe repeats; each cell is TX, RX or shared. Channel hopping changes the physical channel every slotframe.

Three ways to fill the schedule

Autonomous: cells from a hash of node IDs (Orchestra). Distributed: neighbours negotiate with 6P (SF0). Centralized: a scheduler with a global view (TASA, AMUS).

This thesis

A cell becomes (slot, channel, antenna direction).

4.1 · Switched Beam with Distributed Scheduling

SF-DA: Direction-Aware Distributed Scheduling

for each slot in slotframe:
  if SHARED slot:
    if buffer empty: listen on all antennas
       on reception: update neighbour(dst, antenna)
    else: transmit on all antennas
  if TX slot and buffer not empty:
    if broadcast: transmit on all antennas
    else: transmit on antenna[neighbour(dst)]
  if RX slot: listen on the antenna
              facing the scheduled neighbour
  • Cells are triples: (slot, channel, antenna offset).
  • Statistics are kept per antenna, not per cell: antennas facing different directions see different channel conditions.
  • Parallel transmissions on the same channel become possible when they point away from each other.
  • Shared cells still use all antennas, so joining and control traffic keep working.

5 · Steerable Antenna with Distributed Scheduling

Why a Narrow Beam Slows Down Joining

Collisions

Enhanced Beacons are lost to collisions and interference in the shared cell.

Channel hopping

A joining node listens on another channel than the one the beacon uses.

Narrow coverage

A beam pointing the wrong way cannot hear the beacon at all.

False Beacon (5.2). Unsynchronized nodes broadcast a False Beacon instead of an EB. Other unsynchronized nodes keep their radio on, so they hear it easily and store the sender, direction and RSSI in a virtual neighbour list, before joining the network.

Neighbour-aware direction (Alg. 4). In a shared cell a node points to a random neighbour from the list, or with probability 1/(neighbours + 1) to a random direction, to still find newcomers.

Energy trade-off

Duplicated broadcasts cost energy when links are perfect; with worse links or heavier traffic the steerable antenna wins.

Fewer hops

Up to 80 m reach means fewer relays, so poor links hurt less than with 50 m omni links.

6.1 · Steerable Antenna with Centralized Scheduling · Table 5 · Interactive

Direction-Aware TASA (DATASA)

Nodes report parent, neighbours and measured directions to a scheduler behind the border router. The scheduler builds the connectivity, interference (2-hop), conflict, direction and traffic matrices, adds autonomous cells, then repeats per slot:

  1. Matching: conflict-free links, busiest nodes first.
  2. Colouring: assign channels; links that point away from each other may share one.

Example with 20 nodes and one channel: 32 slots with omni antennas, 25 with steerable.

4–6 · Synthesis

Which Approach, When?

Switched beam

  • simple RF switch, no direction search
  • lowest energy (narrower beam, less power)
  • fits distributed 6TiSCH directly

Steerable

  • longer reach, fewer hops
  • best delivery in large networks (82.6% at 81 nodes)
  • needs discovery help: False Beacon, autonomous cells

Distributed

  • scales, adapts locally
  • random cells: higher and more variable delay

Centralized

  • shortest schedules, lowest delay and energy
  • does not scale: tested up to 49 nodes; static, small networks

7 · Conclusion

Conclusion

  • Switched beams in 6TiSCH cut collisions in shared cells and improve delivery, energy and delay when channels are scarce.
  • Steerable antennas need help to join; a hybrid of distributed and autonomous scheduling and the False Beacon solve synchronization, broadcast and discovery problems.
  • A bidirectional, path-based centralized scheduler gives lower delay and energy than distributed scheduling in small, static networks.
  • Scheduling in time, frequency and space outperforms time and frequency alone in delivery, energy and delay.

Review

Check Questions

  1. Why does a crowded 2.4 GHz band limit 6TiSCH, and what does a directional antenna add?
  2. Why do broadcasts in shared cells use all antennas, but unicasts only one?
  3. Why does a steerable antenna make joining the network slower, and how does the False Beacon help?
  4. How can two neighbours find a common autonomous cell without exchanging messages?
  5. When is centralized scheduling the better choice, and when is it not?
Show answers
  1. Only a few channels stay free of Wi-Fi, so frequency diversity is lost; directional antennas let links that point away from each other share a slot and channel (spatial reuse).
  2. In a shared cell any neighbour may be listening, so all directions must be covered; a unicast's destination is known from the neighbour table, and one antenna causes less interference.
  3. The beam must point at the right neighbour at the right time on the right channel; the False Beacon lets unsynchronized nodes learn neighbours and directions in advance, so beacons go in useful directions.
  4. Both hash the pair of IDs (Eq. 5–7): one node's TX slot equals the other's RX slot, on the same channel offset.
  5. In small, static networks with scarce channels and strict delay needs; not in large or changing networks, where collecting topology and distributing schedules costs too much.
