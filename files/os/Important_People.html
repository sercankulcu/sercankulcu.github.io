Operating Systems · A Historical View

Pioneers of Operating Systems

Operating systems are the product of decades of accumulated work. Research on time sharing, concurrency, memory management, interactive use, portability, networking, virtualization, security and open systems shaped the structure we have today.

From the first computers to modern operating systems
The people, systems and ideas that influenced the development of operating systems and of modern computer science.

Auguste Kerckhoffs

Kerckhoffs's principle · 1883

In his 1883 paper La cryptographie militaire he listed six rules for military ciphers. The best known says that a system must remain secure even if it falls into the enemy's hands: security should depend only on the secrecy of the key, not on the secrecy of the algorithm.

Why it matters for engineering

The principle explains why open design and a large key space matter; the encryption and authentication mechanisms that operating systems use are designed under this assumption.

FB

Fred Brooks

OS/360 and The Mythical Man-Month · 1960s–1970s

At IBM, after the System/360 hardware, he took over management of the delayed OS/360 project in 1964. He described his experience in the book The Mythical Man-Month, published in 1975; its most famous observation is that adding people to a late software project makes it later (Brooks's law). He received the Turing Award in 1999.

Why it matters for engineering

OS/360 was one of the first large-scale attempts to manage the complexity of a big operating system; Brooks's lessons are still the starting point of discussions of teams, interfaces and conceptual integrity in operating system design.

LB

László Bélády

The OPT algorithm and Bélády's anomaly · 1960s

Worked at the IBM Watson Research Center. In a 1966 paper in the IBM Systems Journal he showed that the algorithm that evicts the page that will be used farthest in the future (OPT or MIN) gives the fewest page faults. In 1969, together with Nelson and Shedler, he found that with FIFO, increasing the number of frames can increase the number of page faults; this is called Bélády's anomaly.

Why it matters for engineering

OPT cannot be implemented, but it is the lower bound against which other replacement algorithms are measured. The anomaly also shows the property that separates stack algorithms such as LRU from FIFO.

Page replacementOPTBélády's anomaly
Why it matters: OPT cannot be implemented, but it is the lower bound against which other replacement algorithms are measured. The anomaly also shows the property that separates stack algorithms such as LRU from FIFO.

GA

Gene Amdahl

Amdahl's law · 1967

One of the chief designers of the IBM System/360 architecture. At the 1967 AFIPS conference he argued that the serial part of a program that cannot be parallelized puts a hard upper bound on speedup. This observation was later formulated as Amdahl's law: if the serial fraction is s, the speedup cannot exceed 1/s no matter how many cores are added.

Why it matters for engineering

The law explains why adding more threads on a multicore processor yields less and less beyond a point, and shows why shortening serial sections guarded by locks matters.

Amdahl's lawSpeedupParallelism
Why it matters: The law explains why adding more threads on a multicore processor yields less and less beyond a point, and shows why shortening serial sections guarded by locks matters.

DK

Donald Knuth

Dynamic memory allocation and the 50-percent rule · 1968

In the first volume of The Art of Computer Programming, published in 1968, he analyzed dynamic memory allocation (first fit, best fit, the buddy system) in detail. The 50-percent rule comes out of this analysis: in a system running first fit, when there are N allocated blocks there are on average about 0.5 N free holes, so roughly one third of memory can become unusable because of fragmentation.

Why it matters for engineering

The rule summarizes in a single number how large external fragmentation can get, and explains why methods that eliminate fragmentation, such as paging, are needed.

External fragmentationFirst fit50-percent rule
Why it matters: The rule summarizes in a single number how large external fragmentation can get, and explains why methods that eliminate fragmentation, such as paging, are needed.

EC

Edward G. Coffman Jr.

The four conditions for deadlock · 1971

In the paper “System Deadlocks,” published with M. J. Elphick and A. Shoshani in ACM Computing Surveys in 1971, he listed the four necessary conditions for deadlock: mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption and circular wait. These conditions are known by his name as the Coffman conditions. The paper also gives a deadlock detection algorithm for resources with multiple instances of each type.

Why it matters for engineering

Every deadlock prevention method works by breaking one of these four conditions; the detection algorithm is also the basis of the form taught in textbooks today.

DeadlockCoffman conditionsDetection
Why it matters: Every deadlock prevention method works by breaking one of these four conditions; the detection algorithm is also the basis of the form taught in textbooks today.

RH

Richard C. Holt

The resource allocation graph · 1971–1972

He presented “Some Deadlock Properties of Computer Systems” at SOSP in 1971 and published an extended version in ACM Computing Surveys in 1972. The graph model, which shows processes and resources as two kinds of nodes and requests and assignments as edges, is known today as the resource allocation graph.

Why it matters for engineering

The graph makes visible how a deadlock arises and is the data structure on which detection algorithms operate: if each resource has a single instance, a cycle in the graph means deadlock.

Resource allocation graphDeadlockGraph model
Why it matters: The graph makes visible how a deadlock arises and is the data structure on which detection algorithms operate: if each resource has a single instance, a cycle in the graph means deadlock.

LL

C. L. Liu and James W. Layland

Rate-monotonic scheduling and EDF · 1973

Liu, of MIT Project MAC, and Layland, of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, studied hard real-time scheduling of periodic tasks in a paper published in the Journal of the ACM in 1973. They proved that rate-monotonic (RM) priorities are optimal among fixed-priority methods and gave the utilization bound n(21/n − 1) for n tasks. The same paper shows that any task set whose total utilization does not exceed 1 can be scheduled by earliest deadline first (EDF).

Why it matters for engineering

The Liu–Layland test tells whether a task set can run without missing deadlines, without building the schedule; it is the theoretical basis of priority assignment in real-time operating systems.

Real-time schedulingRMEDF
Why it matters: The Liu–Layland test tells whether a task set can run without missing deadlines, without building the schedule; it is the theoretical basis of priority assignment in real-time operating systems.

BL

David E. Bell and Leonard J. LaPadula

The Bell–LaPadula model · 1973

At the MITRE Corporation in 1973 they developed a formal model for multilevel security. The model has two basic rules: by the simple security property, a process cannot read objects above its own level (no read up); by the * property, it cannot write to objects below its own level (no write down).

Why it matters for engineering

The model uses rules to prevent information from leaking from a high confidentiality level to a low one; it is the computer counterpart of mandatory access control (MAC) and of military classification schemes.

Multilevel securityConfidentialityMandatory access control
Why it matters: The model uses rules to prevent information from leaking from a high confidentiality level to a low one; it is the computer counterpart of mandatory access control (MAC) and of military classification schemes.
1973
SS

Jerome Saltzer and Michael Schroeder

Principles of protection and secure design · 1975

In their 1975 paper “The Protection of Information in Computer Systems,” published in the Proceedings of the IEEE, they listed eight principles for secure system design. Among them are keeping the mechanism simple, making the default deny, checking every access, open design, privilege separation and least privilege. Saltzer had also worked on the Multics project.

Why it matters for engineering

The principles offer a common yardstick, still used today, for evaluating operating system mechanisms such as access control, setuid programs and privilege separation.

ProtectionLeast privilegeDesign principles
Why it matters: The principles offer a common yardstick, still used today, for evaluating operating system mechanisms such as access control, setuid programs and privilege separation.

DH

Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman

Public-key cryptography · 1976

In their 1976 paper “New Directions in Cryptography,” published in IEEE Transactions on Information Theory, they introduced the idea of public-key cryptography and the key exchange that bears their names. Two parties can produce a shared secret key by sending only public values over an insecure channel. They shared the 2015 Turing Award.

Why it matters for engineering

This exchange, which needs no pre-shared key, underlies the TLS handshake and digital signatures; the network authentication and secure communication services of operating systems rely on it.

Public keyKey exchangeTLS
Why it matters: This exchange, which needs no pre-shared key, underlies the TLS handshake and digital signatures; the network authentication and secure communication services of operating systems rely on it.

KB

Kenneth J. Biba

The Biba integrity model · 1977

In the MITRE Corporation report “Integrity Considerations for Secure Computer Systems” (1977) he described a model for protecting integrity, based on rules that are the reverse of the Bell–LaPadula model. A process cannot read objects at a lower integrity level than its own (no read down) and cannot write to objects at a higher level (no write up).

Why it matters for engineering

The model keeps untrusted data from corrupting trusted components; mandatory integrity controls such as integrity levels in Windows are applications of this idea.

IntegrityMultilevel securityMandatory access control
Why it matters: The model keeps untrusted data from corrupting trusted components; mandatory integrity controls such as integrity levels in Windows are applications of this idea.

GP

Gary L. Peterson

Peterson's algorithm · 1981

In “Myths About the Mutual Exclusion Problem,” published in Information Processing Letters in 1981, he gave a short mutual exclusion solution for two processes that works correctly with only reads and writes. The algorithm combines each process's “I want to enter” flag with a single variable that records whose turn it is.

Why it matters for engineering

The algorithm is the classic example of a solution that satisfies the three requirements of the critical-section problem (mutual exclusion, progress, bounded waiting); the fact that it breaks on modern processors without a memory barrier also shows the importance of memory ordering.

WSClock · 1981

Remote procedure call (RPC) · 1984

They described the RPC package they developed for the Cedar environment at Xerox PARC in the paper “Implementing Remote Procedure Calls,” published in ACM Transactions on Computer Systems in 1984. A procedure on another machine can be called with the syntax of an ordinary local call, thanks to automatically generated client and server stubs.

JG

John Gustafson

Gustafson's law · 1988

Based on his work on a 1024-processor system at Sandia National Laboratories, he published the note “Reevaluating Amdahl's Law” in Communications of the ACM in 1988. He showed that as the number of cores grows, the problem usually grows too, and in that case the scaled speedup increases almost linearly with the number of cores.

Why it matters for engineering

Amdahl answers “how fast will this job finish?”, while Gustafson answers “how large a job can be done in the same time?”; together they are the two sides of evaluating how parallel systems scale.

Scaled speedupParallelismScalability

PG

David Patterson, Garth Gibson and Randy Katz

RAID · 1988

In the paper “A Case for Redundant Arrays of Inexpensive Disks (RAID),” presented at SIGMOD at UC Berkeley in 1988, they proposed building arrays of small, cheap disks protected by redundancy instead of one large, expensive disk. The paper defined five RAID levels and compared their cost and performance. The “I” in the acronym was later redefined as “independent.”

Why it matters for engineering

Striping, mirroring and parity are the basic reliability and performance methods used today in the software RAID layers of operating systems and in storage controllers.

RAIDRedundancyStorage

RO

Mendel Rosenblum and John Ousterhout

The log-structured file system (LFS) · 1991

They presented the log-structured file system (LFS) they developed for the Sprite operating system at SOSP in 1991. The design assumes that most reads will be served from the cache and that the traffic to disk will be mostly writes; so all changes are written to disk as a single long sequential log. The log is divided into segments, and a cleaner compacts the live data in scattered segments to free space.

Why it matters for engineering

By making writes sequential, LFS gained a large performance improvement on small-file workloads and sped up crash recovery; its ideas live on in journaling and copy-on-write file systems and in the translation layers of SSDs.

LFSLog-structuredCrash recovery
Why it matters: By making writes sequential, LFS gained a large performance improvement on small-file workloads and sped up crash recovery; its ideas live on in journaling and copy-on-write file systems and in the translation layers of SSDs.

MS

John Mellor-Crummey and Michael L. Scott

The MCS queue lock · 1991

In their paper published in ACM Transactions on Computer Systems in 1991, they proposed the MCS queue lock, named after their initials. The waiters form a linked list; each waiter spins on its own flag in a separate cache line, and releasing the lock takes a single remote write. This work received the 2006 Dijkstra Prize.

Why it matters for engineering

Locks that spin on a single shared variable drown in cache-coherence traffic as the number of cores grows; MCS is the fundamental example of scalable synchronization that solves this problem, and Linux's qspinlock is based on this idea.

SpinlockScalable synchronizationCache coherence
Why it matters: Locks that spin on a single shared variable drown in cache-coherence traffic as the number of cores grows; MCS is the fundamental example of scalable synchronization that solves this problem, and Linux's qspinlock is based on this idea.

WW

Carl Waldspurger and William Weihl

Lottery and stride scheduling · 1994–1995

In lottery scheduling, which they presented at OSDI in 1994 at MIT, every process is given tickets and in each time slice the owner of a randomly drawn ticket runs; more tickets mean a larger share of the processor. In 1995 Waldspurger and Weihl proposed stride scheduling, which provides the same shares without randomness.

Why it matters for engineering

These methods implemented the idea of proportional share in a simple and understandable way; the weighted fair sharing in Linux's CFS and EEVDF schedulers comes from the same lineage.

Proportional shareLottery schedulingStride scheduling
Why it matters: These methods implemented the idea of proportional share in a simple and understandable way; the weighted fair sharing in Linux's CFS and EEVDF schedulers comes from the same lineage.

The slab allocator · 1994

He published the slab allocator he designed at Sun Microsystems for the SunOS 5.4 (Solaris) kernel at the USENIX Summer conference in 1994. The allocator keeps frequently used kernel objects in prepared caches; objects are exactly sized and packed side by side within a page.

Why it matters for engineering

The slab allocator reduces internal fragmentation and initialization cost for kernel objects; Linux's SLAB and SLUB allocators derive from this design.

Dawson Engler and Frans Kaashoek

Exokernel · 1995

In the exokernel architecture they presented at SOSP in 1995 at MIT, together with James O'Toole, the kernel only allocates hardware resources in a protected way and tracks ownership. Abstractions such as virtual memory and files are provided by library operating systems running in user mode. In the prototype the kernel is called Aegis and the library operating system is called ExOS.

Why it matters for engineering

The slogan “separate management from protection” is the extreme end of the debate over how much functionality belongs in the kernel; the idea lives on today in unikernels and in I/O libraries that bypass the kernel, such as DPDK and SPDK.

SA

Ion Stoica and Hussein Abdel-Wahab

EEVDF · 1995

The “Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First” (EEVDF) algorithm they proposed in 1995 bases proportional-share scheduling on two clear criteria: a task is eligible only when it has fallen behind its share, and among the eligible tasks the one with the earliest virtual deadline is chosen. Peter Zijlstra adapted the algorithm for the Linux kernel; EEVDF replaced CFS starting with version 6.6.

Why it matters for engineering

EEVDF can give latency-sensitive tasks shorter time slices while preserving fairness; it is now the default scheduler for ordinary tasks in Linux.

Ingo Molnár

The O(1) scheduler and CFS · 2000s

He wrote the O(1) scheduler that arrived in the Linux kernel with version 2.6.0. In 2007 he developed the Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS), which replaced the O(1) scheduler in version 2.6.23. CFS emulates an ideal multitasking processor: it keeps each task's virtual runtime (vruntime) and runs the task that has fallen furthest behind in a red-black tree.

Why it matters for engineering

CFS was the default scheduler for ordinary tasks in Linux for more than fifteen years and made weighted fair sharing practical at large scale.
