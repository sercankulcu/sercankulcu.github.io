Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How is uncertainty measured, and how do we draw reliable conclusions from limited data?

Probability is the language for measuring uncertainty; statistics uses it to draw general conclusions from limited data. Every model in AI and data courses works under uncertainty, and only this course can say how far a prediction can be trusted.

Introduction

A Short History

1654

Pascal and Fermat exchange letters on dividing the stakes of an unfinished game of chance.

1763

Bayes's essay is published after his death: probability updated by evidence.

1908

Gosset, writing as "Student", publishes the t distribution for small samples.

1925

Fisher's Statistical Methods for Research Workers popularizes significance testing.

1933

Kolmogorov builds probability on axioms; probability theory rests on measure theory.

1979

Efron proposes the bootstrap: measuring uncertainty by resampling.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: An AI team asks whether a new recommendation model raises clicks. Half of the users see the new model at random (an A/B test), click rates are estimated, a confidence interval for the difference is computed, and if chance cannot explain the difference the model ships.

  1. Counting, probability axioms, conditional probability

  11. Linear regression and inference
  12. Bayesian inference: priors and posteriors

Try It

Does the New Recommender Work?

Does the new recommendation model raise the purchase rate? Half of the visitors see the old model (A), half the new one (B). Change the sample size and the true difference, and repeat the experiment.

With a small sample the intervals are wide: a real improvement comes out “not significant” in most experiments. Computing the required n before starting the test keeps you from throwing away a good model.

Risks and Ethics

p-hacking

Trying many analysis choices and reporting only the "significant" one makes chance look like a finding.

Multiple testing

At the 0.05 level, a hundred comparisons yield about five false positives; without a correction, spurious findings appear.

Simpson's paradox

A trend can reverse when groups are combined; the 1973 Berkeley graduate admissions data is the well-known example.

Selection bias

A non-random sample misleads however large it is, as the 1936 Literary Digest poll showed.

In depth: Explainable AI and AI Ethics.

Practice

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
If p = 0.03, the hypothesis is 97% likely to be true.The p-value is the probability of data this extreme if the null hypothesis holds; it is not the probability of the hypothesis.
Correlation implies causation.A common cause or chance also produces correlation; causation needs an experiment.
If a test is 99% accurate, a positive means 99% disease.If the disease is rare, most positives may be false alarms; Bayes' rule brings in the base rate.
With enough data, statistics is unnecessary.In big data even trivial differences come out "significant"; effect size and bias still matter.
