Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How does a machine act intelligently by searching, reasoning and planning?

Artificial intelligence studies how to design intelligent agents that perceive their environment and choose the action that best serves their goal. The course brings search, logical inference, probabilistic reasoning, planning and learning into one framework. Machine learning is part of that framework, not all of it.

Introduction

A Short History

1950

Turing proposes the imitation game (the Turing test) in "Computing Machinery and Intelligence".

1956

Dartmouth summer workshop: the name "artificial intelligence" is coined and the field is born.

1980s

Expert systems (MYCIN, XCON) go commercial; unmet expectations bring an "AI winter".

1997

Deep Blue beats Kasparov at chess: powerful search and a hand-written evaluation.

2016

AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol at Go: tree search, deep networks and reinforcement learning together.

2022

Large language models reach a mass audience through chat interfaces.

The Course

Core Concepts

Intelligent agent

Perceives the environment through sensors, acts on it through actuators.

Rationality

Choosing the action expected to maximize the performance measure.

State-space search

BFS, DFS, A*; heuristic functions.

Adversarial search

Minimax and alpha-beta pruning in games.

Constraint satisfaction

Variables, domains, constraints: Sudoku, timetabling.

Logic and knowledge

Propositional and first-order logic, inference rules.

Uncertainty

Bayes' rule, Bayesian networks, decision theory.

Planning

Building a sequence of actions from start to goal.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: A navigation app senses its location, represents the road network as a graph, searches routes with A*, picks the fastest one and guides the driver by voice. Nothing in this loop is "learned"; the system is still AI.

The Course

What Does the Course Cover?

  1. Intelligent agents and types of environments
  2. Uninformed search: BFS, DFS, uniform-cost search
  3. Heuristic search: greedy best-first and A*
  4. Local search: hill climbing, simulated annealing
  5. Adversarial search: minimax and alpha-beta pruning
  6. Constraint satisfaction problems and backtracking
  7. Propositional logic and inference by resolution
  8. First-order logic and knowledge representation
  9. Classical planning: STRIPS and PDDL
  10. Probability, Bayes' rule and Bayesian networks
  11. Decision theory and Markov decision processes
  12. Learning from examples and reinforcement learning

Try It

A Warehouse Robot Finds Its Way

A warehouse robot must travel from cell R between the shelves to the item in cell H. Click a cell to add or remove a shelf; compare how many cells the two search methods examine.

Blue cells are states the search examined, orange cells the path found. A* uses the remaining Manhattan distance to the goal as a heuristic, so it usually looks at fewer cells.
The problem is first set up as a state space: each cell is a state, each move an action, the target cell the goal. The search algorithm finds a path in this space; nothing is learned.

Risks and Ethics

The wrong performance measure

An agent maximizes its measure literally: a route planner that only minimizes travel time can push traffic onto side streets past schools.

Search that does not scale

State spaces grow exponentially; a search that takes seconds on a toy problem runs out of memory and time at real size.

An incomplete knowledge base

A system that treats whatever is missing from its rules as false draws confident but wrong conclusions from one forgotten rule.

Responsibility for autonomy

When an autonomous car or weapon system decides on its own, it becomes unclear who answers for its mistakes.

In depth: Explainable AI and AI Ethics.

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
AI and machine learning are the same thing.ML is a subfield of AI. Search, planning and logic are AI even without learning.
AI aims to think like humans.The course mostly aims at acting rationally; imitating humans is only one approach.
Chess engines learn by playing.Classic engines like Deep Blue use search and a hand-written evaluation; learning is not required.
Chatbots are all of AI.Large language models come from NLP and generative models; AI is a much broader field.
