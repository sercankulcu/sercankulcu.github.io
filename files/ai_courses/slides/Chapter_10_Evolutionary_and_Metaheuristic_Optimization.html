Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How do we find a good solution when there is no gradient or the search space is huge?

Evolutionary and metaheuristic optimization searches for a good solution to problems without derivatives or with huge search spaces through a loop of trying, selecting and varying. Inspired by processes such as natural selection, annealing of metals or ant colonies, these methods do not guarantee the optimum but find a good solution quickly.

Introduction

A Short History

1975

Holland publishes "Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems", laying the basis of genetic algorithms.

1983

Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi introduce simulated annealing.

1992

Dorigo proposes ant colony optimization in his PhD thesis.

1995

Kennedy and Eberhart publish particle swarm optimization (PSO).

1997

Wolpert and Macready publish the "no free lunch" theorem: no method wins on all problems.

2002

Deb and colleagues publish NSGA-II for multi-objective search.

The Course

Core Concepts

Representation

How a solution is encoded: bit string, permutation, real vector.

Fitness

The objective value that says how good a solution is.

Selection

Giving good solutions more offspring: tournament, roulette.

Crossover

Combining parts of two parents into a new solution.

Mutation

Changing a solution by small random steps to add diversity.

Exploration vs. exploitation

Balance between searching new regions and refining a good one.

Local search

Hill climbing, simulated annealing, tabu search.

Pareto front

Solutions in a multi-objective problem that do not dominate each other.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: A courier company wants the shortest tour through 50 stops. 100 random tours are generated, each tour's length is measured, short tours are selected as parents, their orders are combined and a few stops are swapped. After hundreds of generations a tour close to the best known solution is found.

Try It

Evolve the Delivery Van's Route

The delivery van leaves the depot (D), visits 11 addresses and returns. A genetic algorithm improves a population of 40 tours by crossover and mutation. Advance generations, change the mutation rate and compare.

Set mutation to 0% and reset: the population fills with near-identical tours and the search often stalls early. Mutation preserves diversity.
An evolutionary method does not search for a solution, it breeds one: it combines parts of good tours and keeps novelty with small changes. It does not guarantee the optimum, but quickly finds a good tour in huge search spaces.

Risks and Ethics

Loopholes in the fitness function

Search finds and exploits flaws in the objective: the score rises but the intended behaviour never appears; evolution runs often show this.

Unfair comparisons

A single run, unequal evaluation budgets or tuning only your own method make a new algorithm look better than it is.

Designs proven only in simulation

An antenna or structure that looks great in simulation can fail in reality because of effects the model leaves out.

Compute and energy cost

Running expensive simulations over thousands of generations can burn days of processor time and a lot of energy.

Practice

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
A genetic algorithm finds the optimal solution.Metaheuristics do not guarantee the optimum; they aim for a good solution in reasonable time.
Nature-inspired methods are more powerful.The inspiration does not determine performance; many newly named methods are minor variants of older ones.
One method is best for every problem.By the "no free lunch" theorem, no method is better on average over all problems.
Use evolutionary methods even when derivatives exist.When derivatives are available, gradient methods usually reach the result with far fewer evaluations.
