Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How is an expert's knowledge written down and used for inference?

An expert system writes a domain expert's knowledge into a knowledge base of rules and facts and draws conclusions from it with an inference engine. Knowledge representation studies how to encode knowledge so that a computer can reason with it: logic, frames, semantic networks and ontologies.

Introduction

A Short History

1965

DENDRAL starts at Stanford: the first expert system, inferring molecular structure from mass spectra.

1972

Colmerauer and Roussel develop Prolog: logic programming is born.

1970s

MYCIN recommends antibiotics for blood infections, adding certainty factors to its rules.

1980

R1 (XCON) starts configuring computer orders at DEC: the first big commercial success.

1984

Lenat launches Cyc, aiming to hand-code common-sense knowledge.

2012

Google announces the Knowledge Graph; knowledge graphs spread in search.

The Course

Core Concepts

Knowledge base

Domain facts and IF-THEN rules.

Inference engine

Applies rules to facts to derive new conclusions.

Forward chaining

Start from facts and derive every reachable conclusion.

Backward chaining

Start from a goal and look for facts that support it.

Explanation facility

Shows which rules led to a conclusion.

Frames and networks

Representing objects with slots and relations.

Ontology

A domain's concepts, classes and relations: OWL.

Knowledge acquisition

Eliciting knowledge from experts; the bottleneck.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: A fault diagnosis system turns technicians' experience into rules such as "if the engine won't start and battery voltage is low, replace the battery". It asks the user about symptoms, chains the rules to find the fault and shows step by step which rules it used.

Try It

Why Won't the Car Start?

A mechanic's knowledge was written as nine IF–THEN rules. Click the symptoms on the left to mark them; the system fires rules one by one by forward chaining, reaches a diagnosis and explains why.

Knowledge lives in a rule base separate from the code; the inference engine chains the rules. When the mechanic adds a rule, the program does not change, and the system can explain which rules produced each decision.

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
Expert systems were a 1980s fad.Under new names, business rule engines and knowledge graphs are used today in banking, healthcare and search.
An expert system knows as much as the expert.It knows only what was encoded; it fails quickly outside its domain or when common sense is needed.
Writing the rules is easy.Much expert knowledge is tacit; turning it into rules (knowledge acquisition) is the hardest and costliest step.
Large language models made knowledge representation obsolete.Language models can produce false facts; they are paired with knowledge graphs for verifiable results.
