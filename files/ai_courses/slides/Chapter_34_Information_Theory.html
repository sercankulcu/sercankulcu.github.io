Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How is information measured, and how is it compressed and transmitted with the least loss?

Information theory measures the information in a message by probability: an unlikely event carries more information, and the average information is called entropy. The course derives the limits on how far data can be compressed and how fast it can be sent reliably over a noisy channel.

Introduction

A Short History

1928

Hartley proposes measuring information by the logarithm of the number of possible messages.

1948

Shannon defines entropy and channel capacity in "A Mathematical Theory of Communication".

1951

Kullback and Leibler publish a divergence measure between two probability distributions.

1952

Huffman publishes the algorithm that builds the prefix code with minimum average length.

1977

Lempel and Ziv publish the dictionary-based LZ77 compressor, ancestor of ZIP and PNG.

1993

Turbo codes arrive: a practical code comes within about half a decibel of the Shannon limit.

The Course

Core Concepts

Entropy

Average information per symbol of a source, in bits.

Conditional entropy

Uncertainty left in one variable once the other is known.

Mutual information

How much two variables tell about each other.

KL divergence

Extra cost of coding one distribution with a code built for another.

Cross-entropy

The most common loss function in classification.

Source coding

Lossless compression: Huffman, arithmetic coding, LZ.

Channel capacity

Highest rate that can pass reliably through a noisy channel.

Error-correcting codes

Adding redundancy to detect and correct errors.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: A photo sent from a phone is first compressed with JPEG; the wireless channel code then adds controlled redundancy. Bits corrupted in the air are corrected with that redundancy at the receiver and the photo opens. Compression and protection look like opposites, yet both come from the same theory.

Try It

Send the Message in Fewer Bits

A battery-powered sensor sends its messages by radio, and every bit costs energy. Type a text or pick a preset: the Huffman tree gives frequent letters short codes. Click a letter in the tree to see its code.

Information theory sets the limit: no lossless code can be shorter than the entropy on average. Huffman stays within one bit of it. When all letters are equally frequent, the saving comes only from the small alphabet.

Practice

Risks and Ethics

Mutual information from little data

Histogram estimates of mutual information are biased upward on small samples; an irrelevant feature looks informative.

The wrong KL direction

KL(p‖q) and KL(q‖p) give different models: one pushes to cover every mode, the other to lock onto a single mode.

Secrets leaked by compression

Compressing secret data together with attacker-chosen text lets the size reveal the secret; the CRIME attack on TLS did this.

Assuming independence

Treating symbols as independent overstates entropy; compression estimates and randomness assessments go wrong.

In depth: Explainable AI and AI Ethics.

Practice

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
Information is measured by the meaning of a message.Shannon information ignores meaning; it measures how unexpected a message is, that is, its probability.
A good program can shrink any file.No lossless method shrinks every input; data cannot be compressed below its entropy.
Error-free transmission over a noisy channel is impossible.Below capacity the error probability can be made as small as desired (Shannon).
KL divergence is a distance.It is not symmetric and violates the triangle inequality: p to q and q to p give different values.
