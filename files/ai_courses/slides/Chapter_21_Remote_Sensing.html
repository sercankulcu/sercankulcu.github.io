Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

What can satellite and aerial images tell us about the Earth?

Remote sensing extracts information about the Earth without touching it, by measuring reflected and emitted energy with sensors on satellites, aircraft or drones. An image is not just a color photo but multiband data, where each band carries a different physical measurement.

Introduction

A Short History

1858

Nadar photographs Paris from a balloon: the first known aerial photograph.

1960

The first weather satellite, TIROS-1, is launched; clouds are imaged routinely from space.

1972

Landsat 1 is launched: the first civilian program to monitor the Earth in multiple bands.

1978

Seasat becomes the first civilian satellite to collect synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images from space.

2008

The US Geological Survey (USGS) opens the Landsat archive to everyone for free.

2015

Sentinel-2A is launched; Copernicus open data starts an era of frequent, free imagery.

The Course

Core Concepts

Electromagnetic spectrum

Visible, infrared, thermal and microwave bands.

Passive and active

Optical sensors measure sunlight; radar sends its own signal.

Types of resolution

Spatial, spectral, temporal and radiometric.

Spectral signature

Water, plants and soil reflect differently in each band.

Band indices

NDVI for vegetation, NDWI for water.

Corrections

Atmospheric, terrain and geometric correction.

Land classification

Labeling each pixel as forest, field, urban or water.

Change detection

Comparing images from different dates.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: Sentinel-2 images taken before and after a forest fire are corrected for the atmosphere. A burn index is computed from the near-infrared and shortwave infrared bands; the difference between the two dates reveals the burned area. The result is measured in hectares and drawn on a map.

Try It

Measuring Forest Loss from Space

Two satellite images of the same area, from two dates, have red and near-infrared (NIR) bands. Find vegetation with NDVI, slide the threshold and measure how many hectares of forest were lost; click a pixel to see its value.

Remote sensing turns bands the eye cannot see into a physical indicator. The course teaches band ratios, masking disturbances such as clouds, and measuring change as area.

Risks and Ethics

Surveillance and privacy

High-resolution satellite and drone imagery can track people's homes, vehicles and movements without consent.

Maps without ground truth

A classification map never checked in the field can mislead farming or disaster decisions despite high reported accuracy.

Clouds and shadows

Unmasked clouds and shadows look like forest loss or flooding in change detection.

Models that do not transfer

A model trained on European data fails silently in other climates and farming patterns; each region needs validation.

Practice

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
A satellite image is an ordinary color photo.It also has infrared, thermal and radar bands that the eye cannot see.
Higher spatial resolution is always better.For wide areas and frequent revisits, medium resolution is often more suitable.
If there are clouds, the satellite sees nothing.Optical sensors cannot; radar (SAR) sees through clouds and at night.
This is just an image processing course.Sensor physics, the atmosphere, map projections and field validation are also part of it.
