Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How are the most relevant documents or products picked from millions of candidates?

Information retrieval studies systems that find the documents most relevant to a query among millions and rank them. Recommender systems do the same job without a query: they predict what a user will like from past behavior. Both return a ranked list, not an answer.

Introduction

A Short History

1972

Spärck Jones proposes inverse document frequency (IDF): a rare term says more.

1975

Salton and colleagues publish the vector space model: documents and queries are term vectors.

1992

Xerox PARC's Tapestry system coins the term "collaborative filtering".

1998

Brin and Page introduce the Google search engine, built on PageRank.

2006

The Netflix Prize begins; matrix factorization stands out in the winning solutions.

2020

Dense vector retrieval and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) begin to spread.

The Course

Core Concepts

Inverted index

For each term, a list of the documents containing it.

TF-IDF and BM25

Scoring documents by term frequency and rarity.

Vector space model

Measuring similarity by the cosine of the angle.

Precision, recall

Share of results that are relevant; share of relevant ones found.

Ranking metrics

MAP, MRR, NDCG: position in the list matters.

Collaborative filtering

Recommending from what similar users liked.

Matrix factorization

Splitting the user-item table into latent factors.

Cold start

New users or items with no history.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: A video platform collects watch logs, indexes videos as vectors, retrieves a few hundred candidates from millions, ranks them by probability of being watched, and compares the new ranker with the old one in an A/B test.

Try It

Searching a Help Center

A shopping site's help center has nine articles, and customers type questions in their own words. Pick a sample question or type your own; articles are ranked by TF-IDF weights and cosine similarity.

IDF down-weights words such as “my” and “order” that appear in many articles, so the distinctive “damaged” stands out. Compare with the TF button. Cutting each word to its first four letters is a crude stemmer: damage, damaged → dama.

Practice

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
A search engine just finds the words in the query.The real work is ranking thousands of matches; users rarely look past the first page.
Recommending the top-rated items is enough.The same list for everyone is not personalization; diversity and novelty are measured too.
High accuracy means good recommendations.A model with low rating error can still rank badly; rank metrics such as NDCG are needed.
Dense vectors have made classic search obsolete.BM25 is still a strong baseline; many systems combine the two.
