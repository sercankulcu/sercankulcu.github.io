Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

When can we trust an AI system's decision, and who is responsible for the outcome?

This course studies how to explain an AI system's decisions in a way people can understand, and how to make the system fair, reliable and accountable. The technical question is "why did the model decide this?"; the ethical question is "whom can this decision harm, and who is responsible?"

Introduction

A Short History

2016

ProPublica publishes an analysis arguing that the COMPAS recidivism score is biased against Black defendants.

2016

Ribeiro, Singh and Guestrin introduce LIME: explaining one prediction with a simple local model.

2017

Lundberg and Lee publish SHAP: feature contributions from game-theoretic Shapley values.

2018

The EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) takes effect, granting a right to contest automated decisions.

2018

The Gender Shades study shows commercial face analysis systems err far more often on darker-skinned women.

2024

The EU AI Act is adopted: systems are classified by risk level.

The Course

Core Concepts

Black-box model

A model whose inner workings people cannot follow.

Interpretability

A model readable by design, such as a decision tree or a linear model.

Local explanation

Explaining one prediction: LIME, SHAP values.

Global explanation

The model's overall behavior: feature importance, PDP.

Counterfactual

"Had your income been this much, the loan would be approved."

Data bias

Imbalance in training data carried into decisions.

Fairness metrics

Demographic parity, equal opportunity, calibration.

Accountability

Auditing, logging and ownership of a decision.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: A bank's credit model rejects an application. SHAP values show the rejection comes mostly from the debt ratio; a comparison across groups reveals markedly higher rejection rates in some neighborhoods. The team removes the feature that indirectly encodes neighborhood and documents the decision in the model card.

The Course

What Does the Course Cover?

  1. Interpretable models: linear models and trees
  2. Feature importance and partial dependence (PDP, ICE)
  3. Local explanations: LIME
  4. Shapley values and SHAP
  5. Saliency maps for deep networks
  6. Counterfactual explanations and recourse
  7. Evaluating explanations: faithfulness and stability
  8. Fairness metrics and the conflicts between them
  9. Bias mitigation: pre-, in- and post-processing
  10. Privacy: anonymization and differential privacy
  11. Accountability: model cards, datasheets, audits
  12. Regulation: GDPR, KVKK and the EU AI Act

Try It

Is the Credit Model Fair to Both Groups?

The model scores each application from 0 to 100; scores above the threshold are approved. Group B scores lower because of historical data. Move the thresholds and watch how two fairness metrics change.

The same threshold treats the two groups differently; closing one gap opens the other. The course teaches making these measurements, stating openly which fairness definition was chosen, and making the model auditable.

Risks and Ethics

Misleading explanations

LIME and SHAP are approximations; an adversarial model can look innocent to the explainer while hiding its real decision rule.

Ethics washing

An ethics principles document or a single fairness score can serve as a showcase in place of real auditing.

The wrong fairness metric

Choosing a metric is a value judgement: COMPAS could look fair under one metric and biased under another.

Over-trusting explanations

A plausible-looking explanation can lead users to follow a wrong decision without question.

Practice

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
Removing the protected attribute (gender, race) is enough.Proxy features such as postal code or name can carry the same information; the bias remains.
An algorithm is neutral because it works with numbers.A model learns the bias in past decisions from the data and scales it up.
An explanation shows how the model really works.Methods such as LIME and SHAP are approximations; the explanation itself can mislead.
All fairness metrics can be satisfied at once.When base rates differ between groups, calibration and equal error rates usually cannot hold together.
