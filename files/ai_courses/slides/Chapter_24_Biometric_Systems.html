Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How is a person recognized from their face, fingerprint or voice?

Biometric systems recognize a person by physical and behavioral traits such as face, fingerprint, iris or voice. The course covers extracting a template from a sample, matching two templates and accepting or rejecting by a threshold; security and privacy are an inseparable part of that decision.

Introduction

A Short History

1892

Galton publishes "Finger Prints": fingerprints are shown to be unique and permanent.

1901

Scotland Yard starts using fingerprints with the Henry classification system.

1991

Turk and Pentland publish eigenfaces for face recognition.

1993

Daugman publishes his iris recognition method, the basis of today's iris systems.

2013

Fingerprint sensors (Touch ID) start to spread in smartphones.

2014

DeepFace and the deep networks after it approach human level in face verification.

The Course

Core Concepts

Enrollment

Extracting a template from a person's sample and storing it.

Template

The feature vector stored instead of the raw image.

Verification (1:1)

"Is this person who they claim to be?"

Identification (1:N)

"Which enrolled person is this?"

FAR and FRR

False accept and false reject rates; the threshold trades them off.

EER and DET curve

The point where both errors are equal; comparing systems.

Liveness detection

Catching attacks with photos, masks or fake fingers.

Multimodal biometrics

Combining face with voice, or fingerprint with iris.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: A phone's face unlock captures the face with an infrared camera, detects and aligns it, extracts an embedding with a deep network, measures similarity to the enrolled template and unlocks if the score passes the threshold. A liveness check stops a photo from unlocking it.

Try It

Who Should the Gate Let In?

A face recognition gate gives each person a similarity score between 0 and 1; if the score passes the threshold, the gate opens. Move the threshold or pick a scenario to see the trade-off between accepting impostors and rejecting genuine users.

A biometric system never says “certain”: the two distributions overlap, and the threshold is set by which error costs more. The course teaches the feature extraction that produces these scores and evaluation with FAR/FRR.

Risks and Ethics

Mass surveillance

Real-time face recognition in public spaces makes it possible to track everyone without asking; many countries restrict it by law.

Arrests from false matches

In the US there are documented cases of innocent people being detained on the strength of a false face recognition match.

Function creep

Face or fingerprint data collected for door access can be reused for other purposes; GDPR and similar laws treat it as special-category data.

Central template stores

Holding millions of templates in one place exposes everyone at once; the 2015 US OPM breach leaked 5.6 million fingerprints.

Practice

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
Biometric systems make no mistakes.Every system makes false accepts and false rejects; the threshold chooses between them.
Biometric data can be changed like a password.A face or fingerprint cannot be changed; a leaked template is a permanent risk, so templates need protection.
Accuracy is the same for everyone.Performance can vary with age, skin tone and sensor; evaluation is done per group.
A match means the person is there.Presentation attacks use photos, masks or recorded voice; liveness detection is needed.
