Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How do X-ray, CT and MRI images help diagnosis and treatment?

Medical image processing enhances, segments, measures and compares X-ray, CT, MRI and ultrasound images to support diagnosis and treatment. It applies image processing and computer vision methods under the constraints of little data, expensive labels and patient safety.

Introduction

A Short History

1895

Röntgen discovers X-rays; the first medical image is an X-ray of his wife's hand.

1972

Hounsfield's computed tomography (CT) scanner is introduced: cross-sections are obtained by computation.

1973

Lauterbur publishes the first imaging paper using magnetic resonance, laying the foundation of MRI.

1993

The DICOM 3.0 standard is published; devices from different vendors speak the same file format.

2015

U-Net is published: an encoder-decoder network for segmentation with few labeled medical images.

2018

The US FDA authorizes IDx-DR, which screens for diabetic retinopathy without a physician.

The Course

Core Concepts

Imaging modalities

X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound, PET: each a different physics.

DICOM

A standard that carries the image and patient data together.

Voxels and volumes

CT and MRI are 3D; voxels instead of pixels.

Denoising

Preserving quality in low-dose CT and in MRI.

Segmentation

Outlining an organ, tumor or vessel.

Registration

Aligning two images (before/after, CT/MRI).

Computer-aided diagnosis (CAD)

The computer flags suspicious regions for the physician.

Clinical evaluation

Sensitivity, specificity, Dice; comparison with physicians.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: Hundreds of slices from a lung CT scan are read as DICOM, the lung region is segmented and a network flags suspicious nodules. Registered against the previous scan, the growth in nodule volume is measured. The radiologist makes the final call; the system only draws attention.

  1. Imaging physics: X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound

  3. Image reconstruction: filtered back-projection
  4. Denoising and image enhancement
  5. Classical segmentation: thresholds, regions, level sets
  6. Deep segmentation: U-Net and nnU-Net
  7. Rigid and deformable registration
  8. Atlas-based analysis and volumetry
  9. Classification and computer-aided diagnosis
  10. Learning from little data: augmentation, transfer
  11. Clinical validation: Dice, ROC, reader studies
  12. Regulation, privacy and clinical deployment

Try It

Segment the Tumor in an MRI Slice

In a contrast-enhanced brain MRI slice the tumor appears bright; the radiologist wants its volume. Click inside the tumor to place a seed and change the tolerance: does the region cover the tumor or leak into healthy tissue?

■ found region   ▭ expert-drawn boundary. Pixel 3×3 mm. The closer Dice is to 1, the better the segmentation.
In medical imaging a segmentation becomes a number: volume, growth, response to treatment. The course teaches measuring a method against expert labels with Dice, and preprocessing against noise.

Practice

Risks and Ethics

Shortcut learning

A model can learn scanner labels or hospital markers instead of disease; this has been shown in chest X-ray models.

Failure at another hospital

Performance drops with other scanners, protocols and patients; validation at a single site is not enough.

Re-identification

DICOM headers hold identifiers and faces can be rebuilt from head MRI; anonymization is more than deleting a name.

Automation bias

Clinicians may miss a lesion the model did not flag; a support tool can loosen human scrutiny.

Practice

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
AI will soon replace radiologists.Systems support narrow tasks; diagnosis, context and responsibility stay with the physician.
High accuracy means clinical success.For rare diseases sensitivity and specificity matter; performance may drop at another hospital.
A medical image is an ordinary photo.CT and MRI are often 3D, and pixel values carry physical meaning (Hounsfield units).
Collecting lots of data is easy.Labels take expert time, and the data is protected by privacy law.
