Introduction

What Question Does It Ask?

How do many-layered networks learn useful representations from raw data?

Deep learning studies how multilayer neural networks learn useful intermediate representations from raw data (pixels, audio, text) on their own, layer by layer. The key idea is representation learning instead of hand-designed features; big data, GPUs and good architectures make it possible.

Introduction

A Short History

1997

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber propose the LSTM: a cure for vanishing gradients on long sequences.

1998

LeCun et al.'s LeNet-5 convolutional network recognizes handwritten digits.

2006

Hinton et al. train deep belief networks with layer-wise pretraining; interest revives.

2012

AlexNet, a convolutional network trained on GPUs, wins ImageNet by a wide margin.

2015

He et al.'s residual networks (ResNet) win ImageNet with 152 layers.

2017

Vaswani et al. introduce the transformer architecture: attention only.

The Course

Core Concepts

Representation learning

Layers learn ever more abstract features, from edges to objects.

Convolution

Small filters slide over the image; weights are shared.

Pooling

Shrinks spatial size, adds robustness to small shifts.

Recurrent network

RNN and LSTM: process a sequence step by step, carrying a state.

Attention

Each element weighs which other elements of the sequence to look at.

Residual connection

A path that skips a layer; makes very deep networks trainable.

Normalization

Batch and layer normalization stabilize training.

Transfer learning

Adapting a model trained on big data to a new task.

The Course

A Typical Workflow

Example: An app that recognizes plant diseases collects leaf photos, takes a ResNet pretrained on ImageNet, replaces its last layer with the disease classes and fine-tunes it on a GPU with data augmentation. No feature is defined by hand.

Try It

How Do Filters See a Line?

A school app will recognize signs drawn by children. Click the left grid to draw; two convolution filters scan the picture, and class scores come out of the feature maps.

Draw a diagonal line: both filters barely respond. The network needs other filters for other patterns.
The nine numbers in each filter were written by hand; a deep network learns them from data and, by stacking layers, climbs from edges to shapes and from shapes to objects.

Risks and Ethics

Compute and energy cost

Training large models takes thousands of GPU hours and real carbon emissions, concentrating research in a few large organizations.

Adversarial examples

Imperceptible pixel changes fool a classifier; researchers showed the same with stickers placed on a stop sign.

Data scraped from the web

Web-scraped datasets such as ImageNet can contain photos taken without consent and offensive labels; ImageNet later removed many of them.

Illusory progress

Hyperparameters tuned on the test set and results reported from a single seed make small differences look like real gains.

In depth: Explainable AI and AI Ethics.

Common Misconceptions

Common beliefIn fact
Deep learning is best for every problem.On small tabular data, gradient-boosted trees are often better and cheaper.
A deep network is a black box that cannot be inspected.Tools such as gradient-based saliency maps and attention weights give partial explanations.
Deep learning was invented in 2012.The ideas are decades old; big data and GPUs brought the 2012 leap.
More layers always mean better results.Without residual connections and normalization, very deep networks may not train.
