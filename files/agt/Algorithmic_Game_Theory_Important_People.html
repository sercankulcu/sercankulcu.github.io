Important People in Algorithmic Game Theory

53 profiles of the mathematicians, economists and computer scientists behind the results used in the course, from Borda and Condorcet to the price of anarchy and PPAD. Each card links to the course chapters where the person's work appears; open "More" for details.

JB

Jean-Charles de Borda

1733–1799French · naval officer and mathematician, French Academy of Sciences

Borda count · 1770

Proposed the Borda count, a positional voting rule that awards m−1, m−2, …, 0 points to the alternatives in each ranking.

positional scoringranked ballotsCondorcet criterion
Course chaptersCh. 6 · Voting

Why it matters: The Borda count is the classic scoring rule; it never elects a Condorcet loser but can fail to elect the Condorcet winner.

Borda presented his method to the Academy in 1770; it was published in 1784. It is easy to manipulate by burying strong rivals, a weakness later made precise by the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem.

MC

Marquis de Condorcet

1743–1794French · philosopher and mathematician

Condorcet paradox · jury theorem · 1785

Showed in his 1785 Essai that pairwise majority voting can be cyclic (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A).

pairwise majorityCondorcet winnerjury theorem
Course chaptersCh. 6 · Voting

Why it matters: The Condorcet winner and the voting paradox are the starting point of social choice theory and of Arrow's impossibility theorem.

His jury theorem states that if each voter is independently correct with probability p > 1/2, majority accuracy tends to 1 as the electorate grows. He died in prison during the French Revolution.

ÉB

Émile Borel

1871–1956French · University of Paris (also Minister of the Navy, 1925)

Mixed strategies · 1921

Formulated games of strategy with mixed (randomised) strategies in a series of notes from 1921 to 1927.

mixed strategieszero-sum gamesrandomisation
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basics

Why it matters: Borel posed the minimax question for symmetric zero-sum games; von Neumann proved the general minimax theorem in 1928.

Borel solved small cases but doubted that the minimax property held in general. The priority discussion between Borel and von Neumann was revisited in Econometrica in 1953.

EZ

Ernst Zermelo

1871–1953German · Göttingen, Zurich, Freiburg

Zermelo's theorem on chess · 1913

Is credited with the first formal theorem of game theory (1913): in chess, either White can force a win, Black can force a win, or both can force at least a draw.

perfect informationdeterminacybackward induction
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basicsCh. 2 · Introduction to AGT

Why it matters: The result underlies backward induction and the determinacy of finite two-player games of perfect information.

Zermelo is better known for the axioms of set theory (1908). His chess paper is the ancestor of game-tree search used for Tic-Tac-Toe, Nim and Connect Four in the course labs.

AP

Arthur Cecil Pigou

1877–1959English · University of Cambridge

Pigou's example · externalities · 1912/1920

Described two roads, one congestible and one fixed-delay, where selfish drivers overuse the congestible road (Wealth and Welfare, 1912; The Economics of Welfare, 1920).

externalitiesPigouvian taxcongestion
Course chaptersCh. 8 · Network routingCh. 11 · Price of anarchy

Why it matters: Pigou's example is the canonical selfish-routing instance: with linear latencies its price of anarchy is 4/3, the worst case proved by Roughgarden and Tardos.

He proposed taxes on activities with negative externalities; congestion pricing (marginal-cost tolls) is the network version that makes the Wardrop equilibrium optimal.

HS

Hugo Steinhaus

1887–1972Polish · Lwów and Wrocław universities

Fair cake cutting · 1948

Founded the mathematical theory of fair division with "The Problem of Fair Division" (Econometrica, 1948).

proportionalitycut-and-choosecake cutting
Course chaptersCh. 10 · Fairness and ethics

Why it matters: He gave a proportional three-player procedure and reported the Banach–Knaster last-diminisher protocol for n players.

Fair division asks for allocations that are proportional (each gets at least 1/n by own valuation) or envy-free. Later work by Brams, Taylor, Procaccia and others built the algorithmic theory.

OM

Oskar Morgenstern

1902–1977German-born Austrian-American · Princeton University

Theory of Games and Economic Behavior · 1944

Co-authored Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944) with von Neumann, establishing game theory as a discipline in economics.

expected utilitystrategic-form gamesrational choice
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basicsCh. 13 · Decision making

Why it matters: The von Neumann–Morgenstern expected-utility axioms justify modelling players as maximisers of expected payoff.

The utility axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence) were added in the 1947 second edition. Behavioural work by Allais, Kahneman and Tversky later tested them.

JvN

John von Neumann

1903–1957Hungarian-American · Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton

Minimax theorem · 1928

Proved the minimax theorem for two-player zero-sum games (1928) and, with Morgenstern, wrote Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944).

zero-sum gamesmixed strategiessaddle points
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basicsCh. 3 · Nash equilibriumCh. 13 · Decision making

Why it matters: Minimax connects strategic optimisation with linear programming duality and adversarial search.

In every finite two-player zero-sum game, max-min equals min-max in mixed strategies. The 1944 book introduced cooperative games (stable sets) and, in its 1947 edition, the expected-utility axioms.

WV

William Vickrey

1914–1996Canadian-American · Columbia University

Second-price auction · 1961

Analysed auctions in "Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders" (1961), showing that in a sealed-bid second-price auction bidding one's value is a dominant strategy.

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 1996 (with James Mirrlees); he died three days after the announcement

second-price auctiondominant strategiesrevenue equivalence
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism designCh. 5 · AuctionsCh. 9 · Online platforms

Why it matters: The Vickrey auction is the first VCG mechanism and the template for truthful mechanism design.

The same paper anticipated revenue equivalence between the standard auction formats. Vickrey also advocated congestion pricing for roads and transit.

HS

Herbert A. Simon

1916–2001American · Carnegie Mellon University

Bounded rationality · satisficing · 1955

Introduced bounded rationality: real decision makers have limited information and computation, so they satisfice instead of optimise.

Prizes: Turing Award 1975 (with Allen Newell); Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 1978

bounded rationalitysatisficingheuristics
Course chaptersCh. 3 · Nash equilibriumCh. 13 · Decision making

Why it matters: Bounded rationality motivates computational limits on agents, a recurring theme in AGT and multi-agent AI.

"A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice" (1955) argued that search costs make optimisation unrealistic. Simon was also a founder of artificial intelligence (Logic Theorist, 1956).

GB

George W. Brown

1917–2005American · RAND, UCLA, UC Irvine

Fictitious play · 1951

Proposed fictitious play (1951): each player best-responds to the empirical frequency of the opponent's past actions.

fictitious playbest responseempirical frequencies
Course chaptersCh. 12 · Learning and evolution

Why it matters: Fictitious play is the first learning dynamic for games; Julia Robinson proved in 1951 that it converges in two-player zero-sum games.

Shapley (1964) gave a 3×3 game where fictitious play cycles, showing that simple learning need not reach Nash equilibrium in general games.

LH

Leonid Hurwicz

1917–2008Polish-American (born in Moscow) · University of Minnesota

Mechanism design · incentive compatibility · 1972

Founded mechanism design theory and introduced the notion of incentive compatibility (1972).

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2007 (with Eric Maskin and Roger Myerson); at 90, the oldest laureate at the time

incentive compatibilityinformational decentralisationimplementation
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism design

Why it matters: Mechanism design reverses game theory: choose the rules so that self-interested behaviour produces the desired outcome.

Hurwicz showed that no efficient, individually rational mechanism can be dominant-strategy incentive compatible in exchange economies, framing the trade-offs that the field studies.

BH

David Blackwell & James Hannan

1919–2010 · 1922–2010American · UC Berkeley (Blackwell); Michigan State University (Hannan)

Approachability 1956 · Hannan consistency 1957

Laid the foundations of no-regret learning: Hannan (1957) built a strategy with vanishing external regret in repeated games; Blackwell (1956) proved the approachability theorem for vector payoffs.

Prizes: Blackwell: first Black member of the US National Academy of Sciences (1965); von Neumann Theory Prize 1979

external regretapproachabilityrepeated games
Course chaptersCh. 12 · Learning and evolution

Why it matters: No-regret algorithms such as Hedge and regret matching descend from these results and converge to coarse correlated equilibria.

Blackwell's theorem characterises which convex sets a player can force the average vector payoff to approach; it yields Hannan consistency and calibration as corollaries.

JH

John Harsanyi

1920–2000Hungarian-American · UC Berkeley

Bayesian games · 1967–68

Developed the framework for games with incomplete information ("Games with Incomplete Information Played by Bayesian Players", 1967–68).

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 1994 (with John Nash and Reinhard Selten)

type spacescommon priorsBayesian Nash equilibrium
Course chaptersCh. 3 · Nash equilibriumCh. 4 · Mechanism design

Why it matters: Bayesian games model private types (values, costs) drawn from a common prior; they are the basis of auction and mechanism theory.

The Harsanyi transformation adds a chance move ("Nature") that draws each player's type, turning incomplete information into imperfect information. His purification theorem (1973) reinterprets mixed strategies.

JM

John Maynard Smith

1920–2004British · University of Sussex

Evolutionarily stable strategy · 1973

Introduced the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) with George Price in "The Logic of Animal Conflict" (Nature, 1973).

Prizes: Crafoord Prize 1999 (with Ernst Mayr and George C. Williams)

ESSHawk–Dovereplicator dynamics
Course chaptersCh. 12 · Learning and evolution

Why it matters: ESS and the replicator dynamics (Taylor and Jonker, 1978) bring game theory to populations without rational players.

A strategy is evolutionarily stable if, once adopted by a population, no small group of mutants can invade. Every ESS is a symmetric Nash equilibrium. Book: Evolution and the Theory of Games (1982).

DG

David Gale

1921–2008American · UC Berkeley

Deferred acceptance · 1962

Co-invented the deferred-acceptance algorithm with Shapley ("College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage", 1962).

stable matchingblocking pairsdeferred acceptance
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism designCh. 5 · Auctions

Why it matters: Deferred acceptance always finds a stable matching in O(n²) proposals; it is proposer-optimal and strategy-proof for the proposing side.

Gale died in 2008, before the 2012 Nobel Prize recognised the theory of stable allocations. He also contributed to linear programming duality and the game of Hex (Gale proved Hex cannot end in a draw).

KA

Kenneth Arrow

1921–2017American · Stanford University

Arrow's impossibility theorem · 1951

Proved in Social Choice and Individual Values (1951) that no ranking aggregation rule satisfies a small set of natural axioms.

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 1972 (with John Hicks)

preference aggregationIIAnon-dictatorship
Course chaptersCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 4 · Mechanism designCh. 6 · Voting

Why it matters: With three or more alternatives, any rule satisfying unanimity (Pareto) and independence of irrelevant alternatives is a dictatorship.

Arrow's theorem started axiomatic social choice. He also co-proved the existence of competitive equilibrium (Arrow–Debreu, 1954) using fixed-point arguments akin to Nash's.

TS

Thomas Schelling

1921–2016American · Harvard University, University of Maryland

Focal points · commitment · segregation model

Analysed conflict and coordination in The Strategy of Conflict (1960), introducing focal (Schelling) points and credible commitment.

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2005 (with Robert Aumann)

focal pointscommitmentmicromotives and macrobehaviour
Course chaptersCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 3 · Nash equilibriumCh. 7 · Multi-agent systems

Why it matters: His segregation model (1969–71) showed how mild individual preferences produce strong collective segregation, an early agent-based simulation.

Schelling showed that limiting one's own options (burning bridges) can be strategically valuable, a theme in deterrence and bargaining.

JW

John Wardrop

1922–1989English · Road Research Laboratory, University College London

Wardrop equilibrium · 1952

Stated Wardrop's two principles of traffic assignment (1952): user equilibrium and system optimum.

user equilibriumsystem optimumtraffic assignment
Course chaptersCh. 8 · Network routingCh. 11 · Price of anarchy

Why it matters: In a Wardrop equilibrium every used route has equal and minimal latency; the price of anarchy compares it with the system optimum.

Beckmann, McGuire and Winsten (1956) showed that Wardrop equilibria minimise a potential function, which makes them computable by convex optimisation.

LS

Lloyd Shapley

1923–2016American · RAND Corporation, UCLA

Shapley value · stochastic games · matching

Introduced the Shapley value (1953) and stochastic games (1953), and co-invented deferred acceptance with Gale (1962).

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2012 (with Alvin Roth)

Shapley valuecoalitionscore
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism designCh. 5 · AuctionsCh. 7 · Multi-agent systems

Why it matters: The Shapley value is the unique allocation rule satisfying efficiency, symmetry, additivity and the null-player property.

The value averages a player's marginal contribution over all arrival orders; it is used today for cost sharing and to explain machine-learning predictions (SHAP). The Bondareva–Shapley theorem characterises games with a non-empty core.

JN

John Nash

1928–2015American · Princeton University, MIT

Nash equilibrium · 1950–51

Defined Nash equilibrium and proved that every finite game has one in mixed strategies (PNAS 1950; Annals of Mathematics 1951).

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 1994 (with John Harsanyi and Reinhard Selten); Abel Prize 2015 (with Louis Nirenberg)

best responsefixed pointsbargaining solution
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basicsCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 3 · Nash equilibrium

Why it matters: Nash equilibrium is the central solution concept of non-cooperative game theory; its existence proof uses a fixed-point theorem.

The 1950 proof used Kakutani's fixed-point theorem and the 1951 proof Brouwer's. Because the proof is non-constructive, computing an equilibrium is PPAD-complete. He also introduced the Nash bargaining solution (1950).

RS

Reinhard Selten

1930–2016German · University of Bonn

Subgame-perfect equilibrium · 1965

Introduced subgame-perfect equilibrium (1965) and trembling-hand perfect equilibrium (1975).

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 1994 (with John Nash and John Harsanyi)

extensive-form gamesbackward inductiontrembling hand
Course chaptersCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 3 · Nash equilibrium

Why it matters: Refinements remove Nash equilibria that rely on non-credible threats.

A subgame-perfect equilibrium induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame; in finite perfect-information games it is found by backward induction. Selten's chain-store paradox (1978) also pioneered experimental economics.

RA

Robert Aumann

b. 1930Israeli-American · Hebrew University of Jerusalem

Correlated equilibrium · repeated games

Introduced correlated equilibrium (1974), formalised common knowledge ("Agreeing to Disagree", 1976) and developed the theory of repeated games.

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2005 (with Thomas Schelling)

correlated equilibriumcommon knowledgefolk theorem
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basicsCh. 3 · Nash equilibrium

Why it matters: Correlated equilibria form a polytope computable by linear programming, and no-regret learning converges to them.

In a correlated equilibrium, a mediator draws an action profile and privately recommends each action; no player gains by disobeying. The folk theorem explains cooperation in infinitely repeated games.

KT

Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky

1934–2024 · 1937–1996Israeli-American · Princeton (Kahneman); Stanford (Tversky)

Prospect theory · 1979

Documented systematic biases in judgement (heuristics and biases, 1974) and proposed prospect theory (Econometrica, 1979).

Prizes: Kahneman: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2002 (with Vernon Smith); Tversky died in 1996, before the award

loss aversionframingprobability weighting
Course chaptersCh. 13 · Decision making

Why it matters: Prospect theory replaces expected utility with reference dependence, loss aversion and probability weighting, the basis of behavioural game theory.

Framing effects violate the invariance that rational-choice models assume. Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) popularised the dual-process view.

RW

Robert Wilson

b. 1937American · Stanford University

Common-value auctions · auction formats

Pioneered the analysis of common-value auctions and the winner's curse (1969, 1977) and co-designed the simultaneous multiple-round auction.

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2020 (with Paul Milgrom)

common valueswinner's cursesequential equilibrium
Course chaptersCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 5 · Auctions

Why it matters: With Kreps he defined sequential equilibrium (1982); with Milgrom he designed spectrum auctions used by the FCC since 1994.

Wilson was Milgrom's doctoral adviser; he also advised Roth and Rosenthal. His Nonlinear Pricing (1993) shaped electricity market design.

DB

Dietrich Braess

b. 1938German · Ruhr University Bochum

Braess's paradox · 1968

Showed in 1968 that adding a road to a network can increase every driver's equilibrium travel time.

network paradoxWardrop equilibriumselfish routing
Course chaptersCh. 8 · Network routingCh. 11 · Price of anarchy

Why it matters: Braess's paradox is the standard example that selfish routing can be harmed by extra capacity; its price of anarchy with linear latencies is 4/3.

The paper ("Über ein Paradoxon aus der Verkehrsplanung", Unternehmensforschung, 1968) was translated into English in 2005. Braess's main field is numerical analysis (finite elements).

CG

Edward H. Clarke & Theodore Groves

Clarke 1939–2013American · Clarke: US federal government economist; Groves: UC San Diego

Clarke pivot rule 1971 · Groves mechanisms 1973

Generalised Vickrey's idea into the VCG mechanism: Clarke (Public Choice, 1971) introduced the pivot payment, Groves (Econometrica, 1973) the general class.

VCGexternality pricingpublic goods
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism design

Why it matters: VCG (Vickrey 1961, Clarke 1971, Groves 1973) chooses the welfare-maximising outcome and charges each agent the externality it imposes, making truth-telling a dominant strategy.

Green and Laffont (1977) showed that, with unrestricted quasi-linear preferences, Groves mechanisms are the only efficient dominant-strategy mechanisms. VCG may run a deficit and is vulnerable to collusion.

AG

Allan Gibbard

b. 1942American · University of Michigan

Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem · 1973

Proved (Econometrica, 1973) that every non-dictatorial voting rule with at least three possible outcomes can be manipulated.

strategy-proofnessmanipulationdictatorship
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism designCh. 6 · Voting

Why it matters: Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved the theorem independently; it makes strategy-proofness a central constraint in mechanism design.

Escape routes include restricted domains (single-peaked preferences and the median rule), money (VCG), randomisation and computational hardness of manipulation.

RA

Robert Axelrod

b. 1943American · University of Michigan

Iterated prisoner's dilemma tournaments · 1980

Ran computer tournaments of the iterated prisoner's dilemma (1980), won by Anatol Rapoport's Tit for Tat.

Prizes: MacArthur Fellowship 1987

iterated prisoner's dilemmatit for tatreciprocity
Course chaptersCh. 7 · Multi-agent systemsCh. 12 · Learning and evolution

Why it matters: The Evolution of Cooperation (with W. D. Hamilton, Science 1981; book 1984) explained how reciprocity sustains cooperation.

Successful strategies were nice, retaliatory, forgiving and clear. The results inspired agent-based modelling and evolutionary dynamics.

MS

Mark Satterthwaite

b. 1945American · Kellogg School, Northwestern University

Gibbard–Satterthwaite 1975 · Myerson–Satterthwaite 1983

Proved the manipulability theorem independently of Gibbard (Journal of Economic Theory, 1975).

strategy-proofnessbilateral tradeimpossibility
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism designCh. 5 · AuctionsCh. 6 · Voting

Why it matters: The Myerson–Satterthwaite theorem (1983) shows that no bilateral trade mechanism is efficient, incentive compatible, individually rational and budget balanced.

The two impossibility results explain why real markets and elections must give up some desirable property.

RR

Robert W. Rosenthal

1945–2002American · Boston University

Congestion games · potential · 1973

Introduced congestion games (1973) and proved they always have a pure Nash equilibrium via a potential function.

congestion gamespotential functionpure equilibria
Course chaptersCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 8 · Network routingCh. 11 · Price of anarchy

Why it matters: Rosenthal's potential guarantees that best-response dynamics terminate; it is the main tool for routing games and price-of-stability bounds.

Monderer and Shapley (1996) showed that every exact potential game is isomorphic to a congestion game. Rosenthal also introduced the centipede game (1981).

HV

Hal Varian

b. 1947American · UC Berkeley, Google

Position auctions · 2007

Analysed the ad auctions used in sponsored search ("Position Auctions", 2007) as chief economist at Google.

sponsored searchGSP auctionclick-through rates
Course chaptersCh. 9 · Online platforms

Why it matters: His symmetric Nash equilibria of the generalised second-price auction connect ad prices to click-through rates and bidder values.

Varian joined Google in 2002 and helped design its ad auction and reserve-price policies. His textbook Intermediate Microeconomics is widely used.

PM

Paul Milgrom

b. 1948American · Stanford University

Auction theory · market design

Developed the theory of affiliated values and the linkage principle (with Weber, 1982) and designed spectrum auctions.

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2020 (with Robert Wilson)

linkage principleascending auctionsspectrum auctions
Course chaptersCh. 5 · Auctions

Why it matters: Co-designed the FCC simultaneous multiple-round auction (1994) and the 2016–17 incentive auction.

The linkage principle explains why open ascending auctions raise more revenue when values are affiliated. Book: Putting Auction Theory to Work (2004).

CP

Christos Papadimitriou

b. 1949Greek · UC Berkeley, Columbia University

PPAD 1994 · price of anarchy 1999

Defined the complexity class PPAD (1994) and, with Koutsoupias, introduced worst-case equilibria (1999), later named the price of anarchy.

Prizes: Knuth Prize 2002; Gödel Prize 2012 (with Koutsoupias, for the price of anarchy); IEEE John von Neumann Medal 2016

PPADprice of anarchytotal search problems
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basicsCh. 3 · Nash equilibriumCh. 8 · Network routingCh. 11 · Price of anarchy

Why it matters: With Daskalakis and Goldberg he proved that computing a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete (2006).

PPAD captures total search problems whose solutions are guaranteed by a parity argument on directed graphs (end-of-the-line). His 2001 STOC talk "Algorithms, Games, and the Internet" helped launch AGT.

SH

Sergiu Hart

b. 1949Israeli · Hebrew University of Jerusalem

Regret matching · 2000

With Andreu Mas-Colell, introduced regret matching (Econometrica, 2000), a simple adaptive procedure whose empirical play converges to the set of correlated equilibria.

regret matchinginternal regretcorrelated equilibrium
Course chaptersCh. 12 · Learning and evolution

Why it matters: No internal-regret learning leads to correlated equilibria; no external-regret learning leads to coarse correlated equilibria.

Hart and Mas-Colell also showed that "uncoupled" dynamics cannot guarantee convergence to Nash equilibrium in all games (2003).

EM

Eric Maskin

b. 1950American · Harvard University

Implementation theory · Maskin monotonicity · 1977

Characterised which social choice rules can be implemented in Nash equilibrium (1977, published 1999).

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2007 (with Leonid Hurwicz and Roger Myerson)

Nash implementationmonotonicitysocial choice rules
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism design

Why it matters: Maskin monotonicity is necessary for Nash implementation and, with no veto power and three or more agents, sufficient.

Implementation theory asks whether every equilibrium of a designed game yields the desired outcome, not just whether some equilibrium does.

AR

Alvin Roth

b. 1951American · Harvard University, Stanford University

Market design · kidney exchange

Turned matching theory into practice: the redesigned US medical residency match (Roth–Peranson, 1999), school choice in Boston and New York, and kidney exchange.

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2012 (with Lloyd Shapley)

deferred acceptancekidney exchangeschool choice
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism design

Why it matters: Market design treats markets as engineered systems that must be thick, uncongested and safe for truthful participation.

Roth showed that no stable matching mechanism is strategy-proof for both sides (1982). Book: Who Gets What — and Why (2015).

RM

Roger Myerson

b. 1951American · Northwestern University, University of Chicago

Revelation principle 1979 · optimal auctions 1981

Formulated the revelation principle (1979) and characterised revenue-optimal auctions ("Optimal Auction Design", 1981).

Prizes: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2007 (with Leonid Hurwicz and Eric Maskin)

revelation principlevirtual valuesMyerson's lemma
Course chaptersCh. 4 · Mechanism designCh. 5 · Auctions

Why it matters: Virtual values reduce revenue maximisation to welfare maximisation; with regular i.i.d. values the optimal auction is second price with a reserve.

Myerson's lemma: a single-parameter allocation rule is implementable iff it is monotone, and the payment is then uniquely determined. With Satterthwaite he proved the bilateral trade impossibility (1983).

TR

Jean Tirole & Jean-Charles Rochet

Tirole b. 1953French · Toulouse School of Economics

Two-sided markets · 2003

Founded the economics of two-sided platforms ("Platform Competition in Two-Sided Markets", 2003).

Prizes: Tirole: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2014 (sole laureate)

two-sided marketsnetwork effectsplatform pricing
Course chaptersCh. 9 · Online platforms

Why it matters: Platforms set prices for both sides and may subsidise one side to attract the other, explaining pricing of app stores, card networks and marketplaces.

Tirole's Nobel citation is "for his analysis of market power and regulation". With Drew Fudenberg he wrote the textbook Game Theory (1991).

YS

Yoav Shoham

Israeli-American · Stanford University

Multiagent systems

Connected AI and game theory; co-wrote Multiagent Systems: Algorithmic, Game-Theoretic, and Logical Foundations (2009) with Kevin Leyton-Brown.

multi-agent systemsagent designcomputational equilibria
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basicsCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 4 · Mechanism designCh. 7 · Multi-agent systems

ÉT

Éva Tardos

b. 1957Hungarian-American · Cornell University

Selfish routing · 2000–02

With Roughgarden, bounded the price of anarchy of selfish routing ("How Bad Is Selfish Routing?", FOCS 2000; JACM 2002).

Prizes: Fulkerson Prize 1988; Gödel Prize 2012; IEEE John von Neumann Medal 2019

network flowsprice of anarchylearning in games
Course chaptersCh. 1 · Game theory basicsCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 8 · Network routingCh. 11 · Price of anarchy

She co-edited Algorithmic Game Theory (2007) and wrote Algorithm Design (2005) with Kleinberg. Recent work studies no-regret learners in auctions and routing.

VV

Vijay V. Vazirani

b. 1957Indian-American · Georgia Tech, UC Irvine

AdWords · online matching · market equilibria

Co-authored the AdWords algorithm (Mehta, Saberi, U. Vazirani, V. Vazirani, 2005) achieving a 1−1/e competitive ratio for online budgeted allocation.

online matchingAdWordsmarket equilibrium
Course chaptersCh. 2 · Introduction to AGTCh. 9 · Online platforms

Why it matters: Online bipartite matching (Karp–Vazirani–Vazirani, 1990) and its AdWords extension model how ad platforms assign queries to advertisers.

Co-editor of the textbook Algorithmic Game Theory (2007) with Nisan, Roughgarden and Tardos; he also designed combinatorial algorithms for Fisher market equilibria.

CD

Cynthia Dwork

b. 1958American · Harvard University

Differential privacy 2006 · fairness through awareness 2012

Co-invented differential privacy (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith, 2006) and defined individual fairness ("Fairness Through Awareness", 2012).

Prizes: Gödel Prize 2017; Knuth Prize 2020

differential privacyindividual fairnessalgorithmic ethics
Course chaptersCh. 6 · VotingCh. 10 · Fairness and ethics

Why it matters: Individual fairness asks that similar individuals be treated similarly; differential privacy also yields approximately truthful mechanisms (McSherry–Talwar, 2007).

Her work frames privacy and fairness as mathematical guarantees that can be traded off against accuracy.

NN

Noam Nisan

b. 1961Israeli · Hebrew University of Jerusalem

Algorithmic mechanism design · 1999

With Amir Ronen, founded algorithmic mechanism design ("Algorithmic Mechanism Design", STOC 1999; GEB 2001).

Prizes: Gödel Prize 2012 (with Amir Ronen); Knuth Prize 2016

truthful approximationcombinatorial auctionscommunication complexity

Why it matters: Mechanisms must be both incentive compatible and computationally efficient; VCG with an approximate algorithm is generally not truthful.

Co-editor of Algorithmic Game Theory (2007). His work on combinatorial auctions measures how much communication is needed for efficient allocation.

MK

Michael Kearns

American · University of Pennsylvania

Graphical games · ethical algorithms

Introduced graphical games (with Littman and Singh, 2001) and studied fairness and privacy in machine learning.

graphical gameslearning theoryalgorithmic fairness
Course chaptersCh. 10 · Fairness and ethicsCh. 14 · Future directions

Why it matters: Graphical games represent large games compactly when each player's payoff depends only on its neighbours.

With Aaron Roth he wrote The Ethical Algorithm (2019) on building fairness and privacy into algorithms.

EK

Elias Koutsoupias

Greek · University of Oxford

Worst-case equilibria · 1999

With Papadimitriou, introduced the ratio between the worst equilibrium and the optimum ("Worst-case Equilibria", STACS 1999), later named the price of anarchy.

Prizes: Gödel Prize 2012 (with Christos Papadimitriou)

price of anarchyload balancingworst-case analysis
Course chaptersCh. 11 · Price of anarchy

Why it matters: The price of anarchy gives a worst-case guarantee on how much selfish behaviour degrades system performance.

The original model was load balancing of selfish jobs on parallel links. He also worked on online algorithms (the k-server conjecture for the work function algorithm).

PG

Paul W. Goldberg
