∀∃

Sets, functions & logic

Cartesian products · profiles · quantifiers

TR strategy set: strateji kümesi; strategy profile: strateji profili; unilateral deviation: tek taraflı sapma

Every game is a tuple of sets and functions: players N, strategy sets Sᵢ, payoffs uᵢ : S → ℝ. Equilibrium definitions are statements with “for every” and “there exists”.

Needed in Ch 1 Ch 2 Ch 3 Ch 4 Ch 6

  • The set of strategy profiles is the Cartesian product S = S₁ × S₂ × ··· × Sₙ; its size is |S₁|·|S₂|·…·|Sₙ|.
  • s₋ᵢ denotes the strategies of everyone except player i, so a profile can be written (sᵢ, s₋ᵢ).
  • Nash equilibrium as a quantified statement: for every player i and every sᵢ′ ∈ Sᵢ, uᵢ(sᵢ*, s₋ᵢ*) ≥ uᵢ(sᵢ′, s₋ᵢ*).
  • Negating it flips the quantifiers: there exist a player i and a strategy sᵢ′ with uᵢ(sᵢ′, s₋ᵢ*) > uᵢ(sᵢ*, s₋ᵢ*) (a profitable deviation).
  • Relations: preferences are binary relations (≻, ≿); transitivity and completeness are the properties voting theory relies on.

Self-check

Three players have 2, 3 and 4 pure strategies. How many pure strategy profiles are there?

2 · 3 · 4 = 24.

Write the negation of “for every player, no deviation is profitable”.

“There exists a player who has a profitable deviation.” The profile is then not a Nash equilibrium.

∎

Proof techniques

Direct · contradiction · induction · counterexample

TR backward induction: geriye doğru çıkarım; strategy-stealing argument: strateji çalma argümanı

Most course results are claims about every deviation, every mechanism or every preference profile, so you must be able to read and write short proofs.

Needed in Ch 1 Ch 2 Ch 3 Ch 4 Ch 6 Ch 11

  • Direct proof: check that no unilateral deviation improves any player's payoff.
  • Induction: backward induction and Zermelo's theorem work from the leaves of a finite game tree up to the root; Nim strategies are proved by induction on the position.
  • Contradiction: the strategy-stealing argument shows the first player in Hex cannot lose.
  • Counterexample: one small game is enough to refute a general claim (for example, a voting profile that breaks monotonicity).
  • Impossibility theorems (Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, Myerson–Satterthwaite) say that no rule satisfies a list of properties at once.

Self-check

Refute: “every finite game has a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium”.

Matching Pennies: in each of the four pure profiles one player wants to switch. (A mixed equilibrium always exists, by Nash's theorem.)

Which technique does backward induction rely on?

Induction on the depth of the game tree: solve the subgames of depth 1, then depth 2, and so on up to the root.

n!

Combinatorics & permutations

Product rule · n! · subsets · partitions

TR Shapley value: Shapley değeri; marginal contribution: marjinal katkı; Bell number: Bell sayısı

Strategy spaces, rankings, orderings of players and coalition structures grow very fast; counting tells you when brute force is hopeless.

Needed in Ch 1 Ch 6 Ch 7

  • n objects have n! orderings (permutations); a set of n elements has 2ⁿ subsets; C(n, k) = n! / (k!(n − k)!).
  • Shapley value: average marginal contribution of player i over all n! arrival orders. Equivalent form: φᵢ = Σ over S ⊆ N∖{i} of |S|!(n − |S| − 1)!/n! · [v(S ∪ {i}) − v(S)], i.e. over 2ⁿ⁻¹ coalitions.
  • With m candidates there are m! strict rankings, which is why Kemeny's rule searches a huge space.
  • The number of coalition structures (set partitions) of n players is the Bell number Bₙ: B₃ = 5, B₄ = 15, B₁₀ = 115 975.

Self-check

How many arrival orders must be averaged to compute the Shapley value of 4 players exactly?

4! = 24.

For n = 3, what weight does the formula give to a coalition S with |S| = 1?

1! · 1! / 3! = 1/6.

ln

Sums, logarithms & exponentials

Geometric series · Hₙ · ln · ex

TR discount factor: iskonto faktörü; harmonic number H_n: harmonik sayı H_n; learning rate: öğrenme oranı

Discounted repeated games, the Price of Stability, multiplicative weights and differential privacy are all written with geometric sums, logarithms and exponentials.

Needed in Ch 2 Ch 11 Ch 12 Ch 14

  • Geometric series: for 0 ≤ δ < 1, Σt≥0 δᵗ = 1/(1 − δ), so a payoff c in every round is worth c/(1 − δ).
  • Harmonic number Hₙ = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n ≈ ln n + 0.577; it bounds the Price of Stability of fair cost-sharing games (PoS ≤ Hₙ).
  • Log rules: ln(ab) = ln a + ln b, ln(aᵏ) = k ln a; logarithmic growth is very slow (ln 10⁶ ≈ 13.8).
  • Exponentials: ex ≥ 1 + x for all x and eε ≈ 1 + ε for small ε (differential privacy guarantees are eε factors).
  • Multiplicative weights update wᵢ ← wᵢ · eη·gᵢ: adding gains in the exponent is multiplying weights.

Self-check

Grim trigger in the Prisoner's Dilemma with R = 3, T = 5, P = 1: for which δ is cooperation sustainable?

3/(1 − δ) ≥ 5 + δ·1/(1 − δ) ⇔ 3 ≥ 5 − 4δ ⇔ δ ≥ 1/2, i.e. δ ≥ (T − R)/(T − P).

Compute H₄.

1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 = 25/12 ≈ 2.08.

f′

Calculus & first-order conditions

Derivatives · maxima · integrals

TR maximize: en büyüklemek; minimize: en aza indirmek

Continuous strategies (quantities, bids, prices, flows) are optimized by setting a derivative to zero and checking that the point is really a maximum.

Needed in Ch 3 Ch 5 Ch 8 Ch 9 Ch 13

  • First-order condition: at an interior maximum of a differentiable f, f′(x) = 0. Check the second derivative (f″ < 0) or the endpoints; f′ = 0 alone can also be a minimum.
  • Product rule (fg)′ = f′g + fg′ and the power rule (xᵏ)′ = k xᵏ⁻¹ are enough for the course derivations.
  • Cournot: each firm maximizes its profit qᵢ·P(q₁ + q₂) − cost; differentiating gives the best-response function.
  • Integral as accumulated cost: the Beckmann potential Φ(f) = Σₑ ∫₀fₑ ℓₑ(t) dt; its derivative in fₑ is the latency ℓₑ(fₑ).

Example (Ch 5). With n bidders and rivals bidding β·vⱼ, vⱼ ~ U[0, 1], a bid b wins with probability (b/β)ⁿ⁻¹. Maximizing (v − b)(b/β)ⁿ⁻¹: the derivative is zero when (n − 1)(v − b) = b, giving b = (n − 1)v/n.

Self-check

Maximize f(q) = q(10 − q) over q ≥ 0.

f′(q) = 10 − 2q = 0 ⇒ q = 5, and f″ = −2 < 0, so f(5) = 25 is the maximum.

What is ∫₀ˣ t dt, and what is its derivative?

x²/2; its derivative is x, which is the latency ℓ(x) = x of the Pigou edge.

∪

Convex & concave functions

Convex sets · Jensen · risk attitudes

TR convex: dışbükey; concave: içbükey; Jensen's inequality: Jensen eşitsizliği

Convexity makes optimization well behaved (local = global) and explains risk aversion through Jensen's inequality.

Needed in Ch 3 Ch 8 Ch 13

  • f is convex if f(λx + (1 − λ)y) ≤ λf(x) + (1 − λ)f(y) for all λ ∈ [0, 1]; for twice-differentiable f this means f″ ≥ 0. f is concave if −f is convex.
  • A local minimum of a convex function is a global minimum, which is why the convex Beckmann potential gives a well-defined Wardrop equilibrium.
  • A set is convex if it contains the segment between any two of its points; the set of mixed strategies (a simplex) is convex, which Kakutani's theorem needs.
  • Jensen's inequality: f convex ⇒ f(E[X]) ≤ E[f(X)]; u concave ⇒ E[u(X)] ≤ u(E[X]). A concave utility therefore means risk aversion.

Self-check

u(x) = √x. A lottery pays 0 or 900 with probability 1/2 each. Find E[u] and the certainty equivalent.

E[u] = (0 + 30)/2 = 15, so the certainty equivalent is 15² = 225 < 450 = E[X]; the risk premium is 225.

Is f(x) = x² convex, concave, or neither?

Convex, since f″(x) = 2 ≥ 0 (a risk-loving utility, as in Ch 13).

A

Vectors & payoff matrices

Matrix indexing · probability vectors · xᵀAy

TR payoff matrix: kazanç matrisi; mixed strategy: karışık strateji; support (of a mixed strategy): destek

Two-player finite games are pairs of matrices, mixed strategies are probability vectors, and expected payoffs are matrix–vector products.

Needed in Ch 1 Ch 2 Ch 3 Ch 12

  • Bimatrix game (A, B): Aᵢⱼ is the row player's payoff and Bᵢⱼ the column player's when row i meets column j; zero-sum means B = −A.
  • A mixed strategy is a vector in the simplex Δ = { x : xᵢ ≥ 0, Σxᵢ = 1 }; its support is the set of actions with xᵢ > 0.
  • Expected payoffs: (Ay)ᵢ is the row player's payoff of pure action i against y, and xᵀAy is the payoff of x against y.
  • Support enumeration solves small linear systems: equal payoffs on the support, probabilities summing to 1.

Self-check

A = [[3, 0], [5, 1]] (rows C, D). Compute xᵀAy for x = (1/2, 1/2), y = (1, 0).

Ay = (3, 5), so xᵀAy = (3 + 5)/2 = 4.

In the same matrix, does row D dominate row C?

Yes, strictly: 5 > 3 and 1 > 0 in both columns.

Fixed points (Brouwer & Kakutani)

x = f(x) · existence · correspondences

TR fixed-point theorem (Brouwer, Kakutani): sabit nokta teoremi; Nash's existence theorem: Nash'in varlık teoremi; best-response correspondence: en iyi tepki eşlemesi

Nash's existence theorem is a fixed-point theorem: an equilibrium is a profile that is a best response to itself.

Needed in Ch 3 Ch 9 Ch 12

  • A fixed point of f is a point with f(x) = x; for a set-valued map F it is a point with x ∈ F(x).
  • Brouwer: every continuous f from a nonempty, compact (closed and bounded), convex set K ⊂ ℝⁿ into itself has a fixed point.
  • Kakutani: the same holds for a correspondence F whose values F(x) are nonempty and convex and whose graph is closed. Nash applies it to the best-response correspondence on mixed profiles: σ* ∈ BR(σ*).
  • These theorems prove existence but give no efficient algorithm; that gap is what PPAD captures.
  • Dynamics such as the adoption model in Ch 9 or learning rules in Ch 12 stop exactly at fixed points of their update map.

Self-check

Find the fixed point of f(x) = 1 − x on [0, 1].

x = 1 − x ⇒ x = 1/2.

Why does f(x) = x/2 on the open interval (0, 1) have no fixed point, despite Brouwer?

The only solution of x = x/2 is 0, which is not in (0, 1); the set is not closed, so Brouwer does not apply.

Probability & statistics

P

Probability & distributions

Events · independence · CDF · uniform

TR uniform distribution: düzgün dağılım; common prior: ortak önsel; independent private values (IPV): bağımsız özel değerler

Mixed strategies, private types, bidder values and model errors are random variables; you need to read distributions and compute simple probabilities.

Needed in Ch 3 Ch 4 Ch 5 Ch 9 Ch 13

  • Discrete distribution: probabilities pᵢ ≥ 0 with Σpᵢ = 1. Continuous: density f ≥ 0 and CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x).
  • Uniform U[0, 1]: F(x) = x and f(x) = 1 on [0, 1], E[X] = 1/2, Var(X) = 1/12.
  • Independence: P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B). Independent private values (IPV) means each bidder's value is drawn independently.
  • Key auction step: for k independent U[0, 1] values, P(all ≤ b) = bᵏ, which is the probability of outbidding k rivals who bid their values.

Self-check

X ~ U[0, 1]. What is P(X ≤ 0.3)?

F(0.3) = 0.3.

Two independent U[0, 1] values: probability that both are at most 0.5?

0.5 · 0.5 = 0.25.

E

Expected value & variance

Linearity · E[f(X)] · spread

TR expected value: beklenen değer; expected payoff: beklenen kazanç; lottery: piyango

Mixed-strategy payoffs, expected revenue, bandit rewards and decision trees are all expected values.

Needed in Ch 1 Ch 3 Ch 5 Ch 12 Ch 13

  • E[X] = Σ x·P(X = x) (discrete) or ∫ x f(x) dx (continuous).
  • Linearity: E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y], even when X and Y are dependent.
  • In general E[f(X)] ≠ f(E[X]) unless f is linear (see Jensen in the convexity card).
  • Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])² measures spread; two lotteries with the same mean can carry very different risk.
  • The expected payoff is not the most likely payoff: a lottery paying 100 with probability 0.3 never pays 30.

Self-check

A lottery pays 100 with probability 0.3 and 0 otherwise. Find E[X] and Var(X).

E[X] = 30; E[X²] = 3000, so Var(X) = 3000 − 900 = 2100.

Does E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] require independence?

No. Linearity of expectation always holds; independence is needed for E[XY] = E[X]E[Y].

P|

Conditional probability & Bayes' rule

Prior · likelihood · posterior · base rates

TR Bayes' rule: Bayes kuralı; prior: önsel; posterior: sonsal; base rate: taban oranı

Bayesian games, reputation systems, fairness metrics and decision analysis all update beliefs with conditional probabilities.

Needed in Ch 4 Ch 9 Ch 10 Ch 13

  • P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) for P(B) > 0.
  • Bayes' rule: P(H | E) = P(E | H)·P(H) / P(E), with the law of total probability P(E) = P(E | H)P(H) + P(E | ¬H)P(¬H).
  • Fairness rates are conditional probabilities: TPR = P(Ŷ = 1 | Y = 1), FPR = P(Ŷ = 1 | Y = 0), PPV = P(Y = 1 | Ŷ = 1).
  • Base-rate neglect: ignoring P(H) makes a positive result from an accurate test look far more conclusive than it is.
  • Sequential updating: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior.

Self-check

Prevalence 1 %, sensitivity 90 %, false positive rate 5 %. What is P(sick | positive)?

0.9·0.01 / (0.9·0.01 + 0.05·0.99) = 0.009 / 0.0585 ≈ 0.15.

A second, independent test is also positive. Update again.

Use 0.154 as the prior: 0.9·0.154 / (0.9·0.154 + 0.05·0.846) ≈ 0.77.

v₍₁₎

Order statistics for auctions

Highest · second-highest · revenue

TR revenue equivalence theorem: gelir eşdeğerliği teoremi; winner's curse: kazananın laneti; virtual value: sanal değer

Auction revenue is the expected value of the highest or second-highest bid, so the expected k-th largest of n random values appears throughout Chapter 5.

Needed in Ch 5 Ch 14

  • For n independent U[0, 1] values, the expected k-th highest is (n + 1 − k)/(n + 1): highest n/(n + 1), second highest (n − 1)/(n + 1).
  • Second-price revenue = E[second-highest value] = (n − 1)/(n + 1).
  • First-price with b = (n − 1)v/n: revenue = (n − 1)/n · n/(n + 1) = (n − 1)/(n + 1), the same, as revenue equivalence predicts.
  • Winner's curse: if estimation errors are U[−a, a], the expected largest of n errors is a(n − 1)/(n + 1), so the winner overestimates most.
  • Virtual value φ(v) = v − (1 − F(v))/f(v); for U[0, 1], φ(v) = 2v − 1, giving the optimal reserve price 1/2.

Self-check

Two bidders with U[0, 1] values: expected revenue of a second-price auction?

(2 − 1)/(2 + 1) = 1/3 (the expected minimum of two uniforms).

Expected winning value with 4 bidders?

4/5 = 0.8.

x̄

Sampling, rates & simulation

Sample means · error · confusion matrix

TR confusion matrix: karışıklık matrisi; true positive rate (TPR): doğru pozitif oranı; sample complexity: örneklem karmaşıklığı

Simulations, bandit estimates, fairness audits and learned mechanisms all estimate quantities from noisy samples.

Needed in Ch 5 Ch 10 Ch 12 Ch 14

  • Law of large numbers: the sample mean of N independent draws approaches the true mean; its standard error is σ/√N.
  • Confusion-matrix rates: TPR = TP/(TP + FN), FPR = FP/(FP + TN), PPV = TP/(TP + FP), selection rate = (TP + FP)/total.
  • Empirical frequencies of play in a simulation estimate mixed strategies; small samples fluctuate a lot.
  • Sample complexity asks how many samples are needed for ε-accurate estimates; too few samples lead to overfitting.

Self-check

How many times more samples do you need to halve the standard error?

Four times as many, because the error scales as 1/√N.

TP = 40, FN = 10, FP = 10, TN = 40. Compute TPR and FPR.

TPR = 40/50 = 0.8; FPR = 10/50 = 0.2.

γ

Markov chains & MDPs (intuition)

States · transitions · rewards · discounting

TR policy (agent / RL): politika; state–action value: durum-eylem değeri; discount factor: iskonto faktörü

Q-learning and repeated games evaluate long streams of rewards; the Markov decision process is the model behind them.

Needed in Ch 2 Ch 12

  • Markov property: the next state depends only on the current state (and action), not on the full history.
  • An MDP is (states S, actions A, transition probabilities P(s′ | s, a), rewards r, discount γ ∈ [0, 1)); the goal is to maximize E[Σ γᵗ rₜ].
  • A policy maps states to actions; the greedy policy picks argmaxₐ Q(s, a).
  • Q-learning update: Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α[r + γ·maxₐ′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a)], with learning rate α.

Self-check

Q(s, a) = 2, r = 1, γ = 0.9, max Q(s′, ·) = 4, α = 0.5. New Q(s, a)?

2 + 0.5·(1 + 3.6 − 2) = 3.3.

Reward 1 at every step forever, γ = 0.9. Discounted return?

1/(1 − 0.9) = 10.

Optimization

arg

Optimization basics & argmax

Objective · constraints · max vs argmax

TR optimization: optimizasyon; social welfare: sosyal refah; social optimum: sosyal optimum

Best responses, VCG allocations and social optima are all “choose the feasible option with the largest objective”.

Needed in Ch 3 Ch 4 Ch 5 Ch 7 Ch 11

  • An optimization problem has decision variables, an objective to maximize or minimize, and constraints defining the feasible set.
  • max f is a value; argmax f is the set of maximizers. Ties mean argmax can contain several points, so mechanisms need a tie-breaking rule.
  • Best response: BRᵢ(s₋ᵢ) = argmax over sᵢ of uᵢ(sᵢ, s₋ᵢ). VCG chooses x* = argmaxₓ Σᵢ bᵢ(x).
  • OPT denotes the optimal objective value; the Price of Anarchy compares an equilibrium's cost with OPT.

Self-check

f(x) = −(x − 2)² on [0, 5]: give max f and argmax f.

max f = 0, argmax f = {2}.

Payoffs a: 3, b: 5, c: 5. What is the argmax?

{b, c}; a tie-breaking rule must pick one.

LP

Linear programming & duality

Feasible polytope · dual · minimax

TR linear programming (LP): doğrusal programlama; minimax theorem (von Neumann): minimaks teoremi; value of the game: oyunun değeri; saddle point: eyer noktası

Zero-sum games, correlated equilibria and some mechanism-design problems are solved as linear programs, and LP duality is the reason max-min equals min-max.

Needed in Ch 3 Ch 12 Ch 14

  • An LP maximizes or minimizes a linear objective subject to linear inequalities; the feasible set is a convex polyhedron and an optimum (if one exists) is attained at a vertex.
  • LPs are solvable in polynomial time (ellipsoid, interior-point methods); the simplex method is fast in practice but exponential in the worst case.
  • Duality: each max-LP has a dual min-LP. Weak duality: primal value ≤ dual value; strong duality: they are equal at the optimum.
  • Row player's LP in a zero-sum game: maximize v subject to Σᵢ xᵢAᵢⱼ ≥ v for every column j, Σxᵢ = 1, x ≥ 0. The column player's LP is its dual, so max min = min max (von Neumann's minimax theorem).
  • Correlated and coarse correlated equilibria are also LP feasibility problems, hence computable in polynomial time.

Self-check

A = [[3, 1], [4, 2]] (row maximizes). Find maximin, minimax and the saddle point.

Row minima 1, 2 ⇒ maximin 2. Column maxima 4, 2 ⇒ minimax 2. Saddle point at (row 2, column 2), value 2.

Value and optimal strategy in Matching Pennies (payoffs ±1)?

Value 0; each player mixes (1/2, 1/2).

Algorithms & complexity

Running time, algorithm design, XOR, graphs and the classes P, NP, PPAD and PLS.

O

Big-O & running time

Growth rates · polynomial vs exponential

TR greedy (algorithm): açgözlü; alpha–beta pruning: alfa-beta budaması

Algorithmic game theory asks not only whether an outcome exists but whether it can be computed quickly.

Needed in Ch 1 Ch 4 Ch 7 Ch 8 Ch 11

  • Common growth rates: O(1) < O(log n) < O(n) < O(n log n) < O(n²) < O(n³) < O(2ⁿ) < O(n!). O is an upper bound; Θ is a tight bound.
  • Polynomial time (O(nᵏ)) is the working definition of “efficient”.
  • A game tree with branching factor b and depth d has about bᵈ leaves; alpha–beta pruning with perfect move ordering examines about bd/2.
  • Course examples: Gale–Shapley O(n²), Hungarian algorithm O(n³), Dijkstra O((V + E) log V) with a binary heap.

Self-check

Assigning 10 robots to 10 tasks: brute force vs Hungarian algorithm?

10! = 3 628 800 assignments vs about 10³ = 1000 steps.

Is O(n log n) polynomial?

Yes; it is bounded by O(n²).

Algorithm design & tracing

Recursion · greedy · dynamic programming

TR backward induction: geriye doğru çıkarım; Gale–Shapley algorithm (deferred acceptance): Gale–Shapley (ertelenmiş kabul) algoritması; greedy (algorithm): açgözlü

Many decks contain step-by-step code traces; you should be able to follow an algorithm, state its input and output, and argue that it terminates and is correct.

Needed in Ch 1 Ch 2 Ch 4 Ch 7

  • Recursion: minimax evaluates a node from the values of its children; backward induction is the same idea on a game tree.
  • Dynamic programming stores solved subproblems (e.g., winning/losing positions of a subtraction game).
  • Greedy algorithms take the locally best choice; they are fast but not always optimal (contrast with the Hungarian algorithm).
  • Gale–Shapley (deferred acceptance): proposers propose in preference order, receivers hold the best offer; it ends with a stable matching that is best for the proposing side.

Self-check

Why does Gale–Shapley terminate?

Each proposer proposes to each receiver at most once, so there are at most n² proposals.

A minimax leaf layer under a MAX node holds 3, 7, 2. What value does the node get?

7, the maximum of its children.

⊕

Binary numbers & XOR

Bitwise operations · Nim

TR nim-sum: nim toplamı; impartial game: tarafsız oyun; Sprague–Grundy theorem: Sprague–Grundy teoremi

Nim is solved exactly with the bitwise XOR of heap sizes, and Sprague–Grundy theory extends this to other impartial games.

Needed in Ch 1

  • XOR (⊕) adds binary digits without carry: a bit of the result is 1 when an odd number of inputs have a 1 there.
  • x ⊕ x = 0, x ⊕ 0 = x, and XOR is commutative and associative.
  • Nim (normal play): the player to move loses with perfect play exactly when the nim-sum of the heaps is 0.

Self-check

Compute 3 ⊕ 4 ⊕ 5.

011 ⊕ 100 ⊕ 101 = 010 = 2, so the player to move can win.

Heaps 1, 2, 3: who wins with perfect play?

1 ⊕ 2 ⊕ 3 = 0, so the player to move loses.

NP

P, NP, PPAD & PLS

Reductions · hardness · total search problems

TR NP-hard: NP-zor; PPAD-complete: PPAD-tam; PLS-complete: PLS-tam; ε-approximate equilibrium: ε-yaklaşık denge

Complexity classes explain which game-theoretic objects are easy to compute (zero-sum equilibria, correlated equilibria) and which are believed hard (general Nash equilibria, optimal allocations).

Needed in Ch 3 Ch 4 Ch 6 Ch 8 Ch 14

  • P: solvable in polynomial time. NP: a proposed solution can be verified in polynomial time.
  • NP-hard: every NP problem reduces to it in polynomial time; NP-complete = NP-hard and in NP. Examples in the course: the VCG argmax in combinatorial auctions, Kemeny ranking, manipulating some voting rules.
  • Why Nash is not NP-complete: an equilibrium always exists, so the yes/no question is trivial; the difficulty is finding one.
  • PPAD: search problems whose solution is guaranteed by a parity (end-of-line) argument; it contains Brouwer, Sperner and Nash. Two-player Nash is PPAD-complete (Chen–Deng–Teng, 2006).
  • PLS: local search problems; finding a pure equilibrium of a congestion game is PLS-complete.

Self-check

Can a correlated equilibrium be computed in polynomial time?

Yes: it is a linear program in the probabilities of action profiles.

Why is “does this game have a mixed Nash equilibrium?” not an interesting NP question?

The answer is always yes (Nash's theorem); hardness is about finding the equilibrium, which is what PPAD measures.

G

Graphs, shortest paths & flows

Directed graphs · cycles · flows · load

TR graph: çizge; node: düğüm; edge: kenar; latency (delay) function: gecikme fonksiyonu

Routing games, network formation, matching markets, voting tournaments and kidney exchange are all graph problems.

Needed in Ch 4 Ch 6 Ch 7 Ch 8 Ch 11

  • Graphs: vertices and (directed or undirected) edges, stored as adjacency lists or matrices; paths, cycles, connectivity, bipartite graphs.
  • Shortest paths: Dijkstra needs nonnegative weights; Bellman–Ford allows negative weights (no negative cycles).
  • Flows: a flow of r units from s to t is split over paths; an edge's load is the sum of the flows of paths using it, and flow is conserved at every inner vertex.
  • In routing games edge costs are latency functions ℓₑ(x) of the load, not fixed weights; this is what creates congestion and Braess's paradox.
  • Cycles matter elsewhere too: Top Trading Cycles removes cycles of a pointing graph; a majority graph with a cycle is a Condorcet paradox.

Self-check

Pigou network: one unit of flow, edge costs 1 and ℓ(x) = x. Equilibrium cost, optimal cost, PoA?

Equilibrium: all on the x edge, cost 1. Optimum: split 1/2–1/2, cost 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4. PoA = 4/3.

Does Dijkstra work with negative edge weights?

Not in general; use Bellman–Ford.

EU

Expected utility & mixed strategies

Lotteries · indifference · risk attitude

TR expected utility: beklenen fayda; indifference principle: kayıtsızlık ilkesi; risk-neutral: risk nötr; certainty equivalent: kesinlik eşdeğeri (KE)

Under randomness a player compares lotteries by expected utility Σ p(x)·u(x); this is how mixed equilibria are computed and how risk attitudes are modeled.

Needed in Ch 3 Ch 5 Ch 13

  • Expected utility of a lottery: Σ p(x)·u(x). A risk-neutral player has u(x) = x; risk-averse means concave u.
  • Indifference principle: in a mixed Nash equilibrium every action in a player's support earns the same expected payoff, and actions outside the support earn no more.
  • So each player's mixing probabilities are chosen to make the other player indifferent; your own payoffs determine the opponent's mix.
  • Most auction results assume risk-neutral bidders (Ch 5); Ch 13 relaxes this.

Self-check

Column mixes Left with probability q. Row's payoffs: Top = 2q, Bottom = 1 − q. For which q is Row willing to mix?

2q = 1 − q ⇒ q = 1/3.

In a mixed equilibrium, can an action outside the support earn strictly more than the support actions?

No; then the player would shift probability to it, so it would not be a best response.

BR

Best responses & dominance

Best response · dominance · deviation

TR best response: en iyi tepki; dominant strategy: baskın strateji; dominated strategy: baskılanan strateji; Nash equilibrium (NE): Nash dengesi

The core skill of the course: given the others' choices, what is each player's best response, and is anyone tempted to deviate?

Needed in Ch 1 Ch 2 Ch 3 Ch 8

  • Best response: the action(s) maximizing a player's payoff against fixed actions of the others.
  • Strict dominance: sᵢ is always strictly better than sᵢ′. A strictly dominated strategy is never played in any Nash equilibrium (not even with positive probability).
  • Weak dominance: never worse, sometimes strictly better. Weakly dominated strategies can still appear in equilibria, and eliminating them can depend on the order.
  • A profile is a Nash equilibrium when every player is playing a best response to the others; individual stability does not imply social optimality (Prisoner's Dilemma).

Self-check

Prisoner's Dilemma: is (Cooperate, Cooperate) a Nash equilibrium?

No; each player gains by defecting. (Defect, Defect) is the unique equilibrium, although both prefer (C, C).

Can a strictly dominated strategy be in the support of a mixed equilibrium?

No; shifting its probability to the dominating strategy would strictly raise the payoff.

Economics vocabulary

Valuation · utility · welfare · revenue

TR valuation: değerleme; quasi-linear utility: yarı doğrusal fayda; social welfare: sosyal refah; revenue: gelir; surplus (gains from trade): artık

Mechanism design and auctions reuse a compact set of economic terms; mixing them up (especially welfare and revenue) is a common mistake.

Needed in Ch 4 Ch 5 Ch 9 Ch 11

  • Valuation vᵢ: what an outcome is worth to agent i. Quasi-linear utility: uᵢ = vᵢ(x) − pᵢ, value minus payment.
  • Social welfare: Σᵢ vᵢ(x). Payments are transfers between agents and the seller, so they cancel out of welfare.
  • Revenue: Σᵢ pᵢ, what the seller collects. Welfare = total utility of the agents + revenue.
  • Surplus (gains from trade) in a market is buyer value minus seller cost for the trades that happen.
  • An externality is an effect of one agent's action on others' payoffs that the agent does not pay for (congestion, network effects).

Self-check

A bidder with value 10 wins and pays 7. Utility, revenue, welfare?

Utility 3, revenue 7, welfare 10 (= 3 + 7).

Can a mechanism raise revenue while lowering welfare?

Yes; a high reserve price can leave the item unsold, lowering welfare while raising expected revenue.

R

Regret & online learning

External regret · Hedge · bandits

TR regret: pişmanlık; no-regret: pişmanlıksız; multiplicative weights (Hedge): çarpımsal ağırlıklar (Hedge); multi-armed bandit: çok kollu haydut

Learning in games measures an algorithm by its regret: how much worse it did than the best single action in hindsight.

Needed in Ch 11 Ch 12 Ch 13

  • External regret after T rounds: R_T = maxₐ Σₜ gₜ(a) − Σₜ gₜ(aₜ). An algorithm is no-regret if R_T / T → 0.
  • Multiplicative weights (Hedge) with n actions and gains in [0, 1]: R_T ≤ ln n/η + ηT; with η = √(ln n / T), R_T ≤ 2√(T ln n).
  • Follow-the-leader can be forced to alternate and suffer linear regret; randomization fixes this.
  • When all players use no-regret learning, the time-average play converges to a coarse correlated equilibrium, and smooth-game PoA bounds still apply.
  • Bandits reveal only the chosen action's reward; UCB adds an exploration bonus √(2 ln t / Nₐ).
  • Minimax regret (Savage) in Ch 13 is a different, single-decision criterion with the same word.

Self-check

Hedge with n = 2 actions over T = 100 rounds: bound on total and average regret?

2√(100 · ln 2) ≈ 16.7 in total, i.e. about 0.17 per round.

Why does average regret going to 0 not mean the learner plays optimally each round?

Regret compares with the best fixed action in hindsight, not with the best action in each round.
