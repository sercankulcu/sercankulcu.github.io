Algorithmic Game Theory · People

Important People in Algorithmic Game Theory

Short profiles, gathered on 62 cards, of the mathematicians, economists and computer scientists behind the results used in the course. The list runs from Borda and Condorcet to the Price of Anarchy and PPAD. Each card links to the course chapters where the person's work appears; the details open with the button at the bottom of the card.

JB

Jean-Charles de Borda

1733–1799French · naval officer and mathematician, Académie des sciences

Borda count · 1770

Proposed the Borda count. In this positional voting rule, the options in each ranking receive m−1, m−2, …, 0 points.

positional scoringranked ballotCondorcet criterion
ChaptersChapter 7 · Social Choice and Voting

Significance: The Borda count is the classic scoring rule. It never elects the Condorcet loser, but it can fail to elect the Condorcet winner.

Borda presented his method to the Academy in 1770, and it was published in 1784. Strong rivals can be buried at the bottom of the ranking, so the rule is easy to manipulate. This weakness was later made precise by the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem.

MC

Marquis de Condorcet

1743–1794French · philosopher and mathematician

Condorcet paradox · jury theorem · 1785

In his 1785 Essai he showed that pairwise majority voting can cycle: A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A.

pairwise majorityCondorcet winnerjury theorem
ChaptersChapter 7 · Social Choice and Voting

Significance: The Condorcet winner and the voting paradox are the starting point of social choice theory and of Arrow's impossibility theorem.

According to the jury theorem, if each voter independently decides correctly with probability p > 1/2, the probability that the majority decision is correct approaches 1 as the number of voters grows. Condorcet died in prison during the French Revolution.

CB

Charles L. Bouton

1869–1922American · Harvard University

Complete solution of Nim · 1901–1902

In the paper “Nim, a game with a complete mathematical theory” (Annals of Mathematics, 1901–1902) he solved Nim completely. The player to move loses under best play if the binary XOR sum of the pile sizes (the nim-sum) is 0.

Nimnim-sumP- and N-positions
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 2 · Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning

Significance: Bouton's rule gives, with a single XOR operation, the result that minimax finds by traversing the whole game tree. It is among the first complete results of combinatorial game theory.

The winning move is the one that makes the nim-sum 0. Sprague (1935) and Grundy (1939) extended this idea to all impartial games.

ÉB

Émile Borel

1871–1956French · Université de Paris (Minister of the Navy in 1925)

Mixed strategies · 1921

In notes published between 1921 and 1927 he treated strategic games with mixed (randomized) strategies.

mixed strategyzero-sum gamerandomization
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and Representations

Significance: Borel posed the minimax question for symmetric zero-sum games. Von Neumann proved the general minimax theorem in 1928.

Borel solved small cases but doubted that the minimax property holds in every game. The priority dispute between Borel and von Neumann was revisited in Econometrica in 1953.

EZ

Ernst Zermelo

1871–1953German · Göttingen, Zurich, Freiburg

Zermelo's theorem on chess · 1913

The first formal theorem of game theory is attributed to him (1913). According to the theorem, in chess either White can force a win, or Black can force a win, or both sides can guarantee at least a draw.

perfect informationdeterminacybackward induction
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 2 · Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning

Significance: The result is the foundation of backward induction and of the determinacy of finite two-player games of perfect information.

Zermelo is better known for his axioms of set theory (1908). His chess paper is the ancestor of the game tree search used for Tic-Tac-Toe, Nim and Connect Four in the course labs.

AP

Arthur Cecil Pigou

1877–1959British · University of Cambridge

Pigou's example · externalities · 1912/1920

He described two roads, one that can become congested and one with a fixed latency. Selfish drivers overuse the congestible road (Wealth and Welfare, 1912; The Economics of Welfare, 1920).

externalityPigouvian taxcongestion
ChaptersChapter 8 · Network Design and RoutingChapter 9 · The Price of Anarchy

Significance: Pigou's example is the standard example of selfish routing. With linear latencies the price of anarchy is 4/3. Roughgarden and Tardos proved that this is the worst case.

Pigou proposed taxing activities that create negative externalities. Congestion pricing (a marginal-cost toll) is the network counterpart of this idea and makes the Wardrop equilibrium optimal.

HS

Hugo Steinhaus

1887–1972Polish · universities of Lwów and Wrocław

Fair cake cutting · 1948

He began the mathematical theory of fair division with the paper “The Problem of Fair Division” (Econometrica, 1948).

proportionalitycut-and-choosecake cutting
ChaptersChapter 11 · Algorithmic Fairness and Ethics

Significance: He gave a proportional method for three players. For n players he also announced the Banach–Knaster last diminisher protocol.

Fair division looks for two kinds of allocation: proportional (everyone gets at least 1/n by their own valuation) or envy-free. The algorithmic theory was later developed by Brams, Taylor, Procaccia and others.

OM

Oskar Morgenstern

1902–1977German-born Austrian-American · Princeton University

Theory of Games and Economic Behavior · 1944

With von Neumann he wrote Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944). This book established game theory as a separate field within economics.

expected utilitystrategic formrational choice
ChaptersChapter 1 · Decision TheoryChapter 2 · Games and Representations

Significance: The von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility axioms justify modeling players as decision makers who maximize expected payoff.

The utility axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence) were added in the second edition of 1947. The behavioral work of Allais, Kahneman and Tversky later put these axioms to the test.

JvN

John von Neumann

1903–1957Hungarian-American · Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton

Minimax theorem · 1928

He proved the minimax theorem for two-player zero-sum games (1928). With Morgenstern he wrote Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944).

zero-sum gamemixed strategysaddle point
ChaptersChapter 1 · Decision TheoryChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 2 · Minimax and Alpha-Beta PruningChapter 3 · Nash Equilibrium

Significance: Minimax links strategic optimization to duality in linear programming and to adversarial search.

In every finite two-player zero-sum game, the max-min value with mixed strategies equals the min-max value. The 1944 book introduced cooperative games (stable sets), and the 1947 edition added the expected utility axioms.

WV

William Vickrey

1914–1996Canadian-American · Columbia University

Second-price auction · 1961

He studied auctions in the paper “Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders” (1961). He showed that in a sealed-bid second-price auction, bidding one's own value is a dominant strategy.

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 1996 (with James Mirrlees); he died three days after the prize was announced

second-price auctiondominant strategyrevenue equivalence
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism DesignChapter 5 · AuctionsChapter 6 · Online Platforms

Significance: The Vickrey auction is the first VCG mechanism and the template for truthful mechanism design.

The same paper foreshadowed the revenue equivalence of standard auction formats. Vickrey also advocated congestion pricing for roads and public transport.

HS

Herbert A. Simon

1916–2001American · Carnegie Mellon University

Bounded rationality · satisficing · 1955

He introduced the concept of bounded rationality. Real decision makers have limited information and computational power, so they look not for the best option but for one that is good enough (satisficing).

Awards: Turing Award 1975 (with Allen Newell); Nobel Prize in Economics 1978

bounded rationalitysatisficingheuristics
ChaptersChapter 3 · Nash EquilibriumChapter 12 · Behavioral and Collective Decisions

Significance: Bounded rationality highlights the computational limits of agents. The topic comes up often in algorithmic game theory and in multi-agent artificial intelligence.

The paper “A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice” (1955) argued that full optimization is unrealistic because of search costs. Simon is among the founders of artificial intelligence (Logic Theorist, 1956).

CS

Claude E. Shannon

1916–2001American · Bell Labs, MIT

Programming chess · 1950

In the paper “Programming a Computer for Playing Chess” (Philosophical Magazine, 1950) he outlined a chess-playing program: depth-limited minimax search with an evaluation function at the leaves.

Awards: Kyoto Prize 1985

evaluation functiondepth limitShannon number
ChaptersChapter 2 · Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning

Significance: His rough estimate of the number of possible chess games (about 10120) is the standard figure showing why exhaustive brute-force search is impossible, and it is known as the Shannon number.

In the paper he distinguished full-width search (type A), which examines every move to the same depth, from selective search (type B), which examines only promising moves. He is also the author of “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” (1948), which founded information theory.

GB

George W. Brown

1917–2005American · RAND, UCLA, UC Irvine

Fictitious play · 1951

He proposed fictitious play (1951). In this dynamic, each player best-responds to the observed frequencies of the opponent's past moves.

fictitious playbest responseobserved frequencies
ChaptersChapter 13 · Learning and Adaptation

Significance: Fictitious play is the first learning dynamic for games. In 1951 Julia Robinson proved that it converges in two-player zero-sum games.

Shapley (1964) gave a 3×3 game in which fictitious play cycles. This showed that simple learning need not reach a Nash equilibrium in general games.

LH

Leonid Hurwicz

1917–2008Polish-American (born in Moscow) · University of Minnesota

Mechanism design · incentive compatibility · 1972

He founded the theory of mechanism design and introduced the concept of incentive compatibility (1972).

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2007 (with Eric Maskin and Roger Myerson); at 90, the oldest laureate up to that time

incentive compatibilitydecentralization of informationimplementation
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism Design

Significance: Mechanism design turns game theory around: the rules are chosen so that self-interested behavior produces the desired outcome.

Hurwicz showed that in exchange economies no mechanism that is efficient and individually rational can be incentive compatible in dominant strategies. This result framed the trade-offs the field studies.

BH

David Blackwell & James Hannan

1919–2010 · 1922–2010American · UC Berkeley (Blackwell); Michigan State University (Hannan)

Approachability 1956 · Hannan consistency 1957

They laid the foundations of no-regret learning. Hannan (1957) constructed a strategy whose external regret in repeated games goes to zero over time. Blackwell (1956) proved the approachability theorem for vector payoffs.

Awards: Blackwell: first Black member elected to the US National Academy of Sciences (1965); von Neumann Theory Prize 1979

external regretapproachabilityrepeated game
ChaptersChapter 13 · Learning and Adaptation

Significance: No-regret algorithms such as Hedge and regret matching derive from these results and converge to coarse correlated equilibria.

Blackwell's theorem determines which convex sets a player can force the average vector payoff to approach. Hannan consistency and calibration follow as consequences of this theorem.

JH

John Harsanyi

1920–2000Hungarian-American · UC Berkeley

Bayesian games · 1967–68

He developed the theoretical framework for games with incomplete information (“Games with Incomplete Information Played by Bayesian Players”, 1967–68).

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 1994 (with John Nash and Reinhard Selten)

type spacescommon priorBayes–Nash equilibrium
ChaptersChapter 3 · Nash EquilibriumChapter 4 · Mechanism Design

Significance: Bayesian games model private types (values, costs) drawn from a common prior. Auction and mechanism theory rest on this model.

The Harsanyi transformation adds a chance move (“Nature”) that draws each player's type. Incomplete information thus becomes imperfect information. The purification theorem (1973) gives a new interpretation of mixed strategies.

JM

John Maynard Smith

1920–2004British · University of Sussex

Evolutionarily stable strategy · 1973

With George Price he introduced the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) (“The Logic of Animal Conflict”, Nature, 1973).

Awards: Crafoord Prize 1999 (with Ernst Mayr and George C. Williams)

ESSHawk–Dovereplicator dynamics
ChaptersChapter 13 · Learning and Adaptation

Significance: The ESS and the replicator dynamics (Taylor and Jonker, 1978) carry game theory to populations that do not assume rational players.

A strategy adopted by a population is evolutionarily stable if a small group of mutants cannot invade it. Every ESS is a symmetric Nash equilibrium. Book: Evolution and the Theory of Games (1982).

DG

David Gale

1921–2008American · UC Berkeley

Deferred acceptance · 1962

With Shapley he discovered the deferred acceptance algorithm (“College Admissions and the Stability of Marriage”, 1962).

stable matchingblocking pairdeferred acceptance
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism DesignChapter 5 · Auctions

Significance: Deferred acceptance always finds a stable matching with O(n²) proposals. The matching it finds is the best for the proposing side, and the algorithm is strategy-proof for that side.

Gale died in 2008; the theory of stable allocations was honored with the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2012. He also contributed to duality in linear programming and to the game of Hex. Gale gave one of the known proofs that a game of Hex cannot end in a draw and tied this result to the Brouwer fixed-point theorem.

KA

Kenneth Arrow

1921–2017American · Stanford University

Arrow's impossibility theorem · 1951

In the book Social Choice and Individual Values (1951) he proved that no rule for aggregating rankings satisfies a few natural axioms at once.

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 1972 (with John Hicks)

preference aggregationIIAno dictator
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 4 · Mechanism DesignChapter 7 · Social Choice and Voting

Significance: With three or more options, every rule that accepts every preference ranking (universal domain), outputs a complete, transitive ranking as the social outcome, and satisfies unanimity (Pareto) and independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) is a dictatorship.

Arrow's theorem began axiomatic social choice. Arrow also proved, with Debreu, the existence of a competitive equilibrium (Arrow–Debreu, 1954). This proof relies on fixed-point arguments similar to Nash's.

TS

Thomas Schelling

1921–2016American · Harvard University, University of Maryland

Focal points · commitment · segregation model

In the book The Strategy of Conflict (1960) he studied conflict and coordination. He introduced focal points (Schelling points) and the concept of credible commitment.

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2005 (with Robert Aumann)

focal pointcommitmentindividual motives and aggregate behavior
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 3 · Nash EquilibriumChapter 10 · Multi-Agent Systems

Significance: The segregation model (1969–71) showed that mild individual preferences can produce strong aggregate segregation. This model is among the first examples of agent-based simulation.

Schelling showed that limiting one's own options (burning bridges) can have strategic value. This idea appears often in deterrence and bargaining theory.

JW

John Wardrop

1922–1989British · Road Research Laboratory, University College London

Wardrop equilibrium · 1952

He proposed two principles for traffic assignment (1952): user equilibrium and system optimum. These principles are now known by his name.

user equilibriumsystem optimumtraffic assignment
ChaptersChapter 8 · Network Design and RoutingChapter 9 · The Price of Anarchy

Significance: In a Wardrop equilibrium, every used route has the same, minimal latency. The price of anarchy compares this equilibrium with the system optimum.

Beckmann, McGuire and Winsten (1956) showed that Wardrop equilibria minimize a potential function. Equilibria can therefore be computed by convex optimization.

LS

Lloyd Shapley

1923–2016American · RAND Corporation, UCLA

Shapley value · stochastic games · matching

He introduced the Shapley value (1953) and stochastic games (1953). With Gale he discovered the deferred acceptance algorithm (1962).

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2012 (with Alvin Roth)

Shapley valuecoalitioncore
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism DesignChapter 5 · AuctionsChapter 10 · Multi-Agent Systems

Significance: The Shapley value is the only allocation rule that satisfies the axioms of efficiency, symmetry, additivity and the dummy player together.

This value is the average of a player's marginal contribution over all arrival orders. Today it is used in cost sharing and in explaining machine learning predictions (SHAP). The Bondareva–Shapley theorem characterizes the games with a nonempty core.

HK

Harold W. Kuhn

1925–2014American · Princeton University

Kuhn's theorem · 1953 · Hungarian method · 1955

In the paper “Extensive games and the problem of information” (1953) he defined extensive-form games with information sets as we know them today. He showed that pure strategies suffice in finite games of perfect information and that backward induction yields an equilibrium.

Awards: John von Neumann Theory Prize 1980 (with David Gale and Albert W. Tucker)

extensive forminformation setHungarian method
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 2 · Minimax and Alpha-Beta PruningChapter 10 · Multi-Agent Systems

Significance: In Kuhn's framework, Zermelo's chess result becomes a general theorem. The value in the minimax tree is the pure-strategy value of the game of perfect information.

He published the Hungarian method for the assignment problem in 1955. The Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions also bear his name. He was a close friend and colleague of Nash.

JN

John Nash

1928–2015American · Princeton University, MIT

Nash equilibrium · 1950–51

He defined the Nash equilibrium and proved that every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies (PNAS 1950; Annals of Mathematics 1951).

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 1994 (with John Harsanyi and Reinhard Selten); Abel Prize 2015 (with Louis Nirenberg)

best responsefixed pointbargaining solution
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 3 · Nash Equilibrium

Significance: The Nash equilibrium is the central solution concept of noncooperative game theory. The existence proof rests on a fixed-point theorem.

The 1950 proof uses Kakutani's fixed-point theorem and the 1951 proof uses Brouwer's. The proof is not constructive; it was shown much later that computing an equilibrium is PPAD-complete. Nash also introduced the Nash bargaining solution (1950).

RS

Reinhard Selten

1930–2016German · Universität Bonn

Subgame-perfect equilibrium · 1965

He introduced the subgame-perfect equilibrium (1965) and the trembling-hand perfect equilibrium (1975).

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 1994 (with John Nash and John Harsanyi)

extensive formbackward inductiontrembling hand
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 3 · Nash Equilibrium

Significance: These refinements eliminate Nash equilibria that rely on non-credible threats.

A subgame-perfect equilibrium forms a Nash equilibrium in every subgame. In finite games of perfect information it is found by backward induction. Selten also posed the chain-store paradox (1978); he is among the pioneers of experimental economics.

RA

Robert Aumann

b. 1930Israeli-American · Hebrew University of Jerusalem

Correlated equilibrium · repeated games

He introduced the correlated equilibrium (1974). He defined common knowledge formally (“Agreeing to Disagree”, 1976) and developed the theory of repeated games.

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2005 (with Thomas Schelling)

correlated equilibriumcommon knowledgefolk theorem
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 3 · Nash Equilibrium

Significance: Correlated equilibria form a polyhedron that can be computed by linear programming. In learning that drives every player's internal regret to zero, the time-average distribution of the profiles played approaches the set of these equilibria.

In a correlated equilibrium, a mediator draws an action profile and privately recommends to each player their own action. No player gains by not following the recommendation. The folk theorem explains cooperation in infinitely repeated games.

KT

Daniel Kahneman & Amos Tversky

1934–2024 · 1937–1996Israeli-American · Princeton (Kahneman); Stanford (Tversky)

Prospect theory · 1979

They documented systematic biases in judgment (heuristics and biases, 1974). They proposed prospect theory (Econometrica, 1979).

Awards: Kahneman: Nobel Prize in Economics 2002 (with Vernon Smith); Tversky died in 1996, before the prize

loss aversionframingprobability weighting
ChaptersChapter 12 · Behavioral and Collective Decisions

Significance: Prospect theory replaces expected utility with reference dependence, loss aversion and probability weighting. Behavioral game theory builds on this foundation.

Framing effects break the invariance assumed by rational choice models. Kahneman's book Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) introduced the dual-process view to a wide audience.

RW

Robert Wilson

b. 1937American · Stanford University

Common-value auctions · auction formats

He pioneered the analysis of common-value auctions and the winner's curse (1969, 1977). He is one of the designers of the simultaneous multiple-round auction.

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2020 (with Paul Milgrom)

common valuewinner's cursesequential equilibrium
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 5 · Auctions

Significance: With Kreps he defined the sequential equilibrium (1982). With Milgrom he designed the spectrum auctions that the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) has used since 1994.

Wilson was the doctoral advisor of Milgrom and also advised Roth and Rosenthal. His book Nonlinear Pricing (1993) influenced the design of electricity markets.

DB

Dietrich Braess

b. 1938German · Ruhr-Universität Bochum

Braess's paradox · 1968

In 1968 he showed that adding a new road to a network can increase every driver's travel time at equilibrium.

network paradoxWardrop equilibriumselfish routing
ChaptersChapter 8 · Network Design and RoutingChapter 9 · The Price of Anarchy

Significance: Braess's paradox is the standard example showing that added capacity can harm selfish routing. With linear latencies, the price of anarchy of this example is 4/3.

The paper (“Über ein Paradoxon aus der Verkehrsplanung”, Unternehmensforschung, 1968) was translated into English in 2005. Braess's main field is numerical analysis (finite elements).

DK

Donald E. Knuth

b. 1938American · Stanford University

Analysis of alpha-beta pruning · 1975

In the paper “An analysis of alpha-beta pruning” (Artificial Intelligence, 1975), written with Ronald W. Moore, he measured how much work alpha-beta pruning saves.

Awards: Turing Award 1974

alpha-beta pruningmove orderingeffective branching factor
ChaptersChapter 2 · Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning

Significance: When moves are examined in the best order, alpha-beta looks at b⌈d/2⌉ + b⌊d/2⌋ − 1 leaves in a uniform tree of branching factor b and depth d. This result is why roughly twice the depth can be searched in the same time.

The alpha-beta idea had been found independently by several researchers in the 1950s and 1960s. Knuth and Moore wrote the method down precisely and analyzed it. Knuth is better known for The Art of Computer Programming series and for TeX.

CG

Edward H. Clarke & Theodore Groves

Clarke 1939–2013American · Clarke: economist in the US federal government; Groves: UC San Diego

Clarke pivot rule 1971 · Groves mechanisms 1973

They generalized Vickrey's idea into the VCG mechanism. Clarke (Public Choice, 1971) introduced the pivot payment, and Groves (Econometrica, 1973) introduced the general class of mechanisms.

VCGexternality pricingpublic good
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism Design

Significance: VCG (Vickrey 1961, Clarke 1971, Groves 1973) chooses the outcome that maximizes social welfare. Each agent pays the externality it imposes on the others. Truthful reporting therefore becomes a dominant strategy.

Green and Laffont (1977) showed that under unrestricted quasilinear preferences the only mechanisms that are efficient in dominant strategies are the Groves mechanisms. VCG can run a budget deficit and is vulnerable to collusion.

AG

Allan Gibbard

b. 1942American · University of Michigan

Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem · 1973

He proved that every voting rule with at least three possible outcomes that is not a dictatorship can be manipulated (Econometrica, 1973).

strategy-proofnessmanipulationdictatorship
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism DesignChapter 7 · Social Choice and Voting

Significance: Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved the theorem independently of each other. The theorem makes strategy-proofness a fundamental constraint in mechanism design.

The ways out are these: restricted preference domains (single-peaked preferences and the median rule), money (VCG), randomization, and computational hardness of manipulation.

RA

Robert Axelrod

b. 1943American · University of Michigan

Repeated Prisoner's Dilemma tournaments · 1980

He organized computer tournaments for the repeated Prisoner's Dilemma (1980). Anatol Rapoport's Tit for Tat strategy won the tournaments.

Awards: MacArthur Fellowship 1987

repeated Prisoner's DilemmaTit for Tatreciprocity
ChaptersChapter 10 · Multi-Agent SystemsChapter 13 · Learning and Adaptation

Significance: “The Evolution of Cooperation” (with W. D. Hamilton, Science 1981; book 1984) explained how reciprocity sustains cooperation.

Successful strategies were nice, retaliatory, forgiving and clear. The results inspired agent-based modeling and evolutionary dynamics.

MS

Mark Satterthwaite

b. 1945American · Kellogg School, Northwestern University

Gibbard–Satterthwaite 1975 · Myerson–Satterthwaite 1983

He proved the manipulability theorem independently of Gibbard (Journal of Economic Theory, 1975).

strategy-proofnesstwo-sided tradeimpossibility
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism DesignChapter 5 · AuctionsChapter 7 · Social Choice and Voting

Significance: The Myerson–Satterthwaite theorem (1983) shows that in two-sided trade there is no mechanism that is at once efficient, incentive compatible, individually rational and budget balanced.

These two impossibility results explain why real markets and elections must give up some desirable properties.

RR

Robert W. Rosenthal

1945–2002American · Boston University

Congestion games · potential · 1973

He introduced congestion games (1973). With the help of a potential function he proved that these games always have a pure Nash equilibrium.

congestion gamepotential functionpure equilibrium
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 8 · Network Design and RoutingChapter 9 · The Price of Anarchy

Significance: The Rosenthal potential guarantees that best-response dynamics terminate. It is the basic tool for routing games and for Price of Stability bounds.

Monderer and Shapley (1996) showed that every exact potential game is isomorphic to a congestion game. Rosenthal also introduced the centipede game (1981).

HV

Hal Varian

b. 1947American · UC Berkeley, Google

Position auctions · 2007

As Google's chief economist he studied advertising auctions in sponsored search (“Position Auctions”, 2007).

sponsored searchGSP auctionclick-through rate
ChaptersChapter 6 · Online Platforms

Significance: The symmetric Nash equilibria of the generalized second-price (GSP) auction link ad prices to click-through rates and bidders' values.

Varian joined Google in 2002 and contributed to the design of the company's ad auction and reserve price policies. His textbook Intermediate Microeconomics is widely taught.

PM

Paul Milgrom

b. 1948American · Stanford University

Auction theory · market design

He developed the theory of affiliated values and the linkage principle (with Weber, 1982). He designed spectrum auctions.

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2020 (with Robert Wilson)

linkage principleascending auctionspectrum auction
ChaptersChapter 5 · Auctions

Significance: He is among the designers of the FCC's simultaneous multiple-round auction (1994) and of the 2016–17 incentive auction.

The linkage principle explains why open ascending auctions raise more revenue when values are affiliated. Book: Putting Auction Theory to Work (2004).

CP

Christos Papadimitriou

b. 1949Greek · UC Berkeley, Columbia University

PPAD 1994 · Price of Anarchy 1999

He defined the complexity class PPAD (1994). With Koutsoupias he introduced the ratio comparing the cost of the worst equilibrium with the optimum (1999); this ratio was later named the Price of Anarchy.

Awards: Knuth Prize 2002; Gödel Prize 2012 (for the Price of Anarchy work, with Koutsoupias); IEEE John von Neumann Medal 2016

PPADPrice of Anarchytotal search problems
ChaptersChapter 2 · Games and RepresentationsChapter 3 · Nash EquilibriumChapter 8 · Network Design and RoutingChapter 9 · The Price of Anarchy

Significance: With Daskalakis and Goldberg he proved that computing a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete (2006).

PPAD covers total search problems whose solutions are guaranteed to exist by a parity argument on directed graphs (end-of-the-line, end-of-the-line). His 2001 STOC talk titled “Algorithms, Games, and the Internet” contributed to the birth of algorithmic game theory.

SH

Sergiu Hart

b. 1949Israeli · Hebrew University of Jerusalem

Regret matching · 2000

With Andreu Mas-Colell he introduced regret matching (Econometrica, 2000). In this simple adaptive method the observed play converges to the set of correlated equilibria.

regret matchinginternal regretcorrelated equilibrium
ChaptersChapter 13 · Learning and Adaptation

Significance: Learning that drives internal regret to zero leads to correlated equilibria, and learning that drives external regret to zero leads to coarse correlated equilibria.

Hart and Mas-Colell also showed that “uncoupled” dynamics cannot guarantee convergence to a Nash equilibrium in every game (2003).

EM

Eric Maskin

b. 1950American · Harvard University

Implementation theory · Maskin monotonicity · 1977

He determined which social choice rules can be implemented in Nash equilibrium (1977; published 1999).

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2007 (with Leonid Hurwicz and Roger Myerson)

Nash implementationmonotonicitysocial choice rules
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism Design

Significance: Maskin monotonicity is necessary for Nash implementation. It is also sufficient when there are three or more agents and no agent has veto power.

implementation theory asks whether all the equilibria of the designed game, not just some of them, give the desired outcome.

AR

Alvin Roth

b. 1951American · Harvard University, Stanford University

Market design · kidney exchange

He carried matching theory into practice: the redesign of the US medical residency match (Roth–Peranson, 1999), school choice in Boston and New York, and kidney exchange.

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2012 (with Lloyd Shapley)

deferred acceptancekidney exchangeschool choice
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism Design

Significance: market design treats markets as engineered systems. A good market must be thick, uncongested and make honest participation safe.

Roth showed that no stable matching mechanism is strategy-proof for both sides at once (1982). Book: Who Gets What — and Why (2015).

RM

Roger Myerson

b. 1951American · Northwestern University, University of Chicago

Revelation principle 1979 · optimal auctions 1981

He formulated the revelation principle (1979). He characterized revenue-maximizing auctions (“Optimal Auction Design”, 1981).

Awards: Nobel Prize in Economics 2007 (with Leonid Hurwicz and Eric Maskin)

revelation principlevirtual valueMyerson's lemma
ChaptersChapter 4 · Mechanism DesignChapter 5 · Auctions

Significance: virtual values reduce revenue maximization to welfare maximization. When values are independent, identically distributed and regular, the optimal auction is a second-price auction with a reserve price.

Myerson's lemma: a single-parameter allocation rule is implementable if and only if it is monotone, and in that case the payment rule is uniquely determined. With Satterthwaite he proved the impossibility theorem for two-sided trade (1983).

TR

Jean Tirole & Jean-Charles Rochet

Tirole b. 1953French · Toulouse School of Economics

Two-sided markets · 2003

They founded the economics of two-sided platforms (“Platform Competition in Two-Sided Markets”, 2003).

Awards: Tirole: Nobel Prize in Economics 2014 (alone)
