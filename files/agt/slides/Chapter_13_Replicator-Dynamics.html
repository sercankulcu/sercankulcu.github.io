    How to play

    A large population plays a symmetric game. The fitness of a strategy is its average payoff against the current population; each generation a strategy’s share is multiplied by fitness ÷ average fitness. Press Next generation or Run 25 generations and watch the bars and the history chart.

    What to look for: a strategy whose fitness is above the population average grows. Evolutionary stability is about resisting invasion; it does not mean maximizing total welfare.

    Scenarios

    • Hawk–Dove: aggressive and peaceful behaviours compete for a resource. Selection supports a mixed population rather than a single winner; the third strategy, Bourgeois, respects ownership. The payoffs use resource V = 4, fight cost C = 6 and a baseline fitness of 3; with two strategies the Hawk share converges to V/C = 2/3. Bourgeois plays Hawk when it is the owner and Dove when it is an intruder (each role with probability 1/2).
    • Coordination: strategies earn more as they match the population. A small initial majority grows into a self-reinforcing convention (path dependence).
    • Rock–Paper–Scissors: each strategy beats one rival and loses to the other; cyclic dominance keeps diversity alive. Use Level 2 or 3 (three strategies).

    Modes

    • Set up population: shape the initial shares with the boost buttons (generation 0 only), then watch selection.
    • Intervention: boosting is allowed at any time; apply a shock mid-run and see how the evolutionary paths change.
    • Computer: the population evolves without intervention.

    Levels

    • Level 1: only the first two strategies; the basic selection pressure (start 70% / 30%).
    • Level 2: three strategies (start 50% / 30% / 20%).
    • Level 3: three strategies with small mutation and random noise; extinct strategies can reappear and paths are less deterministic.

    Show the calculation: when this box in the side column is ticked, the calculation for the next generation (each strategy's fitness, its position relative to the average and its new share) appears under the bars. While it is off the screen does not change.

    Model

    • A large population plays a symmetric game. pi is the share of players using strategy i (the shares sum to 1). Mij is the payoff of strategy i against j (the matrices on the page).
    • A strategy's fitness is its average payoff against the current population; the population average is the share-weighted sum:

    fi = Σj Mij · pj     avg = Σi pi · fi     pi' = pi · fi / avg

    • This is the discrete-time replicator dynamics: a strategy whose fitness is above the average gains share, one below it loses share. The new shares again sum to 1. There are 2 strategies in level 1 and 3 in the others.
    • Start: 70% / 30% in level 1, 50% / 30% / 20% in the others. The Boost button adds 0.12 to the chosen strategy and the shares are rescaled to sum to 1 again. At most 500 generations are played.
    • Level 3 adds to each strategy, every generation, a random number from [−0.01, +0.01] and 0.004/n (a small mutation); shares cannot fall below 0.001; then everything is renormalised. Randomness occurs only in this level; the others are deterministic.

    Worked example

    Hawk–Dove, level 1: p = (0.70, 0.30), M = [[2, 7], [3, 5]].

    fHawk = 2·0.70 + 7·0.30 = 3.50   fDove = 3·0.70 + 5·0.30 = 3.60   avg = 0.70·3.50 + 0.30·3.60 = 3.53.

    New shares: Hawk 0.70·3.50/3.53 = 69.4%, Dove 0.30·3.60/3.53 = 30.6%. Dove's fitness is above the average, so its share grows a little. In this setting the shares settle at about (66.7%, 33.3%) in roughly 25 generations: there the two strategies' fitnesses are equal (f = 11/3 ≈ 3.67), nobody stays above the average and the shares stop changing. This is the mixed evolutionarily stable state: from 2p + 7(1−p) = 3p + 5(1−p) we get p = 2/3 (= V/C).

    Game-theory connection

    • Fixed points and stability: shares at which all fitnesses are equal are fixed points. An evolutionarily stable state returns after a small invader (mutation) appears. If a strategy's share is 0, the multiplicative update keeps it at 0 forever; testing invasion therefore needs the mutation of level 3.
    • Stable ≠ welfare-maximising: at the Hawk–Dove equilibrium average fitness is about 3.67; if everyone were a Dove it would be 5. Selection rewards relative success, not total payoff. Bourgeois (Hawk when owner, Dove when intruder) beats both in the payoff matrix and takes over the population.
    • Coordination: fA − fB = 4(pA − pB), so the convention with the larger initial share wins (path dependence); A and B are two separate stable states, while Flexible always earns 3 and falls behind.
    • Rock–Paper–Scissors: the interior fixed point (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) is a Nash equilibrium but not evolutionarily stable. In continuous-time replicator dynamics the shares circle around it on closed orbits. The page's step is large (one generation is a full update), so the orbit spirals outward and the shares approach the corners; level 3 with mutation keeps near-extinct strategies alive. For equilibria see Chapter 3 · Nash Equilibrium.

    What the calculation panel shows

    It expands each strategy's fitness as a sum (payoff × share), shows whether it will grow or shrink relative to the average, and the new share you will get when you press Next generation. The panel does the same computation as the real one (except for the noise in level 3) and changes no value.

    The code below is the JavaScript that runs on this page; it was split into lines and commented so it can be read. The behaviour is identical (only the calculation done for the Show the calculation panel was left out).

    1. Fitness and the generation step: fitnesses, step, boost

    const data = {                          // M[i][j]: payoff of strategy i against j
  hawk:  { names: ['Hawk', 'Dove', 'Bourgeois'],
           M: [[2, 7, 4.5], [3, 5, 4], [2.5, 6, 5]] },
  coord: { names: ['A', 'B', 'Flexible'],
           M: [[5, 1, 3], [1, 5, 3], [3, 3, 3]] },
  rps:   { names: ['Rock', 'Paper', 'Scissors'],
           M: [[2, 0, 4], [4, 2, 0], [0, 4, 2]] }
};

function fitnesses() {                  // f_i = Σ_j M[i][j] · p[j]
  const d = data[scenario.value], n = activeN();
  const fs = Array(n).fill(0);
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++)
    for (let j = 0; j < n; j++) fs[i] += d.M[i][j] * p[j];
  return { fs, avg: fs.reduce((s, v, i) => s + v * p[i], 0) };   // avg = Σ p_i f_i
}

function step() {
  if (gen >= MAX_GEN) return;           // at most 500 generations
  const n = activeN();                  // level 1: 2 strategies, the others: 3
  const { fs, avg } = fitnesses();
  const next = Array(3).fill(0);
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++)
    next[i] = p[i] * (fs[i] / Math.max(avg, .0001));   // replicator update
  if (level.value === '3')              // level 3: noise + small mutation
    for (let i = 0; i < n; i++)
      next[i] = Math.max(.001, next[i] + (Math.random() - .5) * .02 + .004 / n);
  const total = next.slice(0, n).reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0);
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) p[i] = next[i] / total;  // shares sum to 1 again
  gen++;
  /* ... log and chart bookkeeping ... */
}

function boost(i) {                     // the "+12% share" button
  p[i] += .12;
  const total = p.slice(0, n).reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0);
  for (let k = 0; k < n; k++) p[k] /= total;
}

/* reset: p = activeN() === 2 ? [.7, .3, 0] : [.5, .3, .2] */

    2. The same idea in Python

    The course's Python repository has no separate file for replicator dynamics; the naming follows the plain style of the Chapter13/ files.

    def replicator_step(M, p):
    n = len(p)
    f = [sum(M[i][j] * p[j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(n)]   # fitness
    avg = sum(p[i] * f[i] for i in range(n))                        # average fitness
    return [p[i] * f[i] / avg for i in range(n)]                    # new shares

M = [[2, 7],                    # Hawk-Dove, level 1 (Hawk, Dove)
     [3, 5]]
p = [0.7, 0.3]
for generation in range(25):
    p = replicator_step(M, p)
print([round(x, 3) for x in p])  # [0.667, 0.333]  (= V/C = 2/3)

    Try it yourself

    1. Predict, then look. Hawk–Dove, level 1; tick Show the calculation. Before pressing Next generation, say which strategy's fitness is above the average and predict how its share will change. Press and compare the bars with the panel. Compute the new share by hand with share × fitness / average.
    2. Find the fixed point. Press Run 25 generations a few times. Where do the shares settle? Once the two fitnesses and the average are equal in the panel, why are the new shares the same as the old ones? Then in Intervention mode boost Hawk a few times and run again, or do the same for Dove. Does the equilibrium come back? To compute the share directly, solve 2p + 7(1−p) = 3p + 5(1−p).
    3. Path dependence. Coordination, level 2 (A 50%, B 30%, Flexible 20%). In the panel look at the fitness difference between A and B: fA − fB is four times which quantity? In Intervention mode boost B until its share passes A's (how many presses does it take?) and run. Did the outcome change?
    4. What does mutation do? Run Rock–Paper–Scissors for 100 generations in level 2 and in level 3. In which do the strategies approach the corners, in which do all three survive? In level 3, why do the new shares in the panel differ slightly from the real shares in the chart?

    Discussion questions

    • Why does an evolutionarily stable state not have to maximise total welfare? Compare the Hawk–Dove equilibrium with an average fitness of 5.
      Answer

      Selection looks at how good a strategy is relative to the average. If everyone were a Dove the average would be 5, but then a single Hawk earns 7 and invades. The equilibrium (66.7% Hawk) resists invasion but its average is only about 3.67. This has the same structure as the prisoner's dilemma: individual interest leads to a worse outcome for the group.

    • What happens in levels 1 and 2 if a strategy's share is 0? Why is level 3 with mutation better for testing evolutionary stability?
      Answer

      The update is multiplicative (pi × a number), so a share of 0 stays 0 forever; a strategy that is absent cannot enter the population however good it is. The definition of stability asks about robustness against a small invader. The mutation in level 3 (+0.004/n) and the 0.001 floor give every strategy a small share, which is exactly this test: if the state is stable, the invader melts away again.

    • In coordination, with two stable states, what decides the outcome? Why does it matter even if neither convention is "better"?
      Answer

      The initial shares: since fA − fB = 4(pA − pB), the convention with the larger share grows and the smaller one disappears (path dependence). Even if both conventions are equally good, the population can move from a small initial difference to a lasting outcome, and going back is hard because a single deviator loses. The QWERTY keyboard and traffic rules are real examples.

    • In Rock–Paper–Scissors (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) is a Nash equilibrium, yet on the page the shares do not settle there. Why?
      Answer

      This equilibrium is not evolutionarily stable: in continuous-time replicator dynamics the shares do not converge to it but circle around it on closed orbits (the equilibrium is "neutrally stable"). The page moves in discrete steps and a generation is a full update of the shares, so the orbit spirals outward and the shares approach the corners. A Nash equilibrium does not have to be an attractor under every dynamic.
