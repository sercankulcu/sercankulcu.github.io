How to play

  1. Players take turns placing a stone on any empty hexagonal cell. Blue moves first.
  2. Blue tries to build an unbroken chain joining the left edge to the right edge (the blue edges).
  3. Purple tries to join the top edge to the bottom edge (the purple edges).
  4. Stones are never moved or captured, so every move permanently changes the geometry of the board.
  5. The first player whose stones form a connected path between the required edges wins at once; the winning path is marked on the board.
  6. Undo takes back the last move (in computer mode, your move together with the computer's reply). Cells can also be chosen with Tab and Enter.

Computer levels

  • Easy plays at random.
  • Medium takes or blocks a winning cell; otherwise, most of the time, it plays the cell that shortens its own path the most.
  • Hard also takes or blocks a winning cell first; otherwise it always picks the cell that most improves the difference between the opponent's and its own remaining shortest path (the number of empty cells each side still needs).

Reading the board

Connections do not have to form a straight line. Neighbouring hexagons touch in six directions, so links that look diagonal can still be part of one unbroken path. A useful pattern is the bridge: two stones placed so that two possible connection points remain between them. Since the opponent can usually block only one point at a time, bridges create robust future paths.

Strategy tips

  • Build flexible networks of connections instead of a single fragile line.
  • Block a cell when a single move would hand the opponent a decisive connection.
  • Cells near the centre usually support more possible paths than edge cells.
  • Do not judge a position by stone count alone; connectivity and future path options matter more.

Connection to game theory

Hex is a deterministic, perfect-information, two-player, zero-sum game. There are no chance events or hidden cards: both players see the whole state. Because a completed Hex board cannot end in a draw, the strategy-stealing argument proves that the first player has a winning strategy, even though on large boards nobody knows what it is.
