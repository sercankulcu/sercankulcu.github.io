Multiple Choice · Agent Properties

Question 1

To keep doing its job, a drone plans on its own to return to the station before its battery runs out. Which property does this behavior illustrate?

The correct answer is B, proactiveness. Without waiting for a warning, the drone takes the initiative and makes a goal-directed plan to prevent a future problem (a dead battery). Reactivity (A) is sensing a change in the environment and responding at once, for example braking when an obstacle appears. Social ability (C) is communication with other agents; a convention (D) is not an agent property but a coordination rule.

Multiple Choice · Spectrum of Interests

Question 2

In a corridor game, two robots each get (1, 1) if they go to the same side and (−1, −1) if they go to different sides. How many pure Nash equilibria are there?

The correct answer is A, 2. At (Right, Right) each robot gets 1; a robot that switches alone to Left drops to −1, so nobody deviates. The same holds for (Left, Left). At (Right, Left) and (Left, Right) both get −1 and the robot that changes sides rises to 1, so these are not equilibria. There is also a ½–½ mixed equilibrium, but the question asks for pure ones. The "everyone drives on the right" convention picks one of the two equally good equilibria in advance.

Multiple Choice · Coordination and Control

Question 3

Which is a drawback of centralized control?

The correct answer is D. In centralized control every decision passes through one center: if it fails, the system stops (a single point of failure), and the center becomes a bottleneck as the number of agents grows. The center also depends on agents to report private information such as costs, and selfish agents may report it dishonestly. A is a drawback of distributed control and C is an advantage of distributed control; B is wrong, because if all the information is at the center, the optimum can be computed.

Multiple Choice · Contract Net

Question 4

What is the order of steps in the contract net protocol?

The correct answer is A. In Smith's (1980) protocol the manager first announces the task (task announcement); contractors that receive it send their costs as bids. The manager compares the bids and gives the contract to the best one (the award), and the contractor reports the result when the job is done. No one can bid on an unannounced task (B), and the award cannot be made before the bids are collected (C, D).

Multiple Choice · Optimum and Evaluation

Question 5

In the slides' example the costs (min) are: for T1, R1 2 and R3 6; for T2, R1 3 and R2 7; for T3, R2 4 and R3 9. The contract net finds 18 min, while the optimum is 13 min. Why does the contract net miss the optimum?

The correct answer is B. The contract net gives each task in turn to the cheapest robot at that moment: for T1 it picks R1 (2), although R1 is also the cheapest candidate for T2 (3). Because R1 is committed early, T2 goes for 7 and T3 for 9; the optimum instead gives T1 to the slightly more expensive R3 (6) and saves R1 for T2. A is wrong (the manager does not know the costs). The first half of D (robots bid honestly) is a valid assumption in the example, but the second half is wrong: inflating costs does not reduce the loss, which comes from the greedy, sequential auction. C concerns communication cost (and it is only O(n) per task).

Multiple Choice · Shapley Value

Question 6

Which axiom says that a player who contributes nothing to any coalition has a Shapley value of 0?

The correct answer is C, the dummy player axiom: if v(S ∪ {i}) = v(S) for every S, then i's marginal contribution in every order is 0, so φi = 0. Efficiency (A) says the shares add up to v(N), symmetry (B) says two players making the same contribution receive equal shares, and additivity (D) says that in the sum of two games the Shapley values add. Shapley (1953) showed that the Shapley value is the only rule satisfying all four together.

Multiple Choice · Power Indices

Question 7

Weighted voting game [51; 50, 49, 1]. What is the Shapley–Shubik index (A, B, C)?

The correct answer is A, (2/3, 1/6, 1/6). The quota is 51: A (50) cannot pass alone but passes with B or with C; B + C = 50 does not pass. Over the six arrival orders, the pivotal player who pushes the total past the quota is B in A → B → C, C in A → C → B, and A in the other four orders: A gets 4/6, B 1/6, C 1/6. (3/5, 1/5, 1/5) is the Banzhaf index (D); (1/2, 49/100, 1/100) is just the weight ratios (B). B's 49 seats give no more power than C's single seat.

Multiple Choice · Public Goods Game

Question 8

In the public goods game, under which condition does the dilemma (individual incentive conflicting with social welfare) exist?

The correct answer is D. A one-token contribution returns r/n to the contributor and a total of r to the group. If r < n, then r/n < 1 and contributing is individually harmful (not contributing dominates); if r > 1, every token raises the group's social welfare. Together, 1 < r < n creates the dilemma. If r < 1 (A), contributing also hurts the group; if r ≥ n (B, C), every token returns at least 1 to the contributor and full contribution is (weakly) dominant. In the slides' example r/n = 1.6/4 = 0.4.

Classic Question · Cooperation and Competition

Question 9: Pure Nash Equilibria

Two robots share a communication channel; each uses Low (L), Medium (M) or High (H) bandwidth. Payoffs (row, column):

R1 ╲ R2LMH
L(4, 4)(2, 5)(1, 6)
M(5, 2)(3, 3)(0, 2)
H(6, 1)(2, 0)(−1, −1)

Mark the best responses and find all pure Nash equilibria. Why is (L, L) not an equilibrium?

Show answer

R1's best responses: R2 = L → H (6); R2 = M → M (3); R2 = H → L (1). The game is symmetric: R2's best responses are the same.

The cells where both players are at a best response: (M, M) = (3, 3), (H, L) = (6, 1), (L, H) = (1, 6): three pure Nash equilibria.

(L, L) = (4, 4) is better than (M, M) for both, but it is not an equilibrium: a robot that switches to H while the other plays L gets 6 instead of 4. As in the Prisoner's Dilemma, individual incentive conflicts with the common good; and which equilibrium gets played is a coordination problem.

Classic Question · Power Indices

Question 15: The Shapley–Shubik Index

Weighted voting game [6; 4, 2, 2, 1]: A has 4 votes, B 2, C 2, D 1; the quota is 6. (a) Compute the Shapley–Shubik index over the 4! = 24 arrival orders. (b) What is the Banzhaf index? Compare weight with power.

Show answer

B + C + D = 5 < 6: nothing can be won without A (A has a veto). A + D = 5 is not enough either; A needs B or C beside it.

(a) A is not pivotal only when A arrives first (6 orders) or when only D precedes A (D, A, ·, ·: 2 orders). In these 8 orders the pivotal player is the first of B or C to arrive after A: B in 4 orders, C in 4. D is pivotal in no order.

Multiple Choice · Agent Architectures

Question 18

A warehouse robot has two layers. The lower layer stops within milliseconds, with no planning, when someone steps in front of it. The upper layer plans the picking route at the start of the day from the order list. Which agent architecture is this?

The correct answer is C, a hybrid architecture: the reactive lower layer supplies the fast response and the upper layer plans the long-term goal (the route); autonomous vehicles are built this way. A describes only the lower layer; planning a route needs a model of the environment and search. B has no separation of beliefs, desires and intentions. D is wrong: neither of the two layers described learns from experience.

Multiple Choice · Agent Architectures

Question 19

A virtual assistant with a BDI architecture holds: (1) “The user's calendar is free on Friday at 2 pm.” (2) “The user wants to see the dentist this week.” (3) “I have decided to book an appointment for Friday at 2 pm and am carrying out this plan.” Which components are these, in order?

The correct answer is D. A belief is information the agent holds to be true about the world: the free slot in the calendar. A desire is a state it wants to reach: an appointment this week. An intention is a plan chosen from among the desires, committed to and carried out: the Friday 2 pm appointment. A swaps the first two, B the last two, and C the first and the last.

Classic Question · The Core

Question 20: The ε-Core

A game with three agents:

SABCABACBCABC
v(S)00070605690

The ε-core: Σi∈N xi = v(N) and Σi∈S xi ≥ v(S) − ε for every S ≠ N (no coalition can gain more than ε by leaving). (a) Is the core empty? (b) What is the smallest ε for which the ε-core is not empty, and what is the allocation then? (c) For which ε is x = (40, 30, 20) in the ε-core?

Show answer

(a) Add the pair constraints: 2(xA + xB + xC) ≥ 70 + 60 + 56 = 186, so total ≥ 93. But the total must be 90 ⇒ the core is empty.

(b) The same sum with ε: 180 ≥ 186 − 3ε ⇒ ε ≥ 2. At ε = 2 the pair constraints hold with equality: xA + xB = 68, xA + xC = 58, xB + xC = 54 ⇒ x = (36, 32, 22). Total 90 ✓, singleton constraints (≥ −2) ✓.

(c) Shortfalls: {A, B} 70 − 70 = 0, {A, C} 60 − 60 = 0, {B, C} 56 − 50 = 6. The largest shortfall is 6 ⇒ ε ≥ 6; at ε = 5, {B, C} would leave with 6 more and block this allocation.

The ε-core with the smallest ε is called the least core; here it is a single point.
