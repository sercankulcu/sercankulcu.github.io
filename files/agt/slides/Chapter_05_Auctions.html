Basic Concepts

Warm-up Game: The Vickrey Auction

The rules

Everyone writes a bid in a sealed envelope; the highest bidder gets the item but pays the second-highest bid, not their own.

Think about it
  • If the item is worth 70 TL to you, would you write less than 70, exactly 70, or more?
  • Would your bid change if you knew the rivals' bids?
  • If the winner paid their own bid (first price), would writing 70 still be a good idea?

Basic Concepts

Why Auctions Matter

  • Efficient mechanisms for allocating resources: the item tends to go to whoever values it most.
  • Price discovery and market transparency: the seller sets a price without knowing the buyers' values.
  • The most widespread application of mechanism design: an allocation rule + a payment rule.
Art

Ascending-price English auctions at auction houses.

Online advertising

Ad auctions run on every search.

Telecom licenses

Spectrum auctions; multi-item designs worth billions.

Procurement, treasury

Sealed bids; government bond auctions.

Basic Concepts

Key Terms and the Model

Value vi

The bidder's true value for the item; private information.

Bid bi

The number reported to the mechanism; it may differ from the value.

Utility

ui = vi − pi if they win (pi: payment), 0 if they lose.

Independent private values (IPV)

Values are drawn independently and each bidder knows their own. The baseline model of auction theory.

Second-price example (Cem 85, second bid 70): quasi-linear utility.

Examples usually assume vi ~ U[0, 1] (uniform) and risk-neutral bidders.

Basic Concepts

Valuation Models

Private values

Each bidder values the item based on their own information (e.g. a painting for their own wall).

Common value

The item's true value is the same for everyone, but the bidders' estimates differ (e.g. an oil field).

Interdependent values

Both private information and the other bidders' information affect valuations.

The common-value model leads to the winner's curse: the winner tends to be the most optimistic estimator, which produces expected losses.

Sealed bids: everyone bids once, in secret. Nobody sees the others' bids.

Sealed-Bid Strategies

The Strategic Property of the Vickrey Auction

  • The highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid.
  • With independent private values and a single item, bidding your true value is a weakly dominant strategy (Vickrey, 1961).
  • The idea: your bid determines only whether you win, not what you pay.

v = 70. Dashed red: the truthful bid. No bid beats it for any B.

The result may fail with common values, budget constraints or interdependent values. Vickrey is the single-item special case of VCG.

Sealed-Bid Strategies

Back to the Game: The Vickrey Auction

Theory's answer: write your value

The opening envelopes: you 70, rivals 60 and 45. Bidding truthfully you win and pay 60: a gain of 10. Writing 65 or 100 you would pay the same 60; writing 55, the item goes for 60 and you miss the gain of 10.

Your bid only decides whether you win; the rivals' highest bid sets the price: truthful bidding is weakly dominant. Under first price, the same 70 leaves 70 − 70 = 0 even if you win; there you must bid below your value.

In practice

In lab experiments, overbidding in second-price sealed-bid auctions is common and does not easily disappear with repetition; the same subjects converge to equilibrium much faster in ascending English auctions (Kagel, Harstad and Levin, 1987).

In everyday life: in eBay's automatic (proxy) bidding, the winner pays one increment above the second-highest bid — very close to the second-price rule.

Moving the bid above or below the value never raises the gain for any B; it only lowers it for some B.

Sealed-Bid Strategies

Bid Shading in First-Price Auctions

  • In a first-price auction, a truthful bid is pointless: even if you win, utility is v − v = 0.
  • Lowering the bid raises the profit when you win but lowers the chance of winning:
E[u(b)] = (v − b) · P(win with b)
  • The equilibrium bid balances this trade-off and depends on how others bid → a Bayes–Nash equilibrium.
  • The Dutch auction is strategically the same: “at what price will I accept?” = a shaded bid.

Two bidders, v = 0.8; the rival bids half their value.

def best_bid(v, n, step=0.1):
  beta = (n - 1) / n        # rivals bid beta*v
  best, b_best = -1, 0
  b = 0.0
  while b <= v:
    p = min(1, b / beta) ** (n - 1)  # P(win)
    eu = (v - b) * p
    if eu > best:
      best, b_best = eu, b
    b = round(b + step, 10)
  return b_best, best

Sealed-Bid Strategies

Bid Shading as the Number of Bidders Grows

Equilibrium bid b(v) = (n − 1)v / n  ·  shading = v − b(v) = v/n  ·  expected revenue = (n − 1)/(n + 1)

As the number of bidders grows, competition reduces shading and bids approach values; expected revenue is the same under first and second price (revenue equivalence) and rises with n.

Revenue and Information

The Revenue Equivalence Theorem

  • With independent private values, risk-neutral symmetric bidders and the same allocation (highest value wins), standard auctions yield the same expected revenue (Vickrey 1961; Myerson 1981; Riley and Samuelson 1981).
  • Why? Myerson's payment formula p(v) = v·x(v) − ∫0v x(z) dz fixes the payment from x(·) and the lowest type's payment (0): same x(·) → same revenue. For n = 2, U[0, 1]: p(v) = v² − v²/2 = v²/2; first price: bid v/2 × win probability v; second price: v × expected rival value v/2. All agree.
  • Risk aversion, budgets, common values or asymmetry can break it.

Revenue and Information

Revenue Maximization: Myerson and the Reserve Price

  • What if the seller wants to maximize expected revenue rather than efficiency? (Myerson, 1981)
  • Virtual value ψ(v) = v − (1 − F(v))/f(v). F(v): the probability that the value is below v; f(v): the density. For U[0, 1], F(v) = v, f(v) = 1 ⇒ ψ(v) = 2v − 1.
  • Expected revenue equals the expected virtual value of the winner. The optimal mechanism: give the item to the largest ψ, and do not sell if ψ < 0.
  • If ψ is increasing and bidders are symmetric, this is a second-price auction with a reserve price: ψ(r) = 0 ⇒ 2r − 1 = 0 ⇒ r = 1/2 (independent of n).
  • With two bidders and reserve r, expected revenue is 1/3 + r² − 4r³/3; at r = 1/2 it rises from 1/3 → 5/12 ≈ 0.417. The cost: sometimes nothing is sold (an efficiency loss).

def revenue(r, m=100):   # 2 bidders, U[0.1]
  v = [(i + .5) / m for i in range(m)]
  total = sum(max(min(a, c), r) for a in v
              for c in v if max(a, c) >= r)
  return total / m**2     # mean payment
def best_reserve(step=0.1):
  best, r_best = -1, None
  for k in range(round(1/step) + 1):
    R = revenue(k * step)
    if R > best: best, r_best = R, k * step
  return r_best, best

Revenue and Information

All-Pay and Multi-Unit Auctions

All-pay auction

The highest bid wins but everyone pays their bid: lobbying, R&D races, election campaigns.

Two bidders, U[0, 1]: in equilibrium b(v) = v²/2. Value 0.8 → bid 0.32 (0.4 under first price). Types with little chance of winning bid almost nothing.

Expected revenue is again 1/3 (revenue equivalence): a type-v bidder's expected payment is v²/2, as under first and second price.

Multi-unit auctions

With identical units (government bonds), winners pay either a uniform price per unit or their own bids; for items valuable together (spectrum licenses), package bids are used.

Further reading: Bulow and Klemperer (1996): attracting one extra bidder earns as much as the optimal reserve price, and often more.

The all-pay model describes contests with a single prize winner: when values are equal and commonly known, expected total spending equals the value of the prize.

Revenue and Information

The Winner's Curse

  • In a common-value auction, the highest bid comes from the bidder who overestimates the value the most.
  • If the winner ignores their estimation error, they may pay more than the item's true value.
  • Even if estimates are right on average, the largest estimate is systematically too high.
  • To reduce this risk, bidders bid below their signals and take the quality of their information into account.
  • First documented in Gulf of Mexico oil-lease auctions (Capen, Clapp, Campbell, 1971).

Estimation errors U[−20, 20]: the winner's expected overestimate is 20(n−1)/(n+1). More rivals, bigger curse (simulation gives the same values).

Two-Sided Markets

Double Auctions

  • Many buyers and many sellers submit orders at once: buyers post bids, sellers post asks.
  • Stock exchanges, electricity markets and carbon permit markets work this way.
  • Matching: sort bids in decreasing and asks in increasing order; pairs match as long as bid ≥ ask.
  • Price: a single market price is chosen from the interval between the last matched pair and the first unmatched pair (e.g. the midpoint).
  • Total surplus (buyer + seller surplus) = Σ (bid − ask) over matched pairs → the largest possible value.

Two-Sided Markets

Step by Step: Matching in a Double Auction

Order book: buyers are willing to pay 70, 40, 90, 55, 80; sellers to accept 60, 85, 35, 75, 50.

Bids are sorted decreasing (the demand curve), asks increasing (the supply curve).

Pair 1: 90 ≥ 35 → match; this trade creates surplus 90 − 35 = 55.

Pair 2: 80 ≥ 50 → match; this trade creates surplus 80 − 50 = 30.

Pair 3: 70 ≥ 60 → match; this trade creates surplus 70 − 60 = 10.

Pair 4: 55 < 75 → the buyer won't pay what the seller asks; stop. 3 trades take place.

Price interval: [max(60, 55), min(70, 75)] = [60, 70]. The midpoint is price = 65; every matched trader trades at this price.

Buyer surplus (90−65)+(80−65)+(70−65) = 45, seller surplus (65−35)+(65−50)+(65−60) = 50; total 95 = the largest possible total surplus.

Two-Sided Markets

Code Trace: double_auction(bids, asks)

Sort bids from high to low: the most eager buyer first.

Asks from low to high: the cheapest seller first.

k: number of matched pairs; n: length of the shorter list.

Condition: b[0] = 90 ≥ s[0] = 35? Yes.

Pair matched → k = 1.

Condition: b[1] = 80 ≥ s[1] = 50? Yes.

Pair matched → k = 2.

Condition: b[2] = 70 ≥ s[2] = 60? Yes.

Pair matched → k = 3.

Condition: b[3] = 55 ≥ s[3] = 75? No. The loop ends.

k ≠ 0: at least one trade, so compute the price.

lo = max(s[k−1], b[k]) = max(60, 55) = 60: the last seller must not lose, and the excluded buyer must not want to buy.

hi = min(b[k−1], s[k]) = min(70, 75) = 70.

Result: 3 trades at price (60 + 70)/2 = 65.

def double_auction(bids, asks):
  b = sorted(bids, reverse=True)  # buyers
  s = sorted(asks)                # sellers
  k, n = 0, min(len(b), len(s))
  while k < n and b[k] >= s[k]:
    k += 1                   # pair matched
  if k == 0:
    return 0, None
  lo = max([s[k-1]] + b[k:k+1])
  hi = min([b[k-1]] + s[k:k+1])
  return k, (lo + hi) / 2

Two-Sided Markets

Incentives in Two-Sided Markets

Impossibility

With independent values and costs whose ranges overlap, bilateral trade cannot achieve efficiency + IC + IR + budget balance together (Myerson–Satterthwaite, 1983).

The midpoint rule

Efficient and budget-balanced, but not IC: the marginal buyer can shade their bid to pull the price down.

McAfee's rule (1992)

Let p0 be the average of the first excluded buyer and seller. If p0 falls between the last matched buyer and seller, everyone trades at p0; otherwise the last (least profitable) pair is dropped. Truthfulness is dominant; the cost is sometimes one trade.

In our example

p0 = (55 + 75)/2 = 65 ∈ [60, 70] → all three trades happen at 65; no trade is lost.

Two-Sided Markets

Code Trace: mcafee(bids, asks)

A new example (buyers 90, 80, 70, 40; sellers 30, 50, 65, 85): bids sorted decreasing, asks increasing; sentinels (−∞ and ∞) are appended.

b[0] = 90 ≥ s[0] = 30? Yes: an efficient pair.

k = 1: 1 efficient trade found.

b[1] = 80 ≥ s[1] = 50? Yes: an efficient pair.

k = 2: 2 efficient trades found.

b[2] = 70 ≥ s[2] = 65? Yes: an efficient pair.

k = 3: 3 efficient trades found.

b[3] = 40 ≥ s[3] = 85? No: the loop ends.

Candidate price from the first excluded pair: p₀ = (40 + 85)/2 = 62.5.

Is p₀ ∈ [s[2], b[2]] = [65, 70]? No (62.5 < 65): the 3rd seller would lose money.

Drop the weakest pair (70, 65): 2 trades; buyers pay 70, sellers receive 65. The leftover 2·5 = 10 stays with the market maker.

def mcafee(bids, asks):
  b = sorted(bids, reverse=True) + [float('-inf')]
  s = sorted(asks) + [float('inf')]
  k = 0
  while b[k] >= s[k]:
    k += 1                 # efficient trade
  if k == 0: return 0, None, None
  p0 = (b[k] + s[k]) / 2   # excluded pair
  if s[k-1] <= p0 <= b[k-1]:
    return k, p0, p0       # one price
  return k - 1, b[k-1], s[k-1]  # two prices

Two-Sided Markets

Auctions and Market Design

  • Market design decides who gets matched with whom, and under which rules.
  • The core goals are efficiency, incentive compatibility, fairness, stability and practicality; rules should be strategyproof.
  • Auctions are market-design tools that allocate scarce resources by prices and bids.
  • Electricity markets, emission permits, procurement and spectrum licenses use prices; in markets without money, priority and matching rules take the place of prices.
  • There can be trade-offs among revenue, efficiency, simplicity and strategyproofness.

Two-Sided Markets

From Theory to Practice: A Brief History

An ancient institution

Herodotus describes auctions in Babylon in the 5th century BC. In AD 193 the Praetorian Guard auctioned the Roman throne to Didius Julianus.

Theory

Vickrey (1961) compared the four formats and found revenue equivalence; Myerson (1981) characterized the revenue-maximizing auction. Nobel: Vickrey 1996, Myerson 2007.

Spectrum auctions

In 1994 the US FCC ran its first spectrum auction using the simultaneous multi-round ascending design proposed by Milgrom, Wilson and McAfee. Milgrom and Wilson: Nobel 2020.

Matching markets

Roth and Shapley (Nobel 2012): stable matching and exchange rules for markets that do not use prices.

The designer's tools: allocation and payment rules, information disclosure (which bids are shown, and when?), entry conditions. Designs are tested in lab experiments and with field data before deployment.

Assessment

Why Are Vickrey Auctions Rarely Used?

  • Trust: bidders must trust that the second-highest bid is reported honestly (risk of shill bids).
  • Intuition: “pay the second price” feels unnatural to most people; overbidding is common in the lab.
  • Model assumptions: with common or interdependent values, truthful bidding may no longer be dominant.
  • Multiple items: budget constraints and complementary goods complicate the payment rule (VCG).
  • Seller concerns: the risk of a low realized price or collusive bidding.
So practical designs often balance incentives for truthfulness against ease of use (e.g. the English auction reaches the same outcome transparently). Details: Rothkopf, Teisberg and Kahn (1990); Ausubel and Milgrom (2006).

Assessment

Discussion: Which Auction, and How Fair?

Which format would you rather join?
  • English: transparent, and seeing the price step by step is an advantage.
  • Vickrey: truthful bidding is best; no need to strategize.
  • Dutch / first price: fast, but you must guess how much to shade.
  • The choice depends on personal strategy and appetite for risk.
Are auctions and market design fair?
  • Rules are fixed in advance → predictability; manipulation can be reduced.
  • But deep-pocketed players have an advantage; access to participation and information asymmetry matter.
  • Seller revenue and equal opportunity are different goals; “perfect fairness for all” is not guaranteed.
