Multiple Choice · Learning Models

Question 1

In the multi-armed bandit problem, what does the player observe in each round?

C is correct. In the multi-armed bandit one arm is pulled per round and only that arm's reward is seen; what the other arms would have paid that round is unknown (partial feedback). That is why exploration is needed: no information can be gathered about an arm that is never tried. B, seeing the payoff of every action, is Hedge's full-information setting. A is the information fictitious play looks at, and D is the information replicator dynamics looks at.

Multiple Choice · Learning Models

Question 2

In which class of games does best-response dynamics always stop at a pure Nash equilibrium?

A is correct. In a potential game every profitable unilateral deviation strictly increases a common potential function. Because the game is finite, the potential cannot increase forever; the dynamics stop at a profile where nobody wants to deviate, that is, at a pure Nash equilibrium (Monderer–Shapley, 1996). D is wrong: Matching Pennies is a finite game but has no pure equilibrium, so the dynamics cycle. Since Matching Pennies is also zero-sum, B is wrong too; C is the cycle itself.

Multiple Choice · Best-Response Dynamics

Question 3

How does best-response dynamics behave in Matching Pennies?

D is correct. The Matcher wants the same face, the Mismatcher a different one. At (H,H) the Mismatcher switches to T → (H,T); the Matcher switches to T → (T,T); the Mismatcher switches to H → (T,H); the Matcher switches to H → (H,H) again. Because there is no pure equilibrium, in every profile some player is unhappy and the dynamics enter an endless cycle; so A and C are wrong. B is wrong too: the dynamics keep jumping between pure profiles; it is the frequencies of fictitious play that approach the mixed equilibrium.

Multiple Choice · Best-Response Dynamics

Question 4

In fictitious play, what does a player best-respond to?

B is correct. In fictitious play the player treats the opponent as a mixed strategy that plays according to the frequencies of their past actions and best-responds to it. For example, if the opponent played T in 5 of 7 rounds, the player assumes they play (2/7, 5/7). A, responding only to the last action, is best-response dynamics. In C the player looks at their own history, but a best response is computed against the opponent's behavior. In D there is no learning at all.

Multiple Choice · Fictitious Play

Question 5

For which of the following is convergence of fictitious-play frequencies to equilibrium NOT guaranteed?

D is correct. Convergence of fictitious-play frequencies to equilibrium is proven for two-player zero-sum games (Robinson, 1951), 2×2 games (Miyasawa, 1961) and potential games (Monderer–Shapley, 1996). Shapley (1964), however, built a general-sum 3×3 game in which the actions rotate in ever-longer cycles and the frequencies approach no equilibrium. A, B and C are classes with a convergence guarantee.

Multiple Choice · Hedge and Equilibrium

Question 6

If all players use a no-regret algorithm such as Hedge, what does the time average of play approach?

A is correct. If every player's external regret satisfies RT/T → 0, the time average of the profiles played approaches a coarse correlated equilibrium (CCE): nobody can gain on average by switching to “always play a′”. D (correlated equilibrium) requires a stronger condition: small internal (swap) regret. B is wrong: a pure Nash equilibrium may not exist (Matching Pennies). C is wrong too: a CCE need not be Pareto optimal.

Multiple Choice · Hedge and UCB

Question 7

With payoffs in [0, 1] and n actions, which bound is obtained if Hedge uses η = √(ln n / T)?

C is correct. Hedge's general bound is RT ≤ (ln n)/η + ηT; the first term grows when η is small, the second when η is large. With η = √(ln n / T) both terms equal √(T ln n), so RT ≤ 2√(T ln n). For example, for n = 3 and T = 10,000 the bound is ≈ 2·√10,986 ≈ 210, only 0.021 per round. A ((ln n)/η) and D (ηT) are each only one part of the bound; B (T/2) is the worst-case regret of following the leader.

Multiple Choice · Bandits and Q-Learning

Question 8

If ε is kept constant, how does the regret of the ε-greedy algorithm grow with T?

A is correct. With constant ε the algorithm pulls a random arm with probability ε in every round; some of these rounds go to bad arms, and each round adds a constant expected loss. Over T rounds this loss accumulates and the regret grows linearly (≈ c·T). In a 5000-round comparison, ε-greedy with 10% exploration grows linearly while UCB1 grows logarithmically (B) (Auer et al., 2002). Logarithmic regret requires ε to shrink over time; C and D cannot be obtained with a constant ε.

Multiple Choice · Multi-Agent Learning and Evolution

Question 9

Why do single-agent Q-learning guarantees fail in a multi-agent environment?

D is correct. The convergence proof of single-agent Q-learning assumes a stationary environment, where transitions and rewards do not change over time. In a multi-agent setting the outcomes an agent sees depend on the other agents' policies; as they learn, the environment stops being stationary and an action that was good yesterday may be bad today. A is wrong: there is a reward signal; B is wrong: the number of states can be finite; C (γ < 1) is a standard assumption that helps convergence. Self-play (AlphaZero) is one way to manage this problem.

Multiple Choice · Axelrod's Tournament

Question 10

In a 10-round match, how many points does Tit-for-Tat score against Always Defect (temptation 5, mutual cooperation 3, mutual defection 1, sucker 0)?

C is correct. TFT cooperates in the first round and Always Defect defects: TFT gets 0 (sucker), the opponent gets 5 (temptation). In the next 9 rounds TFT copies the opponent's last move (defection) and mutual defection continues: 1 per round, 9·1 = 9 in total. TFT scores 0 + 9 = 9, Always Defect 5 + 9 = 14 (D). B (10) counts the first round as 1 as well; A assumes TFT never retaliates.

With Q(s, a) ← Q + α[r + γ·max Q(s′, ·) − Q], what are Q(s0, right), Q(s1, right), Q(s2, right) after three episodes?

Classic Question · Replicator Dynamics

Question 17: Replicator Dynamics

Hawk–Dove: resource V = 6, fight cost C = 10, baseline fitness 3. Payoffs: two hawks fight and each gets (V − C)/2; a hawk against a dove gets V, a dove against a hawk gets 0; two doves share and each gets V/2. (a) Write the payoff table and fH, fD when the hawk share is x. (b) What is the rest point (ESS) x*? (c) Starting from x = 0.5, compute the hawk share one generation later with the discrete replicator x′ = x·fH/f̄.

Show answer

(a) HH = (V − C)/2 = −2, HD = V = 6, DH = 0, DD = V/2 = 3. fH = 3 − 2x + 6(1 − x) = 9 − 8x; fD = 3 + 3(1 − x) = 6 − 3x.

(b) 9 − 8x = 6 − 3x → x* = 3/5 = 0.6 = V/C. Check: at x = 0.6, fH = fD = 4.2.

(c) x = 0.5: fH = 5, fD = 4.5, f̄ = 0.5·5 + 0.5·4.5 = 4.75. x′ = 0.5·5/4.75 = 10/19 ≈ 0.526. Hawks increase because they earn more than the average, and move toward x* = 0.6 (the next generation ≈ 0.546).

Classic Question · ESS Check

Question 19: ESS Check

Hawk–Dove: resource V = 6, fight cost C = 10. Payoffs (row player): u(H, H) = (V − C)/2 = −2, u(H, D) = 6, u(D, H) = 0, u(D, D) = 3.

Strategy x is an ESS against mutant y ⇔ u(x, x) > u(y, x) or [u(x, x) = u(y, x) and u(x, y) > u(y, y)].

(a) Is pure Dove an ESS? (b) Is pure Hawk an ESS? (c) Does the mixed x* (hawk probability V/C) satisfy the ESS condition against mutants H and D? (d) What if V = 10, C = 6?

Show answer

(a) u(D, D) = 3 < u(H, D) = 6: a hawk mutant invades → not an ESS.

(b) u(H, H) = −2 < u(D, H) = 0: a dove mutant invades → not an ESS.

(c) x* = 6/10 = 0.6. At the mixed equilibrium every mutant gets the same payoff against x*: u(H, x*) = u(D, x*) = u(x*, x*) = 1.2. Second condition: u(x*, H) = 0.6·(−2) = −1.2 > u(H, H) = −2 ✓; u(x*, D) = 0.6·6 + 0.4·3 = 4.8 > u(D, D) = 3 ✓ → ESS.

(d) V > C: u(H, H) = 2 > u(D, H) = 0 → pure Hawk is an ESS; if fighting is cheaper than the resource, everyone becomes a hawk.

Classic Question · A Noisy Match

Question 20: A Noisy Match

Prisoner's Dilemma payoffs (C: cooperate, D: defect): CC (3, 3), CD (0, 5), DC (5, 0), DD (1, 1). In an 8-round match player A mistakenly plays D instead of C in round 3; there is no other error.

(a) If two TFTs play, what are the move sequences and scores? (b) If two Win-Stay, Lose-Shift players play (WSLS: if you got 3 or 5, repeat the move; if you got 0 or 1, switch; in the erroneous round the move actually played counts; C in round 1)? (c) Compare with the error-free match (24 – 24).

Show answer

(a) A: C C D C D C D C, B: C C C D C D C D. After round 3 the two TFTs echo each other: 21 – 21.

(b) Round 3 DC: A got 5, stays with D; B got 0, switches to D. Round 4 DD: both got 1 and return to C. After that always CC. A: C C D D C C C C, B: C C C D C C C C → 24 – 19.

(c) Totals: error-free 48, TFT 42, WSLS 43. In TFT a single error starts a retaliation cycle that lasts to the end of the match; WSLS returns to cooperation within two rounds. That is why in noisy settings WSLS and generous TFT can do better than TFT.

Multiple Choice · Thompson and EXP3

Question 21

Which statement is true about Thompson sampling and EXP3?

B is correct: Thompson sampling keeps a prior distribution over each arm's mean, updates it with observations, in each round draws a sample from this posterior, and plays the arm with the largest sample; an uncertain arm is explored automatically because it sometimes gives a high sample. EXP3 is the version of Hedge that sees only the reward of the pulled arm; even when rewards are chosen by a malicious adversary, its regret grows more slowly than T, so the regret per round goes to zero. A is wrong: in a bandit only the pulled arm's reward is seen. C is wrong: Thompson samples randomly. D is wrong: EXP3 is designed precisely for the adversarial case.

Classic Question · Correlated Equilibrium

Question 22: Chicken and the Traffic Light

Chicken (row, column): (Swerve, Swerve) = (6, 6), (Swerve, Straight) = (2, 7), (Straight, Swerve) = (7, 2), (Straight, Straight) = (0, 0). (a) A traffic light recommends (Swerve, Straight) or (Straight, Swerve) with probability ½ each: is it a correlated equilibrium (CE), and what is the expected payoff? (b) Is the distribution that recommends (Swerve, Swerve), (Swerve, Straight), (Straight, Swerve) with probability 1/3 each a CE, and what is the payoff? (c) Test the CCE condition in both. Why do CE and CCE not differ in a 2×2 game?

Show answer

(a) Recommendation “Swerve”: the other is surely Straight; obey 2, deviate 0 ✓. “Straight”: the other is surely Swerve; obey 7, deviate 6 ✓. CE, payoff 4.5.

(b) “Swerve”: the other is Swerve / Straight half and half; obey (6 + 2)/2 = 4, deviate (7 + 0)/2 = 3.5 ✓. “Straight”: obey 7, deviate 6 ✓. CE, payoff (6 + 2 + 7)/3 = 5 (mixed Nash: 14/3).

(c) Light: always Swerve 4, always Straight 3.5 ≤ 4.5 ✓. Triple: always Swerve and always Straight 14/3 ≤ 5 ✓: both are CCE.

“Always Straight” makes a difference only when Swerve is recommended; so the CCE condition is the same as the CE condition “Straight when told Swerve”. Telling them apart needs at least three actions.

Multiple Choice · CE versus CCE

Question 23

Rock–Paper–Scissors (winner +1, loser −1, tie 0). A recommendation device draws (Rock, Rock), (Paper, Paper), (Scissors, Scissors) with probability 1/3 each and tells each player only their own action. Which is true?

The correct answer is B. Obeying always gives 0. CCE: a player who commits to a fixed action faces an opponent playing Rock, Paper, Scissors with probability 1/3 each; the expected payoff is 1/3·(+1 + 0 − 1) = 0, so no gain: a CCE. CE: a player who sees “Rock” knows the other was also told Rock; switching to Paper gives +1 > 0: not a CE. A misses the deviation that depends on the recommendation. C is impossible: every CE is a CCE. D is wrong: always Rock gives 0 too.
