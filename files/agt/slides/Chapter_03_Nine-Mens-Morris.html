Phase: Place

Black

How to play

  1. Placement: White and Black alternate placing their nine pieces on empty points. White starts.
  2. Three of your pieces on one straight line form a mill. After forming a mill you remove one opposing piece (outlined in red). Pieces inside a mill can only be removed if all of the opponent's pieces are in mills.
  3. Movement: once a player has placed all nine pieces, that player moves one piece along a line to an adjacent empty point: click the piece, then a green point.
  4. Flying: a player with only three pieces left may move a piece to any empty point.
  5. A player loses when reduced to two pieces or when unable to move on their turn.

In Human vs Computer you play White and the computer plays Black. Changing the mode starts a new game.

Computer levels

  • Easy: random moves, but often closes a mill when it can.
  • Medium: two-ply search (its move and your reply).
  • Hard: three-ply alpha-beta search valuing material, mills, open two-in-a-rows and mobility.

Understanding mills

A mill is valuable not only because it removes a piece but because it can become a repeated threat: opening and re-closing the same mill forces the opponent to defend every turn. Strong positions combine two almost-complete mills so that one move threatens both.

Strategy tips

  • During placement, prefer flexible points (the middle of the sides connect four lines) over filling one line early.
  • Block the opponent's two-in-a-rows before they close.
  • Avoid becoming immobile, even with more pieces.
  • When removing, consider the opponent's mobility and future mills, not just material.

Game-theory connection

Nine Men's Morris is a deterministic, sequential, perfect-information game whose rules change across phases. It was solved in 1993: with perfect play it is a draw. It is a good example of game-tree search, tempo, threat creation and zugzwang-like mobility problems.
