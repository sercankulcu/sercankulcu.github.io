    How to play

    Pick a scenario, a mode, and a level; then choose an action each round. Payoffs are shown as (row player, column player); you are the row player. The cell of the last round is highlighted and pure Nash equilibria are outlined. The log keeps the most recent rounds. The Show the calculation box shows how those equilibria are found (best-response marks, indifference equations) and the computer's last decision.

    Scenarios

    • Prisoner's Dilemma (Silent / Betray): Betray is a dominant strategy, so (Betray, Betray) is the unique Nash equilibrium, yet mutual silence pays both players more. Individual rationality and collective welfare pull in opposite directions.
    • Coordination (Concert / Cinema): both players gain from choosing the same event but prefer different outcomes. There are two pure Nash equilibria: the problem is picking the same one.
    • Matching Pennies (Heads / Tails): the row player wins if the choices match, the column player if they differ. There is no pure equilibrium; the only Nash equilibrium is mixed: each action with probability 1/2. Predictable play gets exploited.

    Modes

    • Human–Computer: you choose and the computer responds according to the level.
    • Human–Human: on one device; player 1's choice is kept hidden, then player 2 chooses and the round is revealed. The level has no effect.
    • Computer–Computer: when the match starts, 20 rounds are simulated; both agents use the chosen level. Press New match to run it again.

    Levels

    • 1 — Random: each action with probability 1/2; ignores the history.
    • 2 — Best response: the best reply to the opponent's last action (assumes it will be repeated).
    • 3 — Adaptive: counts the opponent's past actions and best-responds to the most frequent one (fictitious play). Repeated patterns become exploitable.

    Suggested path

    Play each scenario first against level 1, then levels 2 and 3. Use Computer–Computer to compare long-run behavior (level 2 against level 2 cycles in Matching Pennies and settles in the Prisoner's Dilemma). Finally play Human–Human and explain your strategy before the choices are revealed.

    The model

    Each scenario is a 2 × 2 matrix game. The row player has two actions and so does the column player; the cell m[a][b] = [u1, u2] holds the row's and the column's payoff when row a and column b are chosen. The payoffs are fixed; randomness appears only in the computer's choices (and in tie-breaking).

    Scenario(row 1, col 1)(row 1, col 2)(row 2, col 1)(row 2, col 2)
    Prisoner's Dilemma(3, 3)(0, 5)(5, 0)(1, 1)
    Coordination(4, 3)(0, 0)(0, 0)(3, 4)
    Matching Pennies(1, −1)(−1, 1)(−1, 1)(1, −1)

    Pure equilibria: best-response marking

    The page finds pure equilibria with isNE. Think of it as marking, for each column, the row player's highest payoff and, for each row, the column player's highest payoff; a cell that carries both marks is a pure Nash equilibrium and gets the outline:

    (a, b) is a pure Nash equilibrium ⇔ u1(a, b) ≥ u1(1−a, b) and u2(a, b) ≥ u2(a, 1−b)

    Example (Prisoner's Dilemma, cell Betray–Betray): with the column on Betray, Betray pays the row 1 and Silent pays 0; 1 ≥ 0 holds. With the row on Betray, Betray pays the column 1 and Silent 0; 1 ≥ 0 holds. Both marks are present, so the cell is an equilibrium. In the Silent–Silent cell, 3 ≥ 5 fails, so it is not. In Matching Pennies no cell carries both marks: there is no pure equilibrium. Because the comparison is ≥, a tie also counts as a best response (these three matrices have no ties).

    Mixed equilibrium: indifference

    The page's own code does not search for mixed equilibria; the "1/2–1/2" in the legend is text. We can find the mixed equilibrium with the indifference principle from the lecture: let p be the probability that the row plays its first action and q the probability that the column does. For the column to mix, the row's mixture must leave it indifferent (and vice versa). With A the row's and B the column's payoff matrix:

    row indifferent: q·A00 + (1−q)·A01 = q·A10 + (1−q)·A11 ⇒ q = (A11 − A01) / (A00 − A01 − A10 + A11)
    column indifferent: p·B00 + (1−p)·B10 = p·B01 + (1−p)·B11 ⇒ p = (B11 − B10) / (B00 − B01 − B10 + B11)

    If the denominator is 0 the preference does not depend on the probability (no solution); if the result is outside (0,1) there is no interior mixed equilibrium. In the Show the calculation panel this computation is written out with the numbers of the open scenario:

    ScenarioqpResult
    Prisoner's Dilemma(1−0)/(3−0−5+1) = −1−1Not a probability: no interior mixed equilibrium (Betray dominates).
    Coordination(3−0)/(4−0−0+3) = 3/7(4−0)/(3−0−0+4) = 4/7Mixed equilibrium; expected payoff (12/7, 12/7) ≈ (1.71, 1.71), below the (4, 3) or (3, 4) of the pure equilibria.
    Matching Pennies(1+1)/(1+1+1+1) = 1/2(−1−1)/(−1−1−1−1) = 1/2Mixed equilibrium 1/2–1/2; expected payoff (0, 0).

    The computer's three levels

    • Level 1: a coin flip (Math.random() < .5), ignoring the history. In Matching Pennies this is the equilibrium strategy; in the other two games it does not exploit the opponent's mistakes.
    • Level 2: a coin flip if the history is empty; otherwise the best response to the opponent's last action (bestResponse): it compares its two payoffs against that action, takes the larger, and flips a coin on a tie.
    • Level 3: counts the opponent's past actions, takes the most frequent as its prediction (coin flip on a tie), and best-responds to the prediction. This is fictitious play with pure actions (Chapter 13).

    Example (Matching Pennies, Level 3, the computer is the column player): you played Heads, Heads, Tails, Heads. Count: Heads × 3, Tails × 1; prediction: Heads. With the row on Heads the column gets −1 for Heads and +1 for Tails, so the computer chooses Tails. The panel shows exactly this line.

    Levels 2 and 3 only look at the past; they do not build a model of "you", but if you play in a pattern you get exploited. In Matching Pennies the equilibrium is precisely being unpredictable: against a player who mixes 1/2–1/2 no level gets ahead in expected payoff. This idea belongs to Chapter 3 · Nash Equilibrium and Chapter 13 · Learning and Adaptation.

    What does the panel show?

    When the box is ticked, each player's best response is underlined in the matrix; the panel writes (1) the computer's last decision (which level, what it looked at, the payoffs, its choice), (2) the pure-equilibrium computation for this matrix, and (3) the indifference equations. The calculation is only displayed; it changes neither the results nor the randomness of the game.

    1. The matrix and pure equilibria

    const games = {
  pd:    { tr: ['Silent', 'Betray'], m: [[[3, 3], [0, 5]], [[5, 0], [1, 1]]], ... },
  coord: { tr: ['Concert', 'Cinema'],    m: [[[4, 3], [0, 0]], [[0, 0], [3, 4]]], ... },
  mp:    { tr: ['Heads', 'Tails'],        m: [[[1, -1], [-1, 1]], [[-1, 1], [1, -1]]], ... }
};
/* m[a][b] = [row payoff, column payoff]; a = row action, b = column action */

/* cell (a, b) is a pure Nash equilibrium if both players are at a best response */
function isNE(m, a, b) {
  return m[a][b][0] >= m[1 - a][b][0] && m[a][b][1] >= m[a][1 - b][1];
}

    2. Best response

    /* best response of player 'role' when the opponent plays 'opp' */
function bestResponse(opp, role) {
  const m = currentGame().m;
  if (role === 0) {                                  /* row player */
    const u0 = m[0][opp][0], u1 = m[1][opp][0];
    return u0 === u1 ? (Math.random() < .5 ? 0 : 1) : (u0 > u1 ? 0 : 1);
  }
  const u0 = m[opp][0][1], u1 = m[opp][1][1];        /* column player */
  return u0 === u1 ? (Math.random() < .5 ? 0 : 1) : (u0 > u1 ? 0 : 1);
}

    3. The computer's levels

    function cpuMove(role) {
  const level = +levelSelect.value;
  const oppIndex = role === 0 ? 1 : 0;               /* opponent's column in hist */
  if (level === 1 || !hist.length) {                 /* level 1, or no history yet */
    whyLast[role] = { kind: level === 1 ? 'random' : 'first' };   /* record for the panel */
    return Math.random() < .5 ? 0 : 1;               /* coin flip */
  }
  if (level === 2) {                                 /* best response to the last action */
    const last = hist[hist.length - 1][oppIndex];
    whyLast[role] = { kind: 'last', pred: last };
    return bestResponse(last, role);
  }
  const counts = [0, 0];                             /* level 3: count the history */
  hist.forEach(r => { counts[r[oppIndex]]++; });
  const predicted = counts[0] === counts[1]
    ? (Math.random() < .5 ? 0 : 1)                   /* tie: coin flip */
    : (counts[0] > counts[1] ? 0 : 1);               /* most frequent action = prediction */
  whyLast[role] = { kind: 'count', counts: counts, pred: predicted };
  return bestResponse(predicted, role);              /* best response to the prediction */
}

    The same idea in Python

    The same pattern as pure_nash and mixed_2x2 in the course's Python repo (Chapter03):

    from fractions import Fraction as F

def pure_nash(U1, U2):                # same as Chapter03/pure_nash.py
    m, n, eq = len(U1), len(U1[0]), []
    for i in range(m):
        for j in range(n):
            br1 = max(U1[k][j] for k in range(m))     # row's best payoff in column j
            br2 = max(U2[i][l] for l in range(n))     # column's best payoff in row i
            if U1[i][j] == br1 and U2[i][j] == br2:   # both marks present: equilibrium
                eq.append((i, j))
    return eq

def mixed_2x2(A, B):                  # same as Chapter03/mixed_2x2.py
    da = A[0][0] - A[0][1] - A[1][0] + A[1][1]
    if da == 0: return None           # degenerate
    q = F(A[1][1] - A[0][1], da)      # q that makes the row indifferent
    db = B[0][0] - B[0][1] - B[1][0] + B[1][1]
    if db == 0: return None
    p = F(B[1][1] - B[1][0], db)      # p that makes the column indifferent
    if not (0 < p < 1 and 0 < q < 1):
        return None                   # no interior eq.
    u1 = A[0][0] * q + A[0][1] * (1 - q)
    return p, q, u1

# Coordination: A = row payoffs, B = column payoffs
print(pure_nash([[4, 0], [0, 3]], [[3, 0], [0, 4]]))         # [(0, 0), (1, 1)]
p, q, u1 = mixed_2x2([[4, 0], [0, 3]], [[3, 0], [0, 4]])
print(p, q, u1)                                            # 4/7 3/7 12/7

    The fictitious-play core of level 3 (same idea as Chapter13/fictitious.py):

    def best_response(U, opp):            # U[action][opponent's action]; the page flips a coin on ties
    return max(range(2), key=lambda k: U[k][opp])

def level3(opp_history, U):           # fictitious play (see Chapter13/fictitious.py)
    counts = [opp_history.count(0), opp_history.count(1)]
    predicted = 0 if counts[0] >= counts[1] else 1   # most frequent action (the page flips a coin on ties)
    return best_response(U, predicted)

    Discussion questions

    • The page outlines only pure equilibria, yet it says "the mixed equilibrium is 1/2–1/2" for Matching Pennies. Where does this number come from, and why does it not appear in any cell?
      Answer

      A mixed equilibrium is not a cell but a probability distribution: each player picks actions with certain probabilities. The number comes from the indifference principle: the column must find both actions equally good because of the row's mixture. In Matching Pennies q = (1+1)/(1+1+1+1) = 1/2. Since what is played each round is a pure cell (such as Heads–Tails), the equilibrium shows up not in one cell but in the long-run frequencies.

    • The mixed equilibrium of Coordination pays an expected 12/7 ≈ 1.71, below the 4 or 3 of the pure equilibria. Why might players still end up there?
      Answer

      A mixed equilibrium is not a compromise but a consistency condition: taking the other side's mixture as given, nobody gains by deviating alone. There is no particular reason to choose it, since the pure equilibria pay more; the real problem is which equilibrium to pick (coordination). Without communication, the mixed equilibrium makes the players often land in a mismatched cell, (Concert, Cinema) or (Cinema, Concert), which is why the payoff is lower.

    • Why does Level 3 beat someone who always plays Heads but not someone who mixes 1/2–1/2?
      Answer

      Level 3 treats the past frequency as a forecast. For an always-Heads player the forecast is right and the best response wins every round. Against a 1/2–1/2 player the frequencies approach 1/2 but the next move is still an independent coin flip; whichever action Level 3 picks, the expected payoff is 0. The equilibrium strategy guarantees this 0 (the security level) whatever the opponent does.

    • In the Prisoner's Dilemma, why do two Level 2 players settle in (Betray, Betray) right away? What would it take for them to move to cooperation?
      Answer

      Level 2 best-responds to the opponent's last action only, and since Betray pays more in every case (5 > 3, 1 > 0) it chooses Betray every round; after the first round both stay there. Cooperation needs a strategy that takes the future into account, such as one that returns the opponent's move (tit-for-tat) or one that carries a threat. Such strategies can form an equilibrium in repeated games (the tournament in Chapter 13).
