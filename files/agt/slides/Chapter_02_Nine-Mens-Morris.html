How to play

  1. Placing: White and Black take turns putting their nine stones on empty points. White starts.
  2. Three stones on one line form a mill. A player who forms a mill removes one of the opponent's stones (shown with a red outline). Stones in a mill can be removed only if all of the opponent's stones are in mills.
  3. Moving: once a player has placed all nine stones, they slide a stone along a line to an adjacent empty point: click the stone first, then the green point.
  4. Flying: a player left with only three stones may move a stone to any empty point.
  5. A player reduced to two stones, or unable to move on their turn, loses.

Against the computer you play White and the computer plays Black. Changing the mode starts a new game. When Show move reasoning is ticked, the point numbers (1–24) appear on the board and the AI's last move stays marked with a yellow ring.

Computer levels

  • Easy: random moves, but it often closes a mill when it gets the chance.
  • Medium: a two-ply search (its own move and your reply).
  • Hard: a three-ply alpha-beta search that evaluates material, mills, open pairs, and mobility.

Understanding mills

A mill is valuable not only because it removes a stone but because it can become a repeated threat: opening and re-closing the same mill forces the opponent to defend every turn. Strong positions combine two nearly complete mills so that a single move threatens both.

Strategy tips

  • During placing, prefer flexible points (the midpoints of the middle square connect to four neighbors) over filling a line too early.
  • Block the opponent's pairs before they close.
  • Avoid getting immobilized, even when you have more stones.
  • When removing a stone, consider not only material but also the opponent's mobility and future mills.

Link to game theory

Nine Men's Morris is a deterministic, sequential game of perfect information whose rules change from phase to phase. It was solved in 1993: with perfect play it ends in a draw. It is a good example for game-tree search, tempo, creating threats, and zugzwang-like mobility problems.

What is searched?

The AI really searches the game tree from the Minimax and Alpha-Beta lecture. A tree node is a position (board, stones in each hand, side to move); a branch is a full move: a step and, if the step closes a mill, the stone to remove. The move generator depends on the phase (steps, fullMoves):

  • Placing: put a stone on any empty point. 24 branches at the start, fewer later.
  • Sliding: move one of your stones to an adjacent empty point. Usually 6–12 branches.
  • Flying: a player with exactly three stones on the board (none in hand) flies a stone to any empty point: 3 stones × dozens of empty points, easily more than 40 branches.
  • Mills and removals: a step that closes a mill is not a single branch; it splits into one branch for every removable opponent stone (removables: stones not in a mill, or all stones if all are in mills). A mill multiplies the branching several times over, which is why removals are the most expensive part of the search.

Search: negamax + alpha-beta

The game is zero-sum, so one function is enough: negamax always returns the value from the point of view of the player to move. The child's value is from the opponent's point of view, so its sign is flipped and the alpha-beta window is negated and swapped: -negamax(child, d-1, -beta, -alpha). This is the MAX/MIN pair of the Minimax lecture written as one function; when alpha ≥ beta the remaining siblings are cut. There is no move ordering and no transposition table.

  • Depth: Easy does not search. Medium searches 2 plies (the AI's move + your reply), Hard 3 plies (AI + you + AI again). If either side is flying, Hard also drops to 2.
  • Terminal positions: if the player to move has fewer than 3 stones in total (board + hand) or no step at all, that player has lost and the value is -10000 - depth. The more depth is left, the earlier the loss happened and the worse it is; the AI prefers to delay a defeat and to speed up a win.
  • Root and randomness: the root moves are shuffled first and each is searched with a full window (−∞, +∞), so every value you see in the panel is exact, not a bound. Among moves tied for the highest value, the first one in the shuffled order is taken, so ties are broken at random.

The evaluation function

When the search reaches its depth, evalFor(state, p) adds up the following for player p:

TermWhat is countedWeight
stones(my stones on the board + in hand) − (the opponent's)×100
millslines with my three stones − lines with the opponent's three×12
pairslines with two of my stones + one empty point − the opponent's×9
mobilityempty neighbors of my stones − the opponent's (only for a side that is past placing and has more than 3 stones)×2

Because stones in hand count too, placing a stone does not change the material; only a removal does. That is where the pull of a removal comes from: a stone is worth 100 points, the mill itself only 12.

Why this depth?

The branching factor is about 15–24 when placing, 6–12 when sliding, and above 40 when flying; with mills it grows several times over because of the choice of stone. At depth 3 the tree without pruning is around 20 × 15 × 20 ≈ 6,000 positions; with flying it jumps to 40 × 40 × 40 ≈ 64,000. The panel shows the number of visited positions, so you can see the difference between Medium and Hard yourself. The page must answer instantly in the browser on every move; keeping the depth at 2 avoids seconds of waiting while flying.

A small example

White stones are on points 4, 11, 22, 23; Black stones on 1, 2, 13. White has placed 4 stones, Black 3, and it is Black's turn (Medium, depth 2). White has two open pairs: 4–11 (completed on point 19) and 22–23 (completed on point 24).

If Black plays put 3 ×4 (completing the line of points 1-2-3 and taking the White stone on point 4), the position right after the move is worth, for Black: stones +100, mills +12, pairs −9 = +103. But the search also sees White's reply: White closes its own mill and takes a Black stone, and the value falls to 0. A move that removes nothing, such as put 9 or even put 19 (which blocks one of the pairs), lets White close the other mill: stones −100 and mills −12, i.e. −112. The gap is 112 points, so the AI picks a removing move. All four removal choices have the value 0; the AI takes one of them at random.

What does the panel show?

When Show move reasoning is ticked, every time the AI moves the panel lists the level, depth, number of candidate moves, number of visited positions and the 6 best candidates. Each row: the move (put N, A→B, ×N = removed stone) · the terms of the position right after the move (the table above) · the search value. The chosen move is marked with ▶ and a yellow background; if several moves tie for best, this is noted at the top.

1. Move generation, evaluation and search

// Points: 0..23 (outer square to inner square, row by row)
const LINES = [ ... ];   // 16 lines of 3 points: each one can become a mill
const ADJ   = ... ;      // ADJ[i]: neighbors of point i (along the lines)

// Opponent stones that may be removed: those not in a mill; if all are in mills, all of them
function removables(b, opp) {
  const all = [];
  const free = [];
  for (let i = 0; i < 24; i++)
    if (b[i] === opp) {
      all.push(i);
      if (!inMill(b, i, opp)) free.push(i);
    }
  return free.length ? free : all;
}

// Single steps, depending on the phase (placing / sliding / flying)
function steps(s, p) {
  const b = s.b;
  const out = [];
  if (placing(s, p)) {                              // stones in hand: may place on every empty point
    for (let i = 0; i < 24; i++) if (!b[i]) out.push({ from: -1, to: i });
    return out;
  }
  const fly = count(b, p) === 3;                    // only a player with exactly 3 stones flies
  for (let i = 0; i < 24; i++) {
    if (b[i] !== p) continue;
    const targets = fly
      ? b.map((v, j) => v ? -1 : j).filter(j => j >= 0)   // any empty point
      : ADJ[i].filter(j => !b[j]);                        // adjacent empty points
    targets.forEach(j => out.push({ from: i, to: j }));
  }
  return out;
}

// Full move = step + (if a mill closes) the stone to remove. A mill multiplies the branching.
function fullMoves(s) {
  const p = s.turn;
  const out = [];
  steps(s, p).forEach(m => {
    const nb = stepB(s.b, m, p);                    // apply the step on a new board
    if (inMill(nb, m.to, p)) {
      const r = removables(nb, 3 - p);
      if (r.length) r.forEach(x => out.push({ from: m.from, to: m.to, rem: x }));
      else out.push({ from: m.from, to: m.to, rem: -1 });
    } else out.push({ from: m.from, to: m.to, rem: -1 });
  });
  return out;
}

// Has the player to move lost? (down to 2 stones, or no move)
function loser(s) {
  const p = s.turn;
  if (total(s, p) < 3) return p;                    // total: stones on the board + in hand
  if (!steps(s, p).length) return p;
  return 0;
}

// Evaluation from the point of view of player p
function evalFor(s, p) {
  const o = 3 - p;
  let v = (total(s, p) - total(s, o)) * 100;        // stone difference (stones in hand included)
  let mob = 0;
  LINES.forEach(l => {
    let a = 0, c = 0, e = 0;                        // my / opponent / empty points on the line
    l.forEach(x => { if (s.b[x] === p) a++; else if (s.b[x] === o) c++; else e++; });
    if (a === 3) v += 12;                           // my mill
    if (c === 3) v -= 12;
    if (a === 2 && e === 1) v += 9;                 // open pair: a mill in one move
    if (c === 2 && e === 1) v -= 9;
  });
  // mobility: only for players past placing with more than 3 stones
  if (!placing(s, p) && count(s.b, p) > 3)
    for (let i = 0; i < 24; i++) if (s.b[i] === p) mob += ADJ[i].filter(j => !s.b[j]).length;
  if (!placing(s, o) && count(s.b, o) > 3)
    for (let i = 0; i < 24; i++) if (s.b[i] === o) mob -= ADJ[i].filter(j => !s.b[j]).length;
  return v + mob * 2;
}

// Negamax + alpha-beta: the value is always from the point of view of the PLAYER TO MOVE
function negamax(s, depth, alpha, beta) {
  const l = loser(s);
  if (l) return -10000 - depth;                     // I lost; the more depth left, the earlier I lost
  if (depth === 0) return evalFor(s, s.turn);
  let best = -Infinity;
  for (const m of fullMoves(s)) {
    const v = -negamax(applyMove(s, m), depth - 1, -beta, -alpha);   // negate the opponent's value
    if (v > best) best = v;
    if (best > alpha) alpha = best;
    if (alpha >= beta) break;                       // cutoff
  }
  return best;
}

// At the root: every move is searched with a full window, ties are broken at random
function chooseMove() {
  const list = fullMoves(st);
  if (!list.length) return null;
  const level = difficultySelect.value;
  if (level === 'Easy') { ... }                     // random move, no search
  const flying = (!placing(st, 1) && count(st.b, 1) === 3) ||
                 (!placing(st, 2) && count(st.b, 2) === 3);
  const depth = level === 'Hard' && !flying ? 3 : 2;   // Hard: 3; Medium, or someone flying: 2
  let best = [], bestVal = -Infinity;
  list.sort(() => Math.random() - .5).forEach(m => {   // shuffle first
    const v = -negamax(applyMove(st, m), depth - 1, -Infinity, Infinity);
    ...                                             // (v is recorded for the reasoning panel)
    if (v > bestVal) { bestVal = v; best = [m]; }
    else if (v === bestVal) best.push(m);           // equal best moves
  });
  return best[0];                                   // first of the shuffled order: random among ties
}

The same idea in Python

The same pattern as the course's Python examples: negamax with alpha-beta, and best_move at the root.

from math import inf
import random

def evaluate(s, p):                       # evaluation for player p
    o = 3 - p
    v = (total(s, p) - total(s, o)) * 100  # stone difference (hand included)
    for line in LINES:
        mine = sum(s.board[x] == p for x in line)
        theirs = sum(s.board[x] == o for x in line)
        empty = 3 - mine - theirs
        if mine == 3:   v += 12            # my mill
        if theirs == 3: v -= 12
        if mine == 2 and empty == 1:   v += 9    # open pair
        if theirs == 2 and empty == 1: v -= 9
    return v + 2 * (mobility(s, p) - mobility(s, o))

def negamax(s, d, a, b):
    if s.loser():                          # the player to move has lost
        return -10000 - d
    if d == 0:
        return evaluate(s, s.turn)
    best = -inf
    for m in s.moves():                    # step + stone to remove
        v = -negamax(s.play(m), d - 1, -b, -a)
        best = max(best, v)
        a = max(a, best)
        if a >= b:
            break
    return best

def best_move(s, d):
    scored = [(-negamax(s.play(m), d - 1, -inf, inf), m) for m in s.moves()]
    top = max(v for v, _ in scored)
    return random.choice([m for v, m in scored if v == top])   # random among ties

Try it yourself

  1. Predict first, then look. In You vs AI mode pick Medium and tick Show move reasoning. During placing, put down one stone; before the AI answers, guess which point it will use. Then compare with the row marked ▶ in the panel. Was your guess among the top 3 candidates?
  2. Recompute a value yourself. Pick one row of the panel and read its "right after the move" terms (stones, mills, pairs). Apply that move in your head and count Black's open pairs and mills on the board. Match the panel: mills = 12 × (mine − opponent's), pairs = 9 × (mine − opponent's).
  3. Change the depth and compare. Play the same first three moves at Medium, then at Hard (New game). Write down the number of visited positions in the header and the values of the top three candidates. By what factor did the number grow? Does Hard put a different move first than Medium picked? If the AI placed on different points in the two games, at least compare the position counts.
  4. Find the AI's mistake. At Medium, the AI sees only its own move and your reply, not its second move. Try to set up a fork: with one stone turn two different lines into pairs at once, so the AI can block only one. When the AI misses the block, look at the values in the panel: why did the candidate that stops the fork not look better? Try the same trap on Hard.

Discussion questions

  • Why is the "right after the move" value of a removing move sometimes much larger than the search value in the panel?
    Answer

    The value right after the move never sees the opponent's reply; the search takes that reply into account. Black removes a stone and reaches +103, but White's mill reply takes a stone back and the value drops to 0. So the static value answers "what is there now" and the search value answers "what is left after the opponent's best reply". With more depth, the gap covers more replies.

  • Why does the evaluation also count the stones in hand? How would the AI play if only the stones on the board counted?
    Answer

    A stone in hand is a stone that will be on the board; the losing condition (fewer than 3 in total) also looks at the board plus hand. If only board stones counted, placing a stone would raise the material by 100 and the AI would try to place stones as fast as possible without thinking about mills, while the worth of a removal would become blurred. Counting the total makes only real losses change the material.

  • At the root every move is searched with the full (−∞, +∞) window. How does that affect the values, and what does it cost?
    Answer

    Every root move's value comes out exact, not a bound; that is why the panel can show the true values of all candidates and why ties are recognized correctly (one of the tied moves is picked at random). The cost is giving up the cutoff gain between siblings at the root: even after a better move is found, the next move is searched from scratch with a full window, which raises the number of visited positions. If only the best move were needed, we could carry alpha through the root and search fewer positions.

  • Nine Men's Morris was solved in 1993 (a draw with perfect play). Why can this three-ply AI still be beaten, and what is missing?
    Answer

    Solving means computing the value of every position in advance (retrograde analysis, about 1010 positions). The AI only looks 2–3 plies ahead and then trusts a hand-written evaluation; it cannot see a mill plan or a fork beyond its horizon. The knowledge of the solution is stored in a table; the AI does not have it. This gap is the basic limit of every game program that works with heuristic evaluation instead of exhaustive search.
