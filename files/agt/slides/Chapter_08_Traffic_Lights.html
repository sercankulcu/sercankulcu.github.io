How the simulation works

Cars arrive at random from four directions and cross a signalised intersection. Only one approach has a green light at a time; the other three are red.

  • Each phase is green → yellow (1.5 s) → all-red (0.8 s), so the intersection clears before the next direction starts.
  • Cars stop at the white stop line on red or yellow and queue behind each other. A car that has already passed the stop line continues until it leaves the crossing.
  • The number in the black badge at each approach is its current queue.
  • Pause stops or resumes the simulation, Reset removes all cars and statistics, Add car creates a car from a random direction.

Signal controllers

  • Fixed cycle: green rotates North → East → South → West, 6 seconds each, regardless of traffic.
  • Queue-adaptive: after a minimum green of 3 s, the light switches as soon as the current approach is empty or another approach has a queue at least 3 cars longer; it then gives green to the longest queue (maximum green 12 s).

Reading the statistics

Wait is the time a car spends stopped. Average and maximum wait are computed over cars that have left the intersection. Compare the two controllers under the same arrival rate: the adaptive controller usually lowers average waiting time, but can make an approach with few cars wait longer — an efficiency-versus-fairness trade-off.

What this shows

An intersection is a shared resource. Drivers would each prefer a green light; the signal is a coordination mechanism that resolves the conflict. Different allocation rules (round-robin vs. demand-based) give different total delay and different distributions of delay across approaches, the same questions mechanism design asks about any shared resource.
