Platforms and Markets

What Is an Online Platform?

  • An online platform: a digital ecosystem (an intermediary) that brings users, sellers and service providers together.
  • The value comes less from the platform itself than from the sides' access to each other: a two-sided market.
  • The platform sets the rules: who joins, who sees whom, who pays what → a mechanism.

Platforms and Markets

Two-Sided Markets and Cross-Side Network Effects

Cross-side network effect

The number of participants on one side determines the value the other side gets (more sellers → more choice for buyers).

Price structure

The platform chooses not only the total price but how the fee is split between the sides (pA, pB).

The chicken-and-egg problem

No sellers, no buyers; no buyers, no sellers: one side has to be “pulled in” first.

Platforms and Markets

The Platform Is a Mechanism, Users Are Players

  • Platform design (fees, ranking and rating rules) steers users' strategic behavior.
  • Users choose strategies according to incentives; the resulting behavior is a Nash equilibrium under the rules.

The platform's incentives

  • Bonuses: extra pay for drivers in busy areas (Uber)
  • Discounts, coupons, free trials
  • Ranking and recommendation algorithms → visibility
  • Ratings and badges (trusted seller)

Users' strategic responses

  • Fake reviews and rating inflation
  • Multiple accounts, bonus abuse
  • Gaming the algorithm (click farms)
  • Retaliatory negative reviews
Right incentives → cooperation and efficiency. Wrong incentives → manipulation and loss of trust.

Network Effects

Types of Network Effects

Direct (same side)

WhatsApp: the more of your friends use it, the more valuable it is to you.

Indirect / cross-side

Uber: more drivers → shorter waits → more riders → more work for drivers.

Negative

Crowding → congestion, information overload; sellers on the same side compete with each other.

Local

Value comes from your neighborhood (city, circle of friends), not from all users; competition is won locally.

A positive network effect creates a feedback loop: winners grow, losers shrink. The market can “tip” one way.

Network Effects

A Simple Adoption Model

  • N = 1000 potential users; user i's type θi ~ U[0, 1] measures how much they care about the platform.
  • If a fraction x of the population joins, i's utility is θi·x − p, where p = 0.16 (a fee or the effort of joining).
  • A user joins ⟺ θi·x ≥ p ⟺ θi ≥ p/x. Since θ is uniform on [0, 1], the fraction with θ above p/x is 1 − p/x.
Fraction joining if x is expected: F(x) = 1 − p/x  (0 if x ≤ p)
  • Equilibrium: a self-fulfilling expectation, F(x) = x.
  • Dynamics: this period's adopters set next period's expectation, xt+1 = F(xt).

A simplified version of the network-effects model in Easley and Kleinberg (2010, Chapter 17).

A lower fee means a lower threshold. For x² − x + p = 0 to have real roots, the discriminant 1 − 4p ≥ 0, i.e. p ≤ 0.25; if p > 0.25 the market never forms.

Launch subsidies

Free periods, bonuses: p falls, and so does the critical mass.

Seeding

The platform supplies the first content, sellers or drivers itself.

Managing expectations

An “everyone's here” perception lifts x₀ above the threshold.

Standalone value

Start with a tool that is useful without the network; build the network later.

Example: WhatsApp's value grew rapidly as users joined; Friendster collapsed through the same loop in reverse once users started leaving.

Platform Pricing

Principles of Two-Sided Pricing

  • Besides the overall price level, the platform chooses the price structure: which side pays, and how much? (Rochet and Tirole, 2003)
  • The side that adds more value to the other side, or is more price-sensitive, gets a lower price — even a subsidy (a negative price).
  • The loss is recovered from fees on the other side, which the subsidized side attracts.
Search / social media

Users are free; advertisers pay.

Credit cards

Rewards for cardholders, fees from merchants.

Marketplaces

Coupons for buyers, commissions from sellers.

Revenue models

Commissions, subscriptions, ads, per-transaction fees.

In a simple linear model (Armstrong, 2006) the rule is: pA = markup − the value one A participant adds to side B. If the second term is large, the price turns negative. Markup: the gap between cost and the price the platform would charge a participant without network effects.

Platform Pricing

Code Trace: participation(pA, pB)

Call with the optimal prices pA = −5, pB = 50. Instead of solving for participation “by hand”, let the sides respond to each other.

Start: the platform is empty, nA = nB = 0.

Round 1: buyers respond to the current number of sellers: nA = 20 + 0.25·0 + 5 = 25.

Sellers respond to the new number of buyers: nB = 80 + 0.75·25 − 50 = 48.8. The buyer subsidy drew in sellers.

Round 2: buyers respond to the current number of sellers: nA = 20 + 0.25·48.8 + 5 = 37.2.

Sellers respond to the new number of buyers: nB = 80 + 0.75·37.2 − 50 = 57.9. The cross effect grows both sides together.

Round 3: buyers respond to the current number of sellers: nA = 20 + 0.25·57.9 + 5 = 39.5.

Sellers respond to the new number of buyers: nB = 80 + 0.75·39.5 − 50 = 59.6. The cross effect grows both sides together.

Round 4: nA = 39.9, nB = 59.9. The change shrinks by a factor of ≈ 0.25·0.75 ≈ 0.19 each round.

Round 5: nA = 40, nB = 60. The change shrinks by a factor of ≈ 0.25·0.75 ≈ 0.19 each round.

Round 6: nA = 40, nB = 60. The change shrinks by a factor of ≈ 0.25·0.75 ≈ 0.19 each round.

def participation(pA, pB, rounds=8):
  nA, nB = 0, 0
  for t in range(rounds):
    nA = 20 + 0.25 * nB - pA   # buyers
    nB = 80 + 0.75 * nA - pB   # sellers
  return nA, nB

Convergence condition: the product of the cross effects eA·eB = 0.1875 < 1. If it exceeded 1, participation would explode or collapse: the network effect is too strong (tipping).

The same model, two more parameters

Model: nA = baseA − sA·pA + eA·nB, nB = baseB − sB·pB + eB·nA.

Sponsored Search

Sponsored Search Auctions

  • Every search query triggers an auction among the advertisers bidding on that keyword.
  • m slots with click counts α1 > α2 > … from top to bottom (here 300, 200, 100 per 1000 impressions).
  • Advertiser i values a click at vi, bids bi per click, and in slot j pays pj per click.
  • The utility of the advertiser in slot j: αj·(vi − pj).
Example: A, B, C, D value a click at 10, 8, 5, 2 TL (and bid that). Three slots, four advertisers.

Sponsored Search

Two Payment Rules: GSP and VCG

GSP (generalized second price)

Rank by bid (b(k): the k-th highest bid). The advertiser in slot j pays the next bid down per click:

pj = b(j+1)

With one slot it equals Vickrey; with several slots truthfulness is lost.

VCG

Same ranking. Each advertiser pays the loss its presence imposes on others:

αj·pj = Σk≥j (αk − αk+1)·b(k+1)

Truthful bidding is a dominant strategy.

So under VCG, the advertiser in slot j pays the total click value lost by those below as each slides down one slot. αj·pj is the total payment, pj the per-click payment; αm+1 = 0.

Code Trace: gsp_vcg(bids, ctr)

m = 3 slots.

Sort bids from high to low and append m zeros so that a “next bid down” always exists.

Append a dummy (m+1)-th slot with 0 clicks to a: αm+1 = 0.

Zero the result arrays; vcg's extra last element is the recursion's starting value.

gsp[0] = a[0]·b[1] = 300·8 = 2400: slot 1 pays the next bid down, per click.

gsp[1] = a[1]·b[2] = 200·5 = 1000: slot 2 pays the next bid down, per click.

gsp[2] = a[2]·b[3] = 100·2 = 200: slot 3 pays the next bid down, per click.

extra = (100 − 0)·2 = 200: the click value lost by the advertiser below, stuck in slot 4 instead of 3.

vcg[2] = 0 + 200 = 200: losses imposed further down carry upward too.

extra = (200 − 100)·5 = 500: the click value lost by the advertiser below, stuck in slot 3 instead of 2.

vcg[1] = 200 + 500 = 700: losses imposed further down carry upward too.

extra = (300 − 200)·8 = 800: the click value lost by the advertiser below, stuck in slot 2 instead of 1.

vcg[0] = 700 + 800 = 1500: losses imposed further down carry upward too.

Result: GSP [2400, 1000, 200] (total 3600), VCG [1500, 700, 200] (total 2400).

def gsp_vcg(bids, ctr):
  m = len(ctr)
  b = sorted(bids, reverse=True) + [0]*m
  a = ctr + [0]     # dummy slot: 0 clicks
  gsp, vcg = [0]*m, [0]*(m + 1)
  for j in range(m):
    gsp[j] = a[j] * b[j+1]   # next bid
  for j in range(m-1, -1, -1):  # bottom up
    extra = (a[j] - a[j+1]) * b[j+1]
    vcg[j] = vcg[j+1] + extra
  return gsp, vcg[:m]

def profitable_dev(i, b, v, ctr, cands):
  old = gsp_utility(i, b, v, ctr)
  for x in cands:
    b2 = b[:]; b2[i] = x   # only i changes
    u = gsp_utility(i, b2, v, ctr)
    if u > old:
      return x, u   # profitable
  return None     # no deviation

gsp_utility(i, b, v, ctr): sort the bids; if i is in slot j, ctr[j]·(v[i] − the next bid down), and 0 if it gets no slot. Bids are assumed distinct.

Budgets and Online Allocation

  • Advertisers have daily budgets; searches arrive one by one, and the platform must assign each to an advertiser immediately, without knowing the future (an online decision).
  • Example: A and B each have a 100 TL budget. Both bid 1 TL on “running shoes”; only A bids 1 TL on “gym bag”. First 100 shoe searches arrive, then 100 bag searches.

Common mistake

“Always picking the highest bid is best.” With budgets, the greedy rule exhausts the flexible advertiser early; in the worst case it guarantees only 1/2 of the optimum.

Guarantee

When bids are small relative to budgets, the MSVV rule — weighting bids by remaining budget — earns at least 1 − 1/e ≈ 63% of the optimum in any search order (Mehta, Saberi, Vazirani and Vazirani, 2007).

Reputation and Competition

Reputation Systems and Information Asymmetry

  • Buyers don't know a seller's quality in advance: information asymmetry. Buyers then offer an average price, good sellers exit, and only bad products remain (Akerlof's “market for lemons”).
  • A reputation system does two jobs: selection (find good sellers) and discipline (punish bad behavior in the future, a repeated game).
Fake reviews

Bought positive reviews, or negative ones for a rival.

Fear of retaliation

With mutual ratings, bad experiences go unreported.

Grade inflation

If everyone has 4.8+, ratings stop discriminating.

Selective participation

Moderately satisfied users stay silent; new sellers get no reviews at all (cold start).

Design measures: require verified purchases, down-weight old reviews, account for the uncertainty of few reviews (Bayesian averages), and reveal both sides' ratings simultaneously in mutual rating.

Reputation and Competition

Platform Competition and Regulation

Competition
  • Winner takes all: through tipping, a single platform can capture most of the market.
  • Switching costs: friends, past ratings, data → keep users locked in.
  • Multi-homing: using several platforms at once (a driver on both Uber and a rival) weakens monopolization.
  • Examples: Hepsiburada – Trendyol, Uber – Lyft, Instagram – TikTok.
Ethics and regulation
  • Data privacy and security.
  • Monopolization risk and self-preferencing.
  • Transparency of ranking and recommendation algorithms.
  • Example: the EU Digital Markets Act (DMA) imposes obligations on large “gatekeeper” platforms.

Reputation and Competition

Recommender Systems, Lock-in and Privacy

  • Recommendation and ranking algorithms allocate visibility (who gets featured?): they are an allocation rule, and participants choose strategies accordingly.
The platform's objective

Maximizing short-term engagement or revenue can conflict with users' long-term interest (clickbait, infinite scroll).

Creators are players too

Creators and sellers compete for visibility and converge on whatever form the algorithm rewards (titles, tags): content can become uniform.

Loyalty programs

Loyalty points, paid memberships (e.g. fast shipping), gamification (badges, streaks): deliberately raise switching costs and reduce multi-homing.

Data and privacy

The price of a “free” service is data: more data means better recommendations but also risks of leaks and misuse. Users choose strategically how much to share.

The mechanism-design question is the same: are the platform's goals aligned with the interests of users and creators?

Reputation and Competition

Discussion: Growth, Behavior and Fairness

Why do some platforms grow fast while others collapse?

Critical mass and tipping: if expectations are above the threshold, feedback drives growth; below it, collapse. Ease of use, trust and early incentives help clear the threshold.

How do platforms shape behavior?

Bonuses and discounts boost specific behaviors (driver bonuses in busy areas); recommendation algorithms steer preferences; spam and fake-review policing limits manipulation.

Can a design be fair to everyone?

Perfect fairness is hard: preferences differ, network effects favor large groups, and players manipulate strategically. Incentives, rules and regulation can improve things.
