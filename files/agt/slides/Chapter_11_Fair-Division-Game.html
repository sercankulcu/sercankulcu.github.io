How to play

Divide indivisible goods between two players. Players pick in turn: Player 1 picks a free item, then Player 2 (the computer or a second person) picks, until every item is taken. Each item card shows the value each player assigns to it (P1 / P2; P = player).

The Bundle values table shows how each player values the two bundles: the bold diagonal is each player’s own utility. A player envies the other if the off-diagonal value in their row is larger than their own.

What to look at

  • Utility: the value each player assigns to their own bundle.
  • Envy-freeness: each player values their own bundle at least as much as the other’s bundle.
  • EF1 (envy-free up to one item): removing a single item from the envied bundle removes all envy. If both players always take their favourite remaining item, round-robin picking guarantees EF1 even when exact envy-freeness is impossible.
  • Equality: getting the same number of items; this does not mean equal value.
  • Efficiency: did the items go to the players who value them most? Welfare ÷ max compares total utility with the largest possible total (each item to the player who values it more).

Fair division separates equal shares from shares of equal value. Try a few new divisions and compare these criteria.

Game modes

  • Human vs. computer: you play Player 1. After each of your picks the computer gives Player 2 the remaining item it values most. Against a greedy opponent, this shows that strategic picking, and maximizing your immediate utility, may not produce an envy-free division.
  • Human vs. human: two people take turns on the same device. Useful for discussing bargaining, honest preferences, strategic blocking, and whether players pursue personal utility or an outcome both sides can accept.
  • Two computers: the division runs automatically; each computer picks the free item it values most. Watch the outcome of two greedy agents and check whether locally rational decisions create global fairness.

Levels

  • Example: 4 fixed goods: a fixed sample scenario: Cake 5/1, Book 4/9, Headphones 3/8, Ball 1/1 (P1 value / P2 value). Against the greedy computer, always taking your most valuable item gives 8 – 10; taking the Book first gives 9 – 9.
  • Level 1: 4 goods: a short, fully visible division; easy to compute utilities by hand and to understand the envy test.
  • Level 2: 6 goods: more options create larger opportunity costs. Think not only about the best item now, but about which valuable items will be left for later turns.
  • Level 3: Hidden values (6 goods): valuations are private. The player whose turn it is sees only their own values; the opponent’s values and utility are revealed when the division ends. It highlights uncertainty, preference elicitation, trust, and the limits of judging fairness when valuations are private.

Suggested learning path

Start with Human vs. computer, Level 1, continue with Human vs. human, Level 2, and finish with Level 3. This path moves from visible, simple choices to strategic interaction and incomplete information.
