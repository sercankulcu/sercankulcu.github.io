How to play

  1. Each round you have a fixed troop budget (Total troops).
  2. The whole budget is split among battlefields A, B, and C. Even and Random fill the fields for you; the total must equal the budget.
  3. The computer chooses its own allocation at the same time.
  4. Whoever has more troops on a battlefield wins it; on a tie each side gets ½ point. The side with more field points wins the round.
  5. Points accumulate over rounds. The table shows the latest rounds; watch for patterns in the bot's play and in your own.

Computer strategies

  • Random: a uniformly random split of the budget; ignores the history.
  • Greedy: concentrates on two random battlefields and gives up the third.
  • Predictive: computes your average allocation and best-responds to it: it narrowly outbids your two cheapest fields and puts the rest on the third.
  • Adaptive: tries many candidate allocations and, weighting recent rounds more, picks (with a little randomness) the one that would have done best against your last (up to 12) rounds (a fictitious-play-style learner).

Why predictability is dangerous

No single allocation is best against every opponent. Against a player who always concentrates the same way, a careful opponent can sacrifice one battlefield and win the other two cheaply. That is why randomization and opponent modeling are central to Blotto.

Link to game theory

Colonel Blotto is a simultaneous, zero-sum resource-allocation game with no pure Nash equilibrium: every pure allocation has a best response that beats it. The equilibria are mixed strategies. The game illustrates best responses, budget constraints, exploiting an opponent's weakness, and the tension between concentrating and spreading resources.

The Show the calculation box shows, after each round, how the bot found its allocation and how every allocation can be beaten.

The model

Both players have T troops (Total troops) and split them over three battlefields: a = (aA, aB, aC), integers summing to T. The choices are simultaneous. Whoever puts more troops on a field wins it:

points(a, b) = Σi [ai > bi] + ½·[ai = bi]    (points(a, b) + points(b, a) = 3)

Since 3 points are handed out every round, the game is constant-sum (a form of zero-sum game), and because the two sides are symmetric the value of the game is 1.5 points per round. The You and Bot boxes show accumulated field points, not the number of rounds won; if you stay above 1.5 per round you are playing better than the equilibrium value. The Winner column of the table is just the label of that round (2–1 and 3–0 both count as one round won).

Why is there no pure equilibrium?

The page finds the best response to an allocation with bestResponseTo: it is enough to outbid the two fields where the other side put the fewest troops, by one troop each; all remaining troops go to the third field. Those two fields are won, the third is sacrificed.

Example (T = 100, you play 34 · 33 · 33): your two smallest fields are B and C (33, 33). Outbidding them takes 34 + 34 = 68 troops; the remaining 32 go to A: 32 · 34 · 34. It wins B and C and loses A: 2–1. This best response has a best response of its own: no allocation is unbeatable.

The general reason: for T ≥ 6 the two smallest fields add up to at most 2T/3, and outbidding each by +1 still costs less than T; so every allocation is beaten by one that scores at least 2 points against it (the other side gets at most 1). If an allocation x were a pure equilibrium it would have to be a best response to itself; but x against itself scores 1.5, while the best response to x scores at least 2. Hence there is no pure equilibrium.

The equilibrium is a mixed strategy: players draw their allocations from a probability distribution, so no choice can be exploited. By a known result (Borel; Roberson 2006), with three fields and equal budgets the equilibrium distribution makes each field's share uniform on 0 to 2T/3. The page's "Random" level is not this equilibrium strategy: the shares that come out of two random cuts are skewed toward small values (the probability that a field's share exceeds 2T/3 is about 11%).

The bot's four levels

  • Random: allocRandom: two random cut points in [0, T], three pieces. It ignores the history.
  • Greedy: greedy: gives up a random field with a random number of troops between 0 and ⌊T/10⌋−1 (0–9 for T = 100) and splits the rest in half over the other two. It ignores the history. Example (T = 100): 4 troops on the abandoned field; the remaining 96 become 48 and 48: 48 · 48 · 4 (the order of the fields is random).
  • Predictive: random if the history is empty; otherwise it averages your past allocations field by field and applies bestResponseTo to the average. Example: if you played 50 · 30 · 20 and 40 · 40 · 20 in two rounds, the average is 45 · 35 · 20; the two smallest fields are C (20) and B (35); the needs are 21 and 36; the remaining 100 − 57 = 43 go to A: 43 · 36 · 21. It beats your average 2–1, but is wrong when you deviate from it. If you play 0 · 50 · 50 next, you win B and C (2–1).
  • Adaptive: random if the history is empty; otherwise it builds 6 + 40 candidates: one best response to each of your last 6 rounds and 40 random allocations. It scores each candidate against your last (up to 12) rounds: points(candidate, roundi) · (1 + i/n) are summed (i = 0 is the oldest, weight 1; the newest round gets a weight near 2) and a random number in 0–0.5 is added; the candidate with the highest total is chosen. Example: over a 3-round history a candidate that scores 2, 2, 1 gets a weighted score of 2·1 + 2·(4/3) + 1·(5/3) ≈ 6.33.

Predictive and Adaptive play randomly in the first round because there is no history. All of them are fictitious-play-style learners: they assume the player's past is a guide to the future (Chapter 13); they exploit someone who plays in a pattern, but lose their grip on someone who does not. This idea completes the mixed-strategy part of Chapter 3 · Nash Equilibrium.

What does the panel show?

When the box is ticked, after every round: (1) the bot's calculation for its level (the cut points; the breakdown of the average and the troops needed; for Adaptive the top 3 candidates and their scores), and (2) the best response to your and the bot's allocation of this round, with its score: you see that every allocation can be beaten. The panel only displays; it changes neither the bot's choice, nor its randomness, nor the points.

1. Points and the random split

/* points of two allocations: 1 per field won, ½ per tie */
function points(a, b) {
  let p = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < 3; i++) p += a[i] > b[i] ? 1 : a[i] === b[i] ? .5 : 0;
  return p;
}

/* random split: two cut points in [0, total], three pieces */
function allocRandom(total) {
  const x = Math.floor(Math.random() * (total + 1));
  const y = Math.floor(Math.random() * (total + 1));
  const lo = Math.min(x, y), hi = Math.max(x, y);
  return [lo, hi - lo, total - hi];
}

2. Best response

/* best response to 'target': narrowly outbid its two smallest fields */
function bestResponseTo(target, total) {
  const order = [0, 1, 2].sort((i, j) => target[i] - target[j]);   /* smallest to largest */
  const res = [0, 0, 0];
  const need0 = Math.floor(target[order[0]]) + 1;   /* to outbid the smallest field */
  const need1 = Math.floor(target[order[1]]) + 1;   /* to outbid the second smallest field */
  lastBR = { order: order, need0: need0, need1: need1, fit: need0 + need1 <= total };   /* record for the panel */
  if (need0 + need1 <= total) {
    res[order[0]] = need0;
    res[order[1]] = need1;
    res[order[2]] = total - need0 - need1;          /* everything left goes to the largest field */
  } else {                                          /* not enough for two fields */
    res[order[0]] = Math.min(need0, total);
    res[order[2]] = total - res[order[0]];
  }
  return res;
}

3. The bot's levels

function bot(total) {
  const level = aiSelect.value;
  if (level === 'Random' || !hist.length) {         /* no history: everyone is random (except Greedy) */
    if (level === 'Greedy') return greedy(total);
    botWhy = { kind: level === 'Random' ? 'random' : 'first' };   /* record for the panel */
    return allocRandom(total);
  }
  if (level === 'Greedy') return greedy(total);
  const past = hist.map(h => scaled(h.you, total));   /* your past allocations */
  if (level === 'Predictive') {
    const avg = [0, 1, 2].map(i => past.reduce((s, p) => s + p[i], 0) / past.length);
    const res = bestResponseTo(avg, total);          /* best response to your average */
    botWhy = { kind: 'predictive', n: past.length, avg: avg, br: lastBR };
    return res;
  }
  /* Adaptive: candidates = best responses to the last 6 rounds + 40 random splits */
  const cands = [];
  past.slice(-6).forEach(p => cands.push(bestResponseTo(p, total)));
  for (let k = 0; k < 40; k++) cands.push(allocRandom(total));
  let best = cands[0], bestVal = -1;
  const recent = past.slice(-12);
  const scored = [];
  cands.forEach((c, idx) => {
    /* weighted points against the last 12 rounds: weight 1 + i/n, recent rounds count more */
    const base = recent.reduce((s, p, i) => s + points(c, p) * (1 + i / recent.length), 0);
    const noise = Math.random() * .5;               /* a little randomness */
    const v = base + noise;
    scored.push({ c: c, base: base, noise: noise, v: v, idx: idx });
    if (v > bestVal) { bestVal = v; best = c; }
  });
  botWhy = { kind: 'adaptive', n: past.length, nBR: Math.min(6, past.length), recent: recent.length, scored: scored, best: best };
  return best;
}
function greedy(total) {
  const drop = Math.floor(Math.random() * 3);       /* the field to give up */
  const res = [0, 0, 0];
  const keep = [0, 1, 2].filter(i => i !== drop);
  const reserve = Math.floor(Math.random() * Math.floor(total * .1));   /* a few troops on the abandoned field */
  const half = Math.floor((total - reserve) / 2);
  res[keep[0]] = half;
  res[keep[1]] = total - reserve - half;
  res[drop] = reserve;
  botWhy = { kind: 'greedy', drop: drop, reserve: reserve };   /* record for the panel */
  return res;
}

The same idea in Python

In the style of the course's Python repos (short functions, a sample call and the expected output); this game has no separate file in the repo:

import math

def points(a, b):                        # points of two allocations
    return sum(1 if x > y else .5 if x == y else 0 for x, y in zip(a, b))

def best_response_to(target, total):     # the page's bestResponseTo
    order = sorted(range(3), key=lambda i: target[i])    # smallest to largest
    need0 = math.floor(target[order[0]]) + 1             # to outbid the smallest field
    need1 = math.floor(target[order[1]]) + 1
    res = [0, 0, 0]
    if need0 + need1 <= total:
        res[order[0]], res[order[1]] = need0, need1
        res[order[2]] = total - need0 - need1            # the rest to the largest field
    else:
        res[order[0]] = min(need0, total)
        res[order[2]] = total - res[order[0]]
    return res

def predictive(history, total):          # the 'Predictive' level
    n = len(history)
    avg = [sum(h[i] for h in history) / n for i in range(3)]
    return best_response_to(avg, total)

print(best_response_to([34, 33, 33], 100))             # [32, 34, 34]
print(predictive([[50, 30, 20], [40, 40, 20]], 100))   # [43, 36, 21]
print(points([32, 34, 34], [34, 33, 33]))              # 2: it wins two fields

In the panel look at where the candidates come from (best response to a round, or random) and when the noise decides the choice. What happens if you always play the same allocation? Discussion questions

  • Why is no allocation "the best"? Find an allocation that beats a balanced-looking one such as 34 · 33 · 33, and then one that beats that.
    Answer

    Every allocation has a best response that outbids two of its fields cheaply. 32 · 34 · 34 beats 34 · 33 · 33 (it takes B and C with 34 and gives up A). 32 · 34 · 34 is in turn beaten by, for example, 0 · 35 · 65: B (35 > 34) and C (65 > 34) are won and A is sacrificed. The beats-relation keeps going around and never stops; that is why there is no pure equilibrium. The equilibrium breaks this cycle by choosing at random which field to sacrifice.

  • Why does Predictive beat the even split every round, and what is this bot's weakness?
    Answer

    With the even split the average stays constant (34 · 33 · 33), so the bot plays its best response 32 · 34 · 34 and wins 2–1 every round from round 2 on. Its weakness is being predictable: its behaviour is an exact function of the average of your history. Someone who pulls the average in one direction and then deviates the other way defeats the bot's forecast (for example, playing 45 · 35 · 20 and then 0 · 50 · 50).

  • Why is the page's Random level not the equilibrium strategy?
    Answer

    The equilibrium strategy makes each field's share uniform on 0 to 2T/3. The shares that come out of two random cuts are piled up at small values instead: on average each field gets T/3, but the distribution is skewed (a field's share exceeds 2T/3 with probability about 11%). That difference can be exploited: even splits like 34 · 33 · 33 score more than 1.5 per round on average against Random (about 1.67 points per round in one simulation). Against the true equilibrium strategy no allocation scores above 1.5 on average.

  • Can you think of real-life resource-allocation problems like this game (election campaigns, advertising budgets, defence)? What does being predictable cost there?
    Answer

    In a presidential election, for example, the candidates split campaign resources over states, and each state is a winner-takes-all battlefield. Knowing where the opponent will invest lets you outbid it cheaply elsewhere; that is why campaigns hide their plans and sometimes make feints. The lesson is the same as in Blotto: a predictable allocation is an allocation that has a best response.
