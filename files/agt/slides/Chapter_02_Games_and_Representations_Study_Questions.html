Multiple Choice · Players and strategies

Question 1

F1 chooses one of three prices (90, 100, 120 TL). F2 sees this price and then quotes 100 or 120 TL. How many pure strategies does F2 have?

The correct answer is D. A strategy is a complete plan that says what to do at every decision node the player might reach. Since F2 can see any of F1's three prices, it has 3 decision nodes and 2 actions at each: 2 · 2 · 2 = 8 strategies. The answer 2 counts actions, and 6 multiplies nodes by actions (3 · 2); neither is the number of strategies.

Multiple Choice · Number of strategies

Question 2

F1 first chooses its price as High or Low. F2 sees F1's price and chooses one of three prices (High, Medium, Low). How many strategies does F2 have? (A strategy is a complete plan that says what to do in every possible situation.)

The correct answer is D. F2 has two decision points (F1 said High, F1 said Low) and 3 options at each: 3 · 3 = 9 strategies. 3 is only the number of actions; 6 = 2 · 3 and 5 = 2 + 3 are wrong ways of combining.

Multiple Choice · Rationality and utility

Question 3

A player voluntarily gives part of their winnings to someone else. According to game theory, which statement is correct?

The correct answer is B. A rational player maximizes their own utility function; this function can include not only money but also fairness, altruism, or concern for reputation (rational ≠ selfish). A choice that reduces money is rational if it raises utility, so A is wrong. Without binding agreements a game does not become cooperative (C); the player has options, and therefore a strategy (D).

Multiple Choice · Utility

Question 4

A player's utilities (A, B, C) = (3, 2, 0) are rewritten as (3, 1, 0); the preference order is unchanged. Which of the following definitely does not change?

The correct answer is B. Any order-preserving change keeps the ranking of pure strategies, dominance, and pure equilibria: these only ask "which is better?". The lottery decision, however, depends on the size of the numbers: with (3, 2, 0) the lottery gives 1.5 < 2 (choose B), with (3, 1, 0) it gives 1.5 > 1 (choose the lottery); so A can change. Expected utilities and the mixed probabilities found by indifference also depend on the cardinal numbers (C, D).

Multiple Choice · Normal form

Question 5

Which statement is correct for a normal-form game G = (N, (Si), (ui))?

The correct answer is B. In normal form, N = {1, …, n} is the set of players, Si is player i's strategy set, and ui is the payoff function from a profile to a real number. The payoff depends on everyone's choice, so A is wrong (this is exactly what strategic interaction means). The set of profiles is S1 × … × Sn, not a single Si (C); N lists the players (D).

Multiple Choice · Information set

Question 6

In a game tree, two decision nodes are joined by a dashed line. What does this mean?

The correct answer is A. A dashed line joins nodes in the same information set: the player to move cannot tell which of these nodes they are at, so they must choose the same action at all of them. Simultaneous moves (the tree form of price competition) and poker with hidden cards are modeled this way. A chance move (B) is shown by a separate Nature node, and the end of the game (C) by leaves where payoffs are written.

Multiple Choice · Zero-sum game

Question 7

Which of the following is a zero-sum game?

The correct answer is C. In Rock-Paper-Scissors the payoffs in every cell sum to 0 (+1 and −1, or 0 and 0): one player's gain is the other's loss. In the Prisoner's Dilemma the total is −2, −3, or −4, and in price competition it is 9, 10, or 12: since the total varies with the outcome, neither is zero-sum. The group assignment, in which everyone can win together, is also not zero-sum.

Multiple Choice · Zero-sum game

Question 8

Which of the following games is zero-sum (one player's gain equals the other's loss)?

The correct answer is C. In Matching Pennies the payoffs in every outcome are (+1, −1) or (−1, +1), so the total is always 0. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, price competition, and Stag Hunt there are outcomes in which both players gain or lose together.

Multiple Choice · Symmetry

Question 9

Which game is symmetric? The cells are given in the order (X,X), (X,Y), (Y,X), (Y,Y).

The correct answer is A. Symmetry requires u₁(a, b) = u₂(b, a) for all a, b. In A, u₁(X,X) = 3 = u₂(X,X), u₁(X,Y) = 0 = u₂(Y,X), u₁(Y,X) = 4 = u₂(X,Y), and u₁(Y,Y) = 1 = u₂(Y,Y): A is symmetric (a Prisoner's Dilemma). In B, u₁(X,X) = 3 ≠ u₂(X,X) = 2 (a Battle of the Sexes type). C is Matching Pennies: u₁(X,X) = 1 ≠ u₂(X,X) = −1. In D, u₁(X,Y) = 0 ≠ u₂(Y,X) = 1.

Multiple Choice · Horizon and discounting

Question 10

In an infinite-horizon game, what does the discount factor δ ∈ (0, 1) express?

The correct answer is D. In an infinite-horizon game the payoff of period t is multiplied by δt and the total is Σ δtut; δ is the weight of future payoffs relative to the present. For example, if δ = 0.9, 10 units one period from now are worth 9 units today. The larger δ is, the more the future matters, and threats, rewards, and reputation can support cooperation. δ is not a probability-1 end of the game (A); it has nothing to do with mixed-strategy probabilities (B) or with the sum of payoffs (C).

Multiple Choice · The Prisoner's Dilemma

Question 11

If Sam and John promise each other before the interrogation, "we will both stay silent", does the outcome change?

The correct answer is C. The Prisoner's Dilemma is a non-cooperative game: a promise cannot be enforced from outside, so it is not binding. Since the payoffs do not change, even if John stays silent, Sam gets 0 > −1 by betraying; after the promise Betray remains dominant and the equilibrium is (Betray, Betray). A promise does not make the game cooperative (B); that would take a binding contract. The game does have a Nash equilibrium (D is wrong).

Classic Question · Pareto optimality

Question 12: Nash Equilibrium and Pareto Optimality

Cells are (u₁, u₂).

row ╲ columnLeftMiddleRight
Up(3, 3)(0, 4)(2, 1)
Down(4, 0)(1, 1)(3, 2)

a) Does the row player have a dominant strategy? Find the pure Nash equilibrium.
b) Which of the six outcomes are Pareto optimal? Is the Nash equilibrium Pareto optimal?

Show answer

a) Down strictly dominates Up: 4 > 3, 1 > 0, 3 > 2. The row player always plays Down. Against Down, the column player's payoffs are 0, 1, 2 → Right.

The only pure Nash equilibrium is (Down, Right) = (3, 2).

b) An outcome is Pareto dominated if there is an outcome that gives everyone at least as much and at least one player more:

  • (3, 3) dominates (2, 1), (1, 1), and (3, 2).
  • No outcome dominates (0, 4) or (4, 0).

Pareto optimal: (3, 3), (0, 4), (4, 0). The equilibrium (3, 2) is dominated by (3, 3): it is not Pareto optimal. Stability and efficiency are separate questions.

Multiple Choice · The Prisoner's Dilemma and Pareto

Question 13

In the Prisoner's Dilemma both players choose Betray and the outcome is (−2, −2); had both stayed silent, it would have been (−1, −1). Which statement about this game's equilibrium is correct?

The correct answer is D. (Silent, Silent) is better than (Betray, Betray) for both players, so the equilibrium is not Pareto optimal. The only Nash equilibrium of the game is (Betray, Betray). The agreement is not binding; each player has an incentive to break their word.

Multiple Choice · Matching Pennies

Question 14

In Matching Pennies, it is known that the column player plays Heads 60% of the time. What should the row player (the Matcher) do?

The correct answer is A. If the column player plays Heads with probability 0.6, the Matcher's expected payoff is 0.6·1 + 0.4·(−1) = 0.2 with Heads and 0.6·(−1) + 0.4·1 = −0.2 with Tails. Since Heads is strictly better, the best response is to always play Heads. Imitating the opponent (B, 60% Heads) cuts the expected payoff to just 0.6·0.2 + 0.4·(−0.2) = 0.04; 1/2–1/2 (C) gives 0. Any predictable tendency gets exploited.

Multiple Choice · Strict and weak equilibrium

Question 15

In Free Money, (Heads, Heads) = (1, 1) and the other three cells are (0, 0). Which statement is correct?

The correct answer is B. A player who deviates from (Heads, Heads) drops from 1 to 0: a strict equilibrium. At (Tails, Tails), deviating takes a player from 0 to 0 again; since the deviation does not pay, this is also a Nash equilibrium, but a weak one. A Nash equilibrium only requires that a unilateral deviation not pay, not that the outcome be good; so A and C are wrong. There are two pure equilibria, so D is wrong too.

Multiple Choice · Stag Hunt

Question 16

In Stag Hunt, (Stag, Stag) = (4, 4), (Stag, Hare) = (0, 3), (Hare, Stag) = (3, 0), (Hare, Hare) = (3, 3). Which does a hunter choose who has no idea what the partner will do and wants to make the worst case as good as possible?

The correct answer is C. Worst cases: Stag → 0 (if the partner goes for the hare), Hare → 3 (in every case). The strategy that makes the worst case best (the safe strategy) is Hare. Stag is a best response only if one trusts the partner enough to go for the stag; the Pareto optimal outcome (A) requires trust. Stag is not part of the (Hare, Hare) equilibrium (D is wrong).

Multiple Choice · Pure Coordination

Question 17

In Pure Coordination, (Party, Party) = (10, 10), (Home, Home) = (5, 5), and different decisions give (0, 0). With no communication, most friends go to the party. Which best explains this choice?

The correct answer is D. There are two pure equilibria and both are self-enforcing: if the other will stay home, staying home (5 > 0) is best, so Party is not dominant (A) and (Home, Home) is an equilibrium (B). Players pick (Party, Party), which Pareto dominates and stands out from the other equilibrium, as Schelling's focal point. A mixed equilibrium exists too, but it is not the only one (C).

Multiple Choice · Two thirds of the average

Question 18

In the two-thirds game (0–90), what is needed to conclude that "nobody writes more than 40"?

The correct answer is B. Since the average can be at most 90, the target is at most 60; to rule out anything above 60, it is enough that I am rational (A gives only this much). To rule out anything above 40, I must use that everyone is rational and everyone knows it: if nobody exceeds 60, the average is ≤ 60 and the target is ≤ 40. Each new round needs one more level of knowledge; carried all the way, common knowledge pushes everyone to 0.

Multiple Choice · Zermelo's theorem

Question 20

What does Zermelo's theorem say about chess?

The correct answer is C. Zermelo (1913): in a finite, perfect-information, chance-free two-player game, either one side can force a win or both sides can force a draw. The theorem shows existence, not which case holds; since the chess tree is too large to solve, the answer is unknown (A is wrong). The rules (threefold repetition, the 50-move rule) make the game finite (B is wrong); chess has no chance (D is wrong).

Multiple Choice · Zermelo's theorem

Question 21

According to Zermelo (1913), which statement is correct for finite, perfect-information games such as chess?

The correct answer is B. Zermelo: in finite, perfect-information games, one side can force a win or both sides can force a draw. Which one holds depends on the game; the theorem does not say that the first player always wins. These games have no chance.

Multiple Choice · Minimax search

Question 22

The MIN children of the MAX root are: B = [5, 9], C = [3, 12, 7], D = [6, 8]. What is the value of the game and MAX's choice?

The correct answer is C. First the values of the MIN nodes are found: B = min(5, 9) = 5, C = min(3, 12, 7) = 3, D = min(6, 8) = 6. MAX at the root picks the largest of these: value 6, choice D. The 12 under C is misleading: if MAX goes to C, MIN picks 3 and never allows 12 (A and D fall into this trap).

Classic Question · Nim and Bouton's theorem

Question 23: The Winning Move in Nim

The Nim position is [4, 6, 9]; whoever takes the last token wins, and it is your move.

a) Compute the nim-sum (bitwise XOR) in binary. Is this a winning position?
b) Find the winning moves: how many tokens must be taken from which heap? Check the nim-sum of the new position.

Show answer

a) 4 = 0100, 6 = 0110, 9 = 1001.

  • 4 ⊕ 6 = 0100 ⊕ 0110 = 0010 = 2
  • 2 ⊕ 9 = 0010 ⊕ 1001 = 1011 = 11

The nim-sum is 11 ≠ 0: a winning position.

b) For each heap h, is h ⊕ 11 < h?

  • 4 ⊕ 11 = 15 > 4 ✗ · 6 ⊕ 11 = 13 > 6 ✗
  • 9 ⊕ 11 = 2 < 9 ✓ → take 7 tokens from the heap of 9 (9 → 2).

This is the only winning move (the leftmost 1 bit of the XOR appears only in 9). The new position [4, 6, 2]: 4 ⊕ 6 ⊕ 2 = 0.

Classic Question · Backward induction

Question 24: Don't Say 15

Two players take turns saying 1, 2, or 3 consecutive numbers, starting from 1. Whoever says 15 loses.

a) Using backward induction, find the "key numbers" that win the game for whoever says them.
b) Who should start, and what should they say in the first turn?
c) If the opponent says "3, 4, 5", what do you say?

Show answer

a) Whoever says 14 wins: the opponent is forced to say 15. To force 14, it is enough to say 10 (the opponent says 11–13, and you complete to 14). By the same logic the key numbers are 2, 6, 10, 14 (spaced 4 apart).

b) The starting player says "1, 2" and reaches 2: the first player wins. After that, if the opponent says k numbers, say 4 − k numbers.

c) The opponent stopped at 5: you say "6" (one number), and you are on a key number again.

The difference from Don't Say 21: there the key numbers are 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and the start of the game (0) is already a key position; so the starting player loses.

Multiple Choice · Combinatorial games

Question 25

How do we know that the first player in Hex has a winning strategy?

The correct answer is B. Hex has no draws, and an extra stone never hurts. If the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could place a random stone and "steal" that strategy, and would win; a contradiction. So the first player wins, but the argument does not say what the strategy is. Large boards cannot be searched (A; generalized Hex is PSPACE-complete), it has not been proved that starting at the center wins on every board (C), and the game has no chance (D).

Multiple Choice · Subgame perfect equilibrium

Question 26

Why is subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) stronger than an ordinary Nash equilibrium?

The correct answer is C. SPE demands a Nash equilibrium in every subgame of the game, even those never reached; so it eliminates non-credible threats such as "if you enter, I will fight". An ordinary Nash equilibrium tests only the decisions along the path actually reached and can allow such threats. SPE need not be Pareto optimal (A): in the six-node Centipede game the SPE outcome is (2, 1), yet (8, 6) is better for both. It is not unique in every game (B), and it is defined for sequential games (D).

Classic Question · Subgame perfect equilibrium

Question 27: Market Entry and the Threat

In one version of the market entry game, the entrant E chooses first. If E stays out, the outcome is (0, 5). If E enters, the incumbent I chooses: Fight → (−2, 1), Accommodate → (2, 2). Payoffs are (E, I).

a) Find the subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) by backward induction.
b) Write the normal form of the game and find all pure Nash equilibria. Which one rests on a non-credible threat?
c) What would the SPE be if Fight gave I 3 instead of 1?

Show answer

a) I's node: Fight 1, Accommodate 2 ⇒ Accommodate, value (2, 2). E: Stay Out 0, Enter 2 ⇒ Enter. SPE: (Enter, Accommodate), outcome (2, 2).

E ╲ IFightAccommodate
Stay Out(0, 5)(0, 5)
Enter(−2, 1)(2, 2)

b) (Enter, Accommodate) and (Stay Out, Fight) are Nash equilibria. In the second, since E does not enter, I is indifferent (5 = 5). But if E enters, fighting gives I 1 and accommodating gives 2: the threat "if you enter, I will fight" is not credible.

c) Fight 3 > Accommodate 2 ⇒ I fights; for E, Enter −2 < Stay Out 0. The SPE becomes (Stay Out, Fight): the threat is now credible.

Classic Question · Backward induction

Question 28: The Six-Node Centipede

Player 1 starts; at the nodes P1, P2, P1, P2, P1, P2 decide in turn. At each node they say Take or Pass. If Pass is said six times, the game ends with (8, 6).

Node123456
DecisionP1P2P1P2P1P2
Take(2, 1)(1, 3)(4, 2)(3, 5)(6, 4)(5, 7)

a) Find the SPE and the outcome by backward induction.
b) What if the final payoff were (8, 8) instead of (8, 6)?

Show answer

a) Start from the last node:

  • 6 (P2): Take 7 > Pass 6 ⇒ Take, value (5, 7)
  • 5 (P1): Take 6 > Pass 5 ⇒ Take, value (6, 4)
  • 4 (P2): Take 5 > Pass 4 ⇒ Take, value (3, 5)
  • 3 (P1): Take 4 > Pass 3 ⇒ Take, value (4, 2)
  • 2 (P2): Take 3 > Pass 2 ⇒ Take, value (1, 3)
  • 1 (P1): Take 2 > Pass 1 ⇒ Take, value (2, 1)

SPE: everyone Takes at every node; the game ends at the first move with (2, 1). Yet (8, 6) was better for both.

b) At node 6, Pass 8 > Take 7 for P2. Going backward, at every node the value of passing is better than taking (P1: 8 > 6, 4, 2; P2: 8 > 5, 3). Everyone passes, and the outcome is (8, 8).

Classic Question · Subgame perfect equilibrium

Question 29: The Ultimatum Game

Ayşe proposes to split 100 TL in 10 TL steps: x ∈ {0, 10, …, 100} to Bora. If Bora accepts, the payoffs are (100 − x, x); if he rejects, (0, 0). Bora cares only about money.

a) What is Bora's best response to each x?
b) If Bora accepts every positive offer, what are the subgame perfect equilibrium and the outcome?
c) If Bora says "I will reject anything below 30" and Ayşe believes it, what is the outcome? Is this threat credible in SPE?

Show answer

a) Accept for x ≥ 10 (x > 0); at x = 0, accepting and rejecting give him the same payoff (0).

b) Ayşe offers the smallest positive amount: x = 10, Bora accepts, and the outcome is (90, 10).

c) Ayşe offers 30 and the outcome is (70, 30); this is a Nash equilibrium. But when x = 10 or 20 is offered, rejecting gives Bora 0 (less than 10 and 20): the threat is not credible, and it is not an SPE. In experiments, however, small offers really are often rejected: players' utility includes concern for fairness.

Classic Question · Grim trigger strategy

Question 30: The δ Threshold in the Trigger Strategy

The Prisoner's Dilemma is repeated infinitely; each round continues with probability δ. Cells show (row, column) payoffs.

row ╲ columnSilentBetray
Silent(4, 4)(0, 6)
Betray(6, 0)(1, 1)

a) Both players play the grim trigger strategy. For cooperation to remain an equilibrium, what is the minimum δ?
b) What happens if δ = 0.3?
c) What would the threshold be if the payoff from Betray were 9 instead of 6?

Show answer

a) Continuing to stay silent: 4 every round ⇒ 4 + 4δ + 4δ² + … = 4/(1 − δ).

Deviating this round: 6; then the opponent betrays forever, 1 every round ⇒ 6 + δ/(1 − δ).

The condition is 4/(1 − δ) ≥ 6 + δ/(1 − δ). Multiply by (1 − δ): 4 ≥ 6(1 − δ) + δ = 6 − 5δ ⇒ δ ≥ 2/5.

b) 0.3 < 0.4: cooperation gives 4/0.7 ≈ 5.71, deviation gives 6 + 0.3/0.7 ≈ 6.43. Deviating pays; the trigger strategies are not an equilibrium: the players are not patient enough.

c) 4 ≥ 9(1 − δ) + δ = 9 − 8δ ⇒ δ ≥ 5/8. In general the threshold is (deviation − cooperation) / (deviation − punishment) = (9 − 4)/(9 − 1). The more tempting the deviation, the higher the threshold.

Multiple Choice · Price of Anarchy

Question 31

In a Prisoner's Dilemma the prison years are: both Silent → 1 year each; one Betrays, the other Silent → 0 and 5 years; both Betray → 3 years each. If total prison years are the cost, what is the Price of Anarchy?

The correct answer is A. Whatever the opponent does, Betray gives a lighter sentence (0 < 1, 3 < 5 years): the only equilibrium is (Betray, Betray), 6 years in total. The outcome with the smallest total is (Silent, Silent): 2 years. Price of Anarchy = cost of the worst equilibrium / cost of the best outcome = 6/2 = 3: selfishness triples the total sentence. 1/3 sets up the ratio upside down; 4 is not a ratio but a difference (6 − 2); 1.2 = 6/5 takes the (Betray, Silent) cell (total 5) instead of the best outcome.

Multiple Choice · Mechanism design

Question 32

A municipality will choose the site of a new park based on the home locations residents report; each resident wants the park to be close to them. What does it mean to see this problem as mechanism design?

The correct answer is D. Game theory predicts the outcome of a game whose rules are given (B); mechanism design does the opposite: it designs the rules and incentives that produce the desired outcome. The locations (private information) are hidden from the municipality, and residents may lie to pull the park toward themselves; A ignores this problem. C is also wrong: facility location is a "without money" setting; incentives come only from the rule, but the problem does not go away.
