Chapter 2 · Game

Mangala

← Chapter 2
P1 0 P2 0 Turn P1
Player 2 (Computer)pits 12 ← 7
Player 1 (You)pits 1 → 6

Reasoning panel is on: each time the computer picks a move, the pits it evaluated and their scores appear here.

    How to play

    1. Each player has six pits and a store on their own side (Player 1: bottom row and the store on the right; Player 2: top row and the store on the left). Every pit starts with 4 stones.
    2. Click a non-empty pit on your side. All its stones are picked up; the first stone is put back into the same pit and the rest are sown one by one counter-clockwise (a single stone simply moves to the next pit). The opponent's store is skipped.
    3. If the last stone lands in your own store, you get another turn.
    4. If the last stone makes the count in one of the opponent's pits even, you capture the stones in that pit.
    5. If the last stone lands in one of your own empty pits and the pit opposite has stones, you capture the stones of both pits.
    6. The game ends when one side runs out of stones; the player who emptied their side first also takes all the stones left on the opponent's side. The player with more stones in their store wins.

    The blue ring marks the pit where the last move started and the pit where its last stone landed.

    The menu at the top sets the computer's level: Easy, Medium or Hard; what they do is described in the How the AI decides tab. If you tick Show move reasoning, each time the computer picks a move, the pits it evaluated and their scores appear in the panel under the board.

    Planning a move

    Before choosing a pit, count where the last stone will land. The last stone matters most: it can win an extra turn, capture stones, or leave stones exposed to the opponent.

    Strategy tips

    • An extra turn can be worth more than a small capture because it keeps the initiative.
    • Watch the opponent's pits for positions that would let them capture many stones.
    • If the endgame does not favor you, do not empty your own side too quickly.

    Link to game theory

    Mangala is a sequential, deterministic game of perfect information. It highlights backward planning, tempo, position evaluation, and the difference between immediate gain and long-term value, making it a natural setting for minimax and alpha-beta search.

    The computer is always Player 2 (top row, store on the left) and scores are from its side: a large number favours the computer. To refresh minimax and alpha-beta: Chapter 2 · Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning.

    Three levels

    LevelWhat it doesLooks ahead?
    EasyPicks one of the non-empty pits at random.No
    MediumGreedy: for each pit it computes the store gain of the move, adds +2 for an extra turn, and picks the largest (with Math.random() * 0.1 added).No
    HardMinimax + alpha-beta: plays the move for each pit and searches 5 more moves below it with minimax (6 moves counting the root).Yes

    How the Hard level works

    1. For every legal pit i, aiMove plays the move with simulate, which returns the new pits P, the stores S and an extra-turn flag extra.
    2. For the resulting state it calls minimax(P, S, next player, 5, −∞, +∞): 5 more moves are searched below the root move.
    3. Math.random() (between 0 and 1) is added to the root value and the pit with the largest value is chosen. The true values are multiples of 10, so this number only breaks ties and never changes the order.
    4. Each root move is searched with the full window (−∞, +∞); alpha-beta only cuts below the root. So every score you see in the panel is exact (unlike Checkers, there is no “≤”). The panel also shows the total number of nodes counted during the search.

    How an extra turn is handled in the search tree

    If the last stone lands in the mover's own store, simulate returns extra = true and the same player moves again. The search treats that as follows:

    • After playing a move, minimax passes r.extra ? same player : other player as the player to move. So the tree can have two nodes of the same player in a row: MAX → MAX or MIN → MIN.
    • Whether a node is MAX or MIN is decided by the variable player, not by the depth; alpha and beta are simply passed down unchanged. That is why alpha-beta stays correct after an extra turn: sibling pruning works just as well on consecutive nodes of the same player.
    • The depth also drops by 1 on an extra turn. An extra turn counts as a move; the depth counter counts moves, not turns. As a result a chain of extra turns shortens the search measured in turns, but it never lengthens the tree for free: the number of leaves in the worst case stays bounded by 66.
    • In the real game, after an extra turn the computer calls aiMove again and searches from scratch from the new state; there is no pre-built two-move plan. The note “extra-turn chain: move 2 of this turn” in the panel header shows this.

    Terminal positions and evaluation

    • Terminal. If one side is empty (finalize), the remaining stones are collected and the value is (S1 − S0) × 100. This check comes before the depth check. There is no ply term as in Checkers: a gain reached in 2 moves scores the same as the same gain reached in 6.
    • Depth 0. If the game is not over, a leaf is scored as follows:

    value = 100 × (P2 store − P1 store) + 10 × (stones on P2's side − stones on P1's side)

    A stone in a store is a sure gain (100); a stone on your own side is not yet sure but will most likely end up in your store, and the opponent might capture it (10).

    Example. At the start, if Player 1 plays pit 3, the pits are [4,4,1,5,5,5 | 4,4,4,4,4,4]. The computer's move pit 12 puts the first stone back, then sows the other 3 into its store, pit 1 and pit 2: pits [5,5,1,5,5,5 | 4,4,4,4,4,1], stores 0 and 1. If the search stopped right here the value would be (1 − 0) × 100 + (21 − 26) × 10 = +50 (21 stones on P2's side, 26 on P1's). In the panel this move shows +520: that value comes from the leaves found at the end of the 6-move search.

    Why depth 6?

    • A player has at most 6 legal pits, so the branching factor is b ≤ 6. An unpruned tree reaches 66 = 46,656 leaves.
    • Node counts measured after the opening (sum over the six root moves): about 1,000 for 3 moves below the root, 16,700 for 5 (the game's setting), 203,000 for 7, 2.3 million for 9 and 25 million for 11. Every two extra moves multiply the count by 11–16.
    • The game's setting (5 moves below the root) finishes a move in about 13 ms on this computer; since the branching is small, full-window root searches are affordable. An 11-move search would take several seconds.

    1. Play the move: simulate

    A pure function: it does not change the board, it returns the new P, S and the extra-turn flag. The search looks at this flag to decide who moves next.

    function simulate(P0, S0, player, index) {
  const P = P0.slice();
  const S = S0.slice();
  let stones = P[index];
  P[index] = 0;
  // board positions: pits 0-5 -> 0-5, P1 store 6, pits 6-11 -> 7-12, P2 store 13
  let pos = index < 6 ? index : index + 1;
  if (stones > 1) {                     // the first stone goes back into the same pit
    P[index] = 1;
    stones--;
  }
  let lastStore = false;
  while (stones > 0) {
    pos = (pos + 1) % 14;
    if (pos === 6) {
      if (player !== 0) continue;       // the opponent's store is skipped
      S[0]++;
    } else if (pos === 13) {
      if (player !== 1) continue;
      S[1]++;
    } else {
      P[pos < 6 ? pos : pos - 1]++;
    }
    stones--;
    lastStore = stones === 0 && (pos === 6 || pos === 13);   // did the last stone hit the store?
  }
  const last = pos === 6 || pos === 13 ? -1 : (pos < 6 ? pos : pos - 1);
  let note = '';
  if (last >= 0) {
    if (own(last, player) && P[last] === 1 && P[opposite(last)] > 0) {
      note = '...';                     // empty pit: both pits' stones are captured (note text shortened)
      S[player] += P[opposite(last)] + 1;
      P[opposite(last)] = 0;
      P[last] = 0;
    } else if (!own(last, player) && P[last] % 2 === 0) {
      note = '...';                     // opponent's pit became even: captured (note text shortened)
      S[player] += P[last];
      P[last] = 0;
    }
  }
  if (lastStore) note = 'extra turn';
  return { P, S, extra: lastStore, last, note };
}

    2. End of game and search: finalize, minimax

    // If one side is empty the game is over; whoever emptied their side takes the other side's stones.
function finalize(P, S) {
  const a = P.slice(0, 6).reduce((x, y) => x + y, 0);   // stones on P1's side
  const b = P.slice(6).reduce((x, y) => x + y, 0);      // stones on P2's side
  if (a && b) return null;
  const s = S.slice();
  if (a === 0) s[0] += b;
  else s[1] += a;
  return { P: Array(12).fill(0), S: s };
}

function minimax(P, S, player, depth, alpha, beta) {
  nodes++;                                  // node counter (for the panel)
  const fin = finalize(P, S);
  if (fin) return (fin.S[1] - fin.S[0]) * 100;           // terminal: before the depth check
  if (depth === 0) {                        // leaf: store difference + stones-on-side difference
    const a = P.slice(0, 6).reduce((x, y) => x + y, 0);
    const b = P.slice(6).reduce((x, y) => x + y, 0);
    return (S[1] - S[0]) * 100 + (b - a) * 10;
  }
  const moves = legalMoves(P, player);
  if (player === 1) {                       // MAX: the computer (P2)
    let best = -Infinity;
    for (const m of moves) {
      const r = simulate(P, S, 1, m);
      // extra turn: the same player stays on move (1), otherwise it passes to the other (0)
      const v = minimax(r.P, r.S, r.extra ? 1 : 0, depth - 1, alpha, beta);
      if (v > best) best = v;
      if (best > alpha) alpha = best;
      if (alpha >= beta) break;             // cutoff
    }
    return best;
  }
  let best = Infinity;                      // MIN: the human (P1)
  for (const m of moves) {
    const r = simulate(P, S, 0, m);
    const v = minimax(r.P, r.S, r.extra ? 0 : 1, depth - 1, alpha, beta);
    if (v < best) best = v;
    if (best < beta) beta = best;
    if (alpha >= beta) break;               // cutoff
  }
  return best;
}

    The same idea in Python

    The same pattern as alphabeta in the course's Python examples; the only difference is that the player to move is chosen from the extra-turn flag. simulate does the same job as the JavaScript simulate above and returns (P, S, extra); legal lists the non-empty pits of a player.

    from math import inf

def finalize(P, S):
    a, b = sum(P[:6]), sum(P[6:])
    if a and b:
        return None
    S = S[:]
    if a == 0:
        S[0] += b
    else:
        S[1] += a
    return S

def minimax(P, S, player, d, a, b):
    done = finalize(P, S)
    if done:
        return (done[1] - done[0]) * 100          # terminal: before the depth check
    if d == 0:
        return (S[1] - S[0]) * 100 + (sum(P[6:]) - sum(P[:6])) * 10
    maxi = player == 1
    best = -inf if maxi else inf
    for m in legal(P, player):
        P2, S2, extra = simulate(P, S, player, m)
        nxt = player if extra else 1 - player     # extra turn: same player moves again
        v = minimax(P2, S2, nxt, d - 1, a, b)
        if maxi:
            best = max(best, v); a = max(a, best)
        else:
            best = min(best, v); b = min(b, best)
        if a >= b:
            break                                 # cutoff
    return best

def best_pit(P, S):
    best, best_v = None, -inf
    for m in legal(P, 1):
        P2, S2, extra = simulate(P, S, 1, m)
        v = minimax(P2, S2, 1 if extra else 0, 5, -inf, inf)
        if v > best_v:
            best, best_v = m, v
    return best

    Try it yourself

    1. Predict first, then look. Set the level to Hard and tick Show move reasoning. Each time the computer is about to move, decide which pit you think it will choose before looking at the panel. Then compare: is the chosen row the one that gains the most store stones in the “now” column? If not, where does the chosen move do better?
    2. Check the extra-turn rule. For the computer (Player 2), if pit number p holds 14 − p stones, the last stone lands in the store (in pit 12, 2 stones or 1). Find a row in the panel that says “extra turn”, count the stones in that pit on the board, and show that the rule holds. Then work out the same rule for Player 1: how many stones does pit number p need?
    3. Compare Medium and Hard. Start a new game and play pit 3 as your first move. Try it with Medium first, then with Hard. Which pit does Medium play, and which does Hard? In Hard's panel, how many points lower is the pit that gives the extra turn than the chosen one? Why can Medium's single “+2 for an extra turn” rule not see this gap?
    4. Hunt for the greedy trap. Play a few games on Hard and look for a moment where the row with the biggest store gain in the “now” column is not the chosen one. Write down the score of the chosen row and of the skipped row. Which reply from the opponent might explain why it was skipped? Play that reply on the board and see.

    Discussion questions

    • An extra turn breaks the alternation of MAX and MIN nodes in the search tree. How does the code handle this, and why does alpha-beta still work?
      Answer

      The type of a node is decided by the variable player, not by the depth; after a move the player to move is passed as r.extra ? same : other. When the same player moves twice in a row, the tree has MAX → MAX or MIN → MIN. The idea alpha-beta rests on is “can the value from this node still beat an option the parent already has?”; that question depends on who the node belongs to, not on the depth. Because alpha and beta are passed down unchanged, pruning stays correct.

    • In the terminal case the value has no depth-related term (like ply in Checkers). What flaw could this cause?
      Answer

      The computer sees winning in 2 moves and winning in 6 moves as equal, so in a won position it may drag the game out or delay the gain and risk something later. When losing, it cannot tell a slow loss from a fast one either. The Checkers page fixes this with ±10000 ∓ ply: a near win scores higher and a distant loss is less bad. Here the terminal value depends only on the store difference.

    • The evaluation weights the store difference by 100 and the difference of stones on the sides by 10. How would the computer play if the weights were equal, or reversed?
      Answer

      With equal weights, the computer would count a sure stone in the store the same as an unsure stone on its own side; it would even like moves that pile stones up on its side and never reach the store, although the opponent can take those with the even rule or an empty pit. With reversed weights it would prefer stones on the board to stones in the store and try to hoard stones without scoring. The 100-to-10 ratio expresses “value sure gain above uncertain gain, but do not count a stone on your own side as zero”.

    • The game's depth of 6 finishes a move in about 13 ms. Why don't we use 12?
      Answer

      Every two extra moves multiply the node count by 11–16: about 17 thousand nodes at depth 6 (≈ 13 ms), 200 thousand at 8, 2.3 million at 10 (≈ 0.35 s) and 25 million at 12 (≈ 4 s). Exponential growth means each added level costs far more than the one before for a smaller gain; on a slow phone a 12-move search could take tens of seconds for one move. Iterative deepening with a time limit (as on the Checkers page) lets the same code run at a suitable depth on any device; a transposition table would also avoid recomputing positions reached by different move orders.
