Multiple Choice · Basic concepts

Question 1

Which statement is true of the independent private values (IPV) model?

The correct answer is B. In the IPV model each bidder knows their own value (vi); this is their private information, and the bidders' values are drawn independently of one another. So other people's information does not change your value. A describes the common value model (e.g. an oil field), and C describes a model with interdependent values. D is wrong: if the seller knew the values there would be no need for an auction; they would simply sell to the highest value.

Multiple Choice · Four basic formats

Question 2

Which of the following pairings is strategically equivalent?

The correct answer is C. In a Dutch auction the price falls from a high level and the first bidder to say “accept” pays the current price; the bidder answers in advance the question “at what price will I accept?” and learns nothing useful as the process unfolds. This is the same as submitting a single secret bid in a first-price sealed-bid auction and paying your own bid if you win: Dutch ≡ first-price. A and D are wrong; the English auction (with private values) is equivalent to the second-price auction. B reverses the pairing: in a Dutch auction the winner pays the price at which they accepted, not the second bid.

Multiple Choice · Vickrey

Question 3

Why is truthful bidding a weakly dominant strategy in a Vickrey auction?

The correct answer is A. In Vickrey the bid only decides whether you win; if you win, the price you pay is the highest rival bid b* and does not depend on your own bid. So bidding b = v makes the right decision for every b*: if b* < v you win (a profit), and if b* > v you lose (avoiding a loss). No deviation does better, and some do worse: truthful bidding is weakly dominant. B is the first-price rule, and C is wrong (if b* > v a truthful bid loses); D is a trap, because dominance holds whatever the rivals do.

Multiple Choice · Competition and revenue

Question 4

Three bidders, values U[0, 1]. What is the expected revenue of the second-price auction?

The correct answer is D. In the second-price auction everyone bids truthfully and the seller receives the second-highest value. The expected second-largest of n values from U[0, 1] is (n − 1)/(n + 1); for n = 3 this is 2/4 = 1/2. A (1/3) is the answer for two bidders. C (3/4) is the expectation of the largest value, n/(n + 1), that is, the winner's value rather than the price paid; B (2/3) is the revenue for n = 5.

Multiple Choice · Revenue equivalence

Question 5

Which of the following can break the revenue equivalence result?

The correct answer is B. The revenue equivalence theorem assumes risk-neutral and symmetric bidders, independent private values, and the same allocation rule (the highest value wins). Risk-averse bidders shade less in the first-price auction to reduce the risk of losing, so the first-price auction brings more revenue than the second-price one and the equality fails. A, C, and D are assumptions of the theorem; satisfying them does not break the result, it makes it possible. Budget constraints, common values, and asymmetric information can also change the result.

Multiple Choice · Myerson reserve price

Question 6

Three bidders, values U[0, 1]. What is the reserve price in Myerson's revenue-maximizing auction?

The correct answer is C. By Myerson, the best auction gives the item to the bidder with the highest virtual value and does not sell if the virtual value is negative. For U[0, 1], ψ(v) = v − (1 − v)/1 = 2v − 1, and ψ(r) = 0 ⇒ r = 1/2. This threshold depends only on the value distribution, not on the number of bidders; having three bidders does not change the answer. A (0) is the second-price auction with no reserve; B (1/3) and D (2/3) come from thinking the reserve depends on n.

Multiple Choice · Winner's curse

Question 7

Which statement best explains the winner's curse?

The correct answer is A. With a common value the item's true value is the same for everyone, but each firm estimates it with error. Even if the estimates are right on average, the winner is usually the participant who overestimates the value the most; if they ignore this, they pay more than the true value. B is wrong: with private values the winner knows their own value, so they do not overpay. C is wrong (with a common value, profit can be negative even in the second-price auction), and D is wrong too: as rivals increase, the largest estimate rises and the curse grows.

Multiple Choice · Two-sided markets

Question 8

Buy bids 90, 80, 70, 55, 40; sell asks 35, 50, 60, 75, 85 (midpoint price 65). What happens if the marginal buyer bids 66 instead of 70?

The correct answer is A. With honest orders there are 3 trades, the range is [max(60, 55), min(70, 75)] = [60, 70], and the price is 65; the marginal buyer's utility is 70 − 65 = 5. If they bid 66, the trade still happens since 66 ≥ 60, but the range becomes [60, min(66, 75)] = [60, 66] and the price is 63. The buyer whose true value is 70 gets 70 − 63 = 7 > 5: lying pays, so the midpoint rule is not incentive compatible. B is wrong (the trade stays, since 66 ≥ 60); C and D contradict the calculation.

Multiple Choice · Why is Vickrey rarely used?

Question 9

Which is one of the reasons Vickrey auctions are rarely used in practice?

The correct answer is D. In Vickrey the winner pays the second-highest bid, and only the auctioneer sees that bid; the seller could inflate the price by inserting a fake “second bid”. So participants have to trust the reported second bid; counterintuitiveness and the difficulty of multi-item settings are other reasons. A is wrong: truthful bidding is weakly dominant in Vickrey. B contradicts revenue equivalence (revenue is the same on average), and C is wrong too: the winner is the highest bidder.

Classic Question · Competition and revenue

Question 13: Competition and Shading

In a first-price auction the values are U[0, 1] and the equilibrium bid is b = (n − 1)v/n. For a bidder with value v = 0.6, find the equilibrium bid, the amount of shading (v − b), the probability of winning, and the expected utility for n = 3 and n = 5.

Show answer

Rival j bids β·vj, and your bid is b = β·v (β = (n − 1)/n). b > β·vj holds only when vj < v. For each rival P(vj < v) = v, and since the rivals are independent, P(win) = vn−1.

Classic Question · Revenue equivalence

Question 14: Revenue Equivalence, n = 4

Four bidders' values are independent U[0, 1]. Using P(max < t) = t⁴ and E[max] = ∫01 (1 − P(max < t)) dt, find the expected highest value. If the expected second-highest value is (n − 1)/(n + 1), what are the expected revenues of the second-price and first-price auctions?

Show answer

E[max] = ∫01 (1 − t⁴) dt = 1 − 1/5 = 4/5.

Second-price: everyone is truthful, and the winner pays the second-highest value: E[revenue] = (4 − 1)/(4 + 1) = 3/5 = 0.6.

First-price: the equilibrium is b = 3v/4; the winner is the bidder with the highest value and pays 3/4 · vmax: E[revenue] = 3/4 · 4/5 = 3/5 = 0.6.

The two revenues are equal: the revenue equivalence theorem. For general n, n/(n + 1) · (n − 1)/n = (n − 1)/(n + 1).

Classic Question · Reserve price

Question 15: Myerson: U[0, 2] Values

Values are U[0, 2]: F(v) = v/2, f(v) = 1/2. (a) What is the virtual value ψ(v) = v − (1 − F(v))/f(v)? (b) What reserve price r maximizes revenue? (c) In a second-price auction with a reserve and two bidders, for the value pairs (0.7, 1.4), (0.4, 0.9), and (1.3, 1.6), who wins and what do they pay?

Show answer

(a) ψ(v) = v − (1 − v/2)/(1/2) = v − 2 + v = 2v − 2.

(b) r = ψ⁻¹(0): 2r − 2 = 0 ⇒ r = 1 (independent of the number of bidders).

Classic Question · Winner's curse

Question 16: The Winner's Curse

The true value of a field is V = 200 (nobody knows it). Four firms' estimates are 172, 196, 209, 223 (mean 200). (a) If everyone bids their estimate, what is the winner's profit in the first-price and second-price auctions? (b) If the estimation errors are U[−30, 30], what is the expected error of the largest estimate, 30(n − 1)/(n + 1)? What if n = 8?

Show answer

(a) The winner is the most optimistic firm (223). First-price: pays 223 → profit 200 − 223 = −23. Second-price: pays the second-highest bid, 209 → profit 200 − 209 = −9.

(b) n = 4: 30 · 3/5 = 18; bidding their estimate in the first-price auction, the expected profit is ≈ −18 (≈ −6 in the second-price auction). n = 8: 30 · 7/9 ≈ 23.3.

Even if the estimates are right on average, winning means “I am the one who overestimates the most.” A firm should bid below its estimate, and since the curse grows with the number of rivals, it should shade more.

Classic Question · Double auction

Question 17: Double Auction and McAfee

Buyers are willing to pay 95, 40, 85, 72, 33; sellers are willing to accept 68, 30, 90, 45, 99. (a) With the midpoint rule, how many trades occur, what are the price range and the price, and what are the buyer, seller, and total surplus? (b) What does the McAfee rule give?

Show answer

Sort: buy 95, 85, 72, 40, 33; sell 30, 45, 68, 90, 99. The pairs 95 ≥ 30, 85 ≥ 45, 72 ≥ 68 match; 40 < 90 → stop: k = 3.

(a) The range is [max(68, 40), min(72, 90)] = [68, 72] → price = 70. Buyer surplus 25 + 15 + 2 = 42, seller surplus 40 + 25 + 2 = 67; total 109 = 65 + 40 + 4 (the largest possible).

(b) p₀ = (40 + 90)/2 = 65. Is p₀ ∈ [68, 72]? No (65 < 68: the 3rd seller would lose). The weakest pair (72, 68) is dropped: 2 trades; buyers pay 72, sellers receive 68; 2 · 4 = 8 stays with the market maker. The price of DSIC: 4 units of surplus are lost.

Multiple Choice · All-pay auction

Question 18

Two bidders' values are independent U[0, 1]. In an all-pay auction the highest bid wins but both bidders pay their bids; in the symmetric equilibrium b(v) = v²/2. Which statement is true for a bidder with value 0.6?

The correct answer is B. b(0.6) = 0.36/2 = 0.18. In the all-pay auction this amount is paid in every case, so the expected payment is 0.18. In the first-price auction the bid is v/2 = 0.3 and the winning probability is 0.6: the expected payment is 0.3·0.6 = 0.18. Since the allocation rule is the same (the highest value wins), revenue equivalence holds; the seller's expected revenue is 1/3 in both formats (D is wrong). A is the first-price bid; in C the bidder always wins but pays 0.6: utility 0. With the equilibrium bid 0.18 and winning probability 0.6, the expected utility is 0.6·0.6 − 0.18 = 0.18 > 0.

Multiple Choice · Bayes–Nash bid in first-price

Question 19

Two bidders, values U[0, 1]. In the Bayes–Nash equilibrium of the first-price auction, what does a bidder with value 0.8 bid?

The correct answer is B. With two bidders and U[0, 1] values, the symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium of the first-price auction is b(v) = v/2: when the rival also bids v/2, a bid b wins with probability 2b, the expected utility is (v − b)·2b, and it is largest at b = v/2. A bidder with value 0.8 bids 0.4. A (0.8) is the truthful bid; in the first-price auction it leaves utility 0 on winning, and it is the dominant strategy of the second-price auction.

Classic Question · Myerson payment formula

Question 20: Myerson Payment, n = 3

Three bidders' values are independent U[0, 1]; the highest value wins (e.g. second-price with truthful bids). The expected payment is found by Myerson's formula: p(v) = v·x(v) − ∫0v x(z) dz.

a) What is the winning probability x(v) of a bidder with value v?
b) Find p(v) and p(0.6).
c) Find a bidder's expectation E[p(v)] and the seller's expected revenue.
d) What is the equilibrium bid b(v) = p(v)/x(v) in the first-price auction?

Show answer

a) Both rivals' values must be below v: x(v) = v².

b) ∫0v z² dz = v³/3 ⇒ p(v) = v³ − v³/3 = 2v³/3. p(0.6) = 2·0.216/3 = 0.144. Check: wins with probability 0.36, and when winning pays the larger of the two rivals' values, which averages (2/3)·0.6 = 0.4; 0.36·0.4 = 0.144 ✓.

c) E[p] = ∫01 2v³/3 dv = 2/12 = 1/6. Revenue 3·1/6 = 1/2 = (n − 1)/(n + 1) ✓.

d) b(v) = (2v³/3)/v² = 2v/3; e.g. b(0.6) = 0.4. The same allocation rule gives the same payment: revenue equivalence.

Multiple Choice · Myerson payment formula

Question 21

Two bidders, values U[0, 1], a second-price auction with reserve price r = 0.5. The allocation is x(v) = 0 (v < 0.5), x(v) = v (v ≥ 0.5). By p(v) = v·x(v) − ∫0v x(z) dz, what is the expected payment of a bidder with value 0.8?

The correct answer is D. v·x(v) = 0.8·0.8 = 0.64; ∫00.8 x = ∫0.50.8 z dz = (0.64 − 0.25)/2 = 0.195; p(0.8) = 0.64 − 0.195 = 0.445. Check: if the rival is below 0.5 (probability 0.5) they pay the reserve, and if the rival is between 0.5 and 0.8 they pay the rival's value: 0.25 + 0.195 = 0.445 ✓. A is the payment with no reserve (v²/2). B is the integral itself, that is, the bidder's utility, not the payment. C multiplies the winning probability by the reserve (0.8·0.5) and forgets the rival's values above the reserve.
