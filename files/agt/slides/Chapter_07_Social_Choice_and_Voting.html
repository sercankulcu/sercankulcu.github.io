Social Choice

Warm-up: The Condorcet Paradox

The rules

Each voter ranks the candidates. Every pair is decided by pairwise majority, and a candidate who beats every rival is the Condorcet winner.

Think about it
  • If every voter's ranking is consistent, must the group's preference be consistent too?
  • If you set the order of the votes, could you make any candidate you like win?

Social Choice

What Is Social Choice?

  • Social choice studies rules that turn individual preferences into a collective decision or a social ranking.
  • The goal is not one universal definition of fairness, but to understand which criteria a rule satisfies and what trade-offs it makes.
  • Applications: elections, committee and club decisions, choosing a class event, sharing common resources, online polls.
Mechanism design without money: no payments, only rankings.

Voting Rules

The Pairwise Majority Graph and Copeland

  • Each candidate is a node; an arrow x → y means a majority prefers x to y (label: vote counts).
  • The graph is a tournament: exactly one arrow between every pair (an odd number of voters rules out ties).
  • Condorcet winner = the node with no incoming arrows (D). Condorcet loser = the node with only incoming arrows (A).
  • Copeland rule: most outgoing arrows (wins) wins ⇒ D (3) ≻ B (2) ≻ C (1) ≻ A (0).
If every candidate has an incoming arrow, there is no Condorcet winner; a cycle through all the candidates causes exactly this.

Voting Rules

Code Trace: borda(ballots)

ballots = 4 groups (rows). The score dict starts empty and accumulates points per candidate.

Group 1: 4 voters, ranking ADBC. m = 4 candidates: position k earns m − 1 − k.

k = 0: A is 1st ⇒ p = 4 × (3 − 0) = 12; A total 12.

k = 1: D is 2nd ⇒ p = 4 × (3 − 1) = 8; D total 8.

k = 2: B is 3rd ⇒ p = 4 × (3 − 2) = 4; B total 4.

k = 3: C is 4th ⇒ p = 4 × (3 − 3) = 0; C total 0.

Group 2 (4 × CBDA): the inner loop runs 4 times; C +12, B +8, D +4, A +0.

Group 3 (3 × DBCA): the inner loop runs 4 times; D +9, B +6, C +3, A +0.

Group 4 (2 × ABCD): the inner loop runs 4 times; A +6, B +4, C +2, D +0.

max: the top scorer is B (22), whom no voter ranked first. On ties max returns the first it sees; real rules need a tie-breaking rule.

Complexity: n voter groups, m candidates ⇒ O(n·m). Counting is easy; the hard part is the rule's properties.

def borda(ballots):
  score = {}
  for count, order in ballots:
    m = len(order)
    for k, cand in enumerate(order):
      p = count * (m - 1 - k)
      score[cand] = score.get(cand, 0) + p
  winner = max(score, key=score.get)
  return winner, score

Voting Rules

Code Trace: irv(ballots, cands)

All candidates are in the race. Each round: count, check for a majority, otherwise drop the weakest.

Round 1 begins: a zero counter for each remaining candidate.

ADBC: top remaining candidate A ⇒ tally[A] += 4.

CBDA: top remaining candidate C ⇒ tally[C] += 4.

DBCA: top remaining candidate D ⇒ tally[D] += 3.

ABCD: top remaining candidate A ⇒ tally[A] += 2.

leader = A: 2 × 6 = 12 ≤ 13 ⇒ no majority.

B has the fewest votes (0) and is dropped; remaining = {A, C, D}. On ties min and max return the first they see; real rules need a tie-break.

Round 2 begins: a zero counter for each remaining candidate.

Round 2 count: nobody ranked the eliminated B first, so the counts are unchanged.

leader = A: 2 × 6 = 12 ≤ 13 ⇒ no majority.

D has the fewest (3) and is dropped; remaining = {A, C}.

Round 3 begins: a zero counter for each remaining candidate.

DBCA: top remaining candidate C ⇒ tally[C] += 3. (D is out, so these votes move to C.)

leader = C: 2 × 7 = 14 > 13 ⇒ return C.

def irv(ballots, cands):
  remaining = list(cands)
  while True:
    tally = {a: 0 for a in remaining}
    for count, order in ballots:
      top = next(a for a in order if a in remaining)
      tally[top] += count
    leader = max(tally, key=tally.get)
    if 2 * tally[leader] > sum(tally.values()):
      return leader
    loser = min(tally, key=tally.get)
    remaining.remove(loser)

Condorcet Paradox

Back to the Warm-up: The Condorcet Paradox

The theory's answer: no

The warm-up profile (A≻B≻C, B≻C≻A, C≻A≻B): A–B 2–1, B–C 2–1, C–A 2–1. The social preference is cyclic; there is no Condorcet winner, and the agenda setter picks the outcome.

AgendaFirst voteWinner
(A–B), then CAC
(B–C), then ABA
(A–C), then BCB
How often?

With three voters, three candidates and all rankings equally likely, 12 of the 63 = 216 profiles are cycles: 5.6%. As the number of voters grows, this rises to about 8.8% (Guilbaud, 1952).

In practice: Election and survey data show cycles even less often than this "random preferences" estimate; real preferences are not random but similar to one another (Regenwetter et al., 2006).

In a cycle the agenda setter decides the winner: each of the three possible agendas elects a different candidate, and the one saved for last has the edge.

Condorcet Paradox

The Kemeny Rule: The Most Consistent Ranking

  • Kemeny (1959): choose the social ranking that agrees most with the voters' pairwise preferences.
  • The score of a ranking: for every pair where x comes before y, add the number of voters who prefer x to y.
  • If a Condorcet winner exists, it goes first: on the main profile D ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A (44).
  • It also decides cycles: it breaks the weakest majority ⇒ A ≻ B ≻ C (17).
  • Finding the best ranking is NP-hard (Bartholdi, Tovey, Trick, 1989).
Applications: rank aggregation (Dwork et al., 2001): combining search engine results, ranking contestants and committee rankings.

Condorcet Paradox

Code Trace: kemeny(ballots, cands)

ballots = [(4, "ABC"), (3, "BCA"), (2, "CAB")] (the cyclic profile). No best ranking yet; score −1.

order = A ≻ B ≻ C: agreeing voters over the 3 pairs 6 + 4 + 7 = 17.

17 > previous best ⇒ best = A ≻ B ≻ C.

order = A ≻ C ≻ B: agreeing voters over the 3 pairs 4 + 6 + 2 = 12.

12 ≤ 17: best unchanged.

order = B ≻ A ≻ C: agreeing voters over the 3 pairs 3 + 7 + 4 = 14.

14 ≤ 17: best unchanged.

order = B ≻ C ≻ A: agreeing voters over the 3 pairs 7 + 3 + 5 = 15.

15 ≤ 17: best unchanged.

order = C ≻ A ≻ B: agreeing voters over the 3 pairs 5 + 2 + 6 = 13.

13 ≤ 17: best unchanged.

order = C ≻ B ≻ A: agreeing voters over the 3 pairs 2 + 5 + 3 = 10.

10 ≤ 17: best unchanged.

Result A ≻ B ≻ C (17): only the weakest majority, C ≻ A (5–4), is overridden. With m candidates, all m! rankings are tried.

from itertools import permutations
def kemeny(ballots, cands):
  best, best_score = None, -1
  for order in permutations(cands):
    score = 0
    for i, x in enumerate(order):
      for y in order[i + 1:]:
        score += sum(n for n, b in ballots
                     if b.index(x) < b.index(y))
    if score > best_score:
      best, best_score = order, score
  return best, best_score

Strategic Voting

How Much Damage Does Strategic Voting Do?

PluralityVote splitting: two similar candidates share votes and a third one wins. Tactical voting is common and simple.
BordaBurying a rival at the bottom is very effective; if everyone buries, the ranking drifts away from voters' true preferences.
Instant-runoffManipulation is harder to compute but possible, and the rule is not monotone. "IRV is immune to strategic voting" is a common myth.
Computational barrierFor some rules (e.g. IRV and a variant of Copeland) finding a profitable manipulation is NP-hard (Bartholdi et al., 1989–91).
The effect depends on the preference distribution: in close races strategic voting can change the winner. Transparent rules and clear information reduce some manipulation, but no general rule eliminates strategic behavior in every case.

Impossibility Theorems

Arrow's Impossibility Theorem: The Conditions

Reasonable-looking conditions for a rule that outputs a social ranking (a social welfare function):

Unrestricted domainEvery preference profile is allowed; the output is a complete, transitive ranking.
Pareto (unanimity)If everyone prefers x to y, so does society.
Independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA)The social order of x and y depends only on how voters rank x versus y.
Non-dictatorshipNo single voter's preference is society's preference in every profile.
Arrow (1951): With at least 3 candidates, no rule satisfies all four conditions. (With 2 candidates, simple majority satisfies them all: May's theorem.)

What Arrow's Theorem Means in Practice

What it saysNo collective decision rule meets every reasonable criterion at once; every rule gives up some condition.
What it does not sayIt does not say voting is meaningless, nor does it rule out any particular rule.
Who gives up what?Borda: IIA. Condorcet/majority ranking: transitivity (cycles). Dictatorship: fairness.
For institutionsDecide explicitly which criterion takes priority, and document it.
The way out is to relax a condition: restrict the preference domain (single-peaked preferences), or use intensity information (scores, money) instead of rankings.

Impossibility Theorems

The Gibbard–Satterthwaite Theorem

Theorem (Gibbard 1973, Satterthwaite 1975): With at least 3 candidates and every ranking allowed (unrestricted domain), every rule that picks a single winner in every profile, lets every candidate win (onto) and is strategy-proof (nobody gains by lying) is a dictatorship.
Evidence in our profileGroup 3 voting for C under plurality; burying B under Borda.
Relation to ArrowArrow is about rankings, G–S about a single winner; G–S can be derived from Arrow.
Mechanism designWithout money there is no general incentive-compatible rule. With money, VCG works.
  • The design goal is not to eliminate strategic behavior but to limit it: less information, computational hardness, a restricted preference domain.

Impossibility Theorems

May's Theorem: Simple Majority for Two Candidates

With two candidates (x, y), which rule is "fair"? May (1952) showed that only simple majority satisfies three conditions.

ConditionMeaningViolated by
AnonymityVoter names don't matter; permuting the ballots leaves the outcome unchangedDictatorship; weighted voting
NeutralityCandidate names don't matter; both candidates are treated symmetrically"The status quo stays without ⅔" (supermajority)
Positive responsivenessIf x is tied or winning and one voter switches to x, x wins outright"Always flip a coin"; "odd vote count wins"
With two candidates the debate over rules ends; the trouble (cycles, Arrow, G–S) starts with three or more candidates.

Impossibility Theorems

A Way Out: Single-Peaked Preferences

  • Preferences are single-peaked if candidates lie on an axis (left–right, budget size, location), each voter has a favorite position (peak), and utility falls as a candidate moves away from the peak.
  • Median voter theorem (Black, 1948): with an odd number of voters, the median voter's peak is the Condorcet winner; no cycles arise.
  • "Report your peak, pick the median" is strategy-proof: G–S no longer applies once the domain is restricted.
The median mechanism for facility location is an application of this idea.

Computational Social Choice

Computational Social Choice

Where computer science, economics and social choice meet: rules are judged not only by the criteria they satisfy but also by their computational cost.

Computing the winnerWith n voters and m candidates, plurality and Borda take O(nm) time, Copeland O(nm²). For Kemeny and Dodgson (the winner is the candidate needing the fewest adjacent swaps to become a Condorcet winner), finding the winner is NP-hard (Bartholdi, Tovey, Trick, 1989).
Computing manipulationsA single manipulator finds a profitable ballot under plurality and Borda with a polynomial-time greedy algorithm; under IRV the problem is NP-hard. Hardness is a “barrier”, but only in the worst case.
Preference elicitationA full ranking of m candidates carries log2(m!) bits (≈ 22 bits for m = 10). Asking voters questions step by step and collecting only enough to settle the winner cuts communication.
Fair division and judgment aggregationTwo more topics in this field.

Judgment Aggregation: The Doctrinal Paradox

A three-judge court: the defendant pays damages only if p (the contract was valid) and q (the contract was breached).

JudgepqVerdict: p ∧ q (p and q)
Judge 1yesyesyes
Judge 2yesnono
Judge 3noyesno
Majorityyes (2–1)yes (2–1)no (1–2)
  • Each judge is consistent, but voting premise by premise the majority says “p true, q true, p ∧ q false”: an inconsistent collective judgment (Kornhauser and Sager, 1986).
  • Decide by the premises and the defendant pays; decide by the conclusion and they don't: the outcome again depends on procedure.
  • List and Pettit (2002): no aggregation rule is anonymous, systematic and always consistent; Arrow's theorem for judgments.

Multiple Seats: Proportional Representation and D'Hondt

  • S seats instead of one winner; seat shares should track vote shares (proportional representation).
  • D'Hondt: divide each party's votes by 1, 2, 3, …; the S largest quotients win. Türkiye uses D'Hondt per district, plus a national threshold.
  • Example: 6 seats; A 42,000, B 31,000, C 15,000, D 12,000 votes → A 3, B 2, C 1, D 0.
  • A gets 50% of seats with 42% of votes; D gets none with 12%: D'Hondt favors large parties. Largest remainder (Hare): integer part of share × 6, leftovers to the largest fractions: A 2, B 2, C 1, D 1.

Summary

Which Voting Rule Can Be Called Fair?

  • Fairness depends on criteria such as representation, majority support, protection of minorities and strategy-proofness.
  • Plurality is simple and fast, but can split votes and elect a winner without majority support (A: 6/13).
  • Borda uses more preference information and favors the compromise candidate (B), but is open to strategic ranking.
  • Instant-runoff seeks majority support, but is neither monotone nor strategy-proof.
  • Condorcet methods respect pairwise majorities, but a Condorcet winner does not always exist and cycles need an extension rule.
Choose the rule by the context and the criteria that matter most: quick plurality may suit a club presidency, a ranked rule may be fairer for a class event.
