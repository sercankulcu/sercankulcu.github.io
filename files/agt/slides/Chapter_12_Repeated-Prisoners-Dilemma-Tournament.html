    How to play

    Each round both players choose Cooperate (C) or Defect (D). Payoffs: both C → 3/3, both D → 1/1, D against C → 5 for the defector and 0 for the cooperator. Pick a scenario, a level and (in a duel) the strategies, then press Run experiment.

    • Duel view: each small block is one round; green = cooperate, red = defect, an orange outline marks a move flipped by noise.
    • Cooperation is the share of all moves that were C.

    How this teaches game theory

    Repeated interaction changes incentives because today's move influences how opponents behave tomorrow. Repeated games can sustain cooperation through reciprocity, punishment and reputation; noise reveals whether a strategy can recover from accidental defections.

    Scenarios

    • Strategy Duel: compare two strategies directly.
    • Round-Robin Tournament: every strategy plays every other strategy (and a copy of itself); total payoff ranks the leaderboard.
    • Evolutionary Tournament: the average payoff of each pairing is used as fitness in replicator dynamics over 60 generations, starting from equal shares — linking repeated games to evolutionary selection.

    Modes

    • Human Strategy vs Computer: you choose Strategy A, the computer draws B at random on each run.
    • Human Selects Both Strategies: you set both A and B.
    • Computer Tournament: the computer draws both strategies at random.

    Strategies

    • Always Cooperate / Always Defect: baselines that expose exploitation and the short-run temptation to defect.
    • Tit-for-Tat: starts with C, then copies the opponent's previous move — reciprocity with immediate forgiveness.
    • Grim Trigger: cooperates until the opponent defects once, then defects forever.
    • Generous TFT: like Tit-for-Tat but forgives a defection with probability 1/4, which makes it more robust to noise.
    • Win-Stay Lose-Shift: repeats its move after a payoff of 3 or 5, switches after 0 or 1.
    • Random: C or D with probability 1/2.

    Levels

    • Level 1: 20 rounds — a short horizon to understand strategy logic.
    • Level 2: 100 rounds — reputation and retaliation matter more.
    • Level 3: 100 rounds where each move flips with probability 5%, testing forgiveness.

    What to observe: no strategy is universally best. Performance depends on the population of opponents, the horizon and whether mistakes occur.
