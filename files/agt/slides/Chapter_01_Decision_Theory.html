Expected Utility

Decision Making: Three Kinds

Decision making is the process of choosing among options by evaluating their consequences. Three kinds, by what drives the outcome:

KindOutcome driven byToolsExample
Individual, under riskNature / chance (probabilities known or estimated)Expected utility, Bayes, decision treesInvestment, insurance, medical tests
StrategicChoices of other rational agentsBest response, Nash equilibriumPrice competition, bargaining, sports
CollectiveJoint choice and interaction of a groupVoting, common goodsPolicy, corporate strategy
In an individual decision the decision maker plays only against nature (chance). Expected utility, Bayes' rule and decision trees are also the building blocks of strategic and collective decisions.

Expected Utility

Rational Choice: Expected Value or Expected Utility?

  • Lottery: a set of outcomes with probabilities. A = 900 TL with probability 50%, 0 TL with probability 50%.
  • Expected value: E[X] = Σ pᵢ xᵢ. E[A] = ½·900 + ½·0 = 450 TL > a sure B = 400 TL.
  • Expected utility (von Neumann–Morgenstern): E[u] = Σ pᵢ u(xᵢ). A rational decision maker maximizes expected utility.
  • With u(x) = √x: E[u(A)] = ½·30 + ½·0 = 15; u(B) = √400 = 20 ⇒ B is chosen.

Red: each criterion's choice. Expected value picks A, expected utility (√x) picks B.

Expected monetary value and expected utility may disagree; the gap is driven by the curvature of the utility function, i.e. the risk attitude.

St. Petersburg paradox: nobody pays much for a game with infinite expected value; this is why Daniel Bernoulli (1738) proposed utility (a similar idea: Cramer, 1728).

Expected Utility

Foundations of Expected Utility and the Allais Paradox

  • von Neumann–Morgenstern: if preferences satisfy four axioms, some u exists and the decision maker maximizes expected utility. A ≻ B: A preferred to B.
  • Completeness: any two options are comparable. Transitivity: A ≻ B and B ≻ C imply A ≻ C. Continuity: a middle option equals some mix of the extremes.
  • Independence: mixing the same component (same probability) into two lotteries keeps their ranking.
  • Allais (1953): most people pick A in choice 1, D in choice 2.
Option1 million5 million0
Choice 1A100%——
B89%10%1%
Choice 2C11%—89%
D—10%90%

Swap the common 89% "1 million" in A and B for 89% "0": C and D.

For every u, E[u(A)] − E[u(B)] = 0.11·u(1) − 0.10·u(5) − 0.01·u(0) = E[u(C)] − E[u(D)]. So A ≻ B forces C ≻ D; "A and D" fits no utility function. Overweighting certainty (the certainty effect) is a starting point of prospect theory.

Expected Utility

What Do Utility Numbers Measure? Uniqueness of the Scale

  • Utility is like a thermometer: its zero and its unit are arbitrary. Celsius and Fahrenheit rank temperatures the same way.
  • Uniqueness (vNM): a transformation v = a·u + b (a > 0) is called an affine transformation: it rescales and shifts, but changes no decision between lotteries. Other (non-affine) transformations can change decisions.
  • Link to game theory: multiplying all of a player's payoffs by 2 and adding 5 changes none of its decisions.
Utilitylottery Asure Bchoice
u = √x1520B
v = 10√x + 5155205B
w = (√x)² = x450400A

A: 900 TL with probability ½, 0 TL with probability ½; B: 400 TL for sure. The affine v preserves the decision; the increasing but non-affine w reverses it.

Common mistake: saying "u(B) − u(A) = 5 units of happiness" or "Ayşe's utility is 20 and Bora's 15, so Ayşe is happier". The size of differences and comparisons across people depend on the choice of scale; what is meaningful is one person's ranking of options.

Risk Attitudes

Certainty Equivalent and Risk Premium, Step by Step

Concave utility u(x) = √x: each extra lira adds less utility than the one before (diminishing marginal utility). Endpoints: u(0) = 0, u(900) = 30.

Chord: the line joining the two ends of the curve. Lottery A's expected utility is the chord's midpoint: E[u] = ½·0 + ½·30 = 15 (at x = 450).

The same 450 TL for sure gives u(450) ≈ 21.2 > 15. On a concave curve the chord lies below the curve, hence E[u(X)] < u(E[X]) (Jensen's inequality).

Certainty equivalent (CE): the sure amount with utility 15. √x = 15 ⇒ CE = 225 TL. This person would sell the lottery for 225 TL.

Risk premium = E[X] − CE = 450 − 225 = 225 TL. A sure 400 TL has utility 20 > 15 ⇒ a risk-averse person chooses B.

Risk Attitudes

Application: Why Does Insurance Exist?

  • Wealth 900 (thousand TL); a loss of 800 (fire) with probability 10%. Utility u(x) = √x.
  • Uninsured: E[u] = 0.9·√900 + 0.1·√100 = 27 + 1 = 28.
  • Certainty equivalent: 28² = 784. The person accepts a sure 784 in place of a risky 900.
  • Maximum premium = 900 − 784 = 116; the expected loss is only 0.1·800 = 80.

Insured: paying a premium ≤ 116 to shed the risk raises utility. Insurer: with many independent policies it is nearly risk-neutral; any premium above 80 is profit.

Any premium between 80 and 116 benefits both sides: risk moves to the party best able to bear it.

Risk Attitudes

Known Risk or Unknown Probabilities? The Ellsberg Urn

  • Under risk the probabilities are known (dice, roulette); under uncertainty they are not. People treat the two differently.
  • Urn: 90 balls; 30 are red, the other 60 are black or yellow, but how many are black is unknown. Drawing the winning colour pays 100 TL.
  • Typical choices: "red" in bet 1, "black or yellow" in bet 2; in both, the option with a known winning probability.
BetOptionwinning balls
1red30 (known)
blackb (0–60, unknown)
2red or yellow30 + (60 − b)
black or yellow60 (known)

b: number of black balls in the urn. Orange: the majority choice.

Choice 1 means 30 > b, choice 2 means 60 > 90 − b, i.e. b > 30: no probability explains both choices (Ellsberg, 1961). Avoiding options with unknown probabilities is called ambiguity aversion; when probabilities are entirely unknown, choosing to make the worst case as good as possible (maximin) is its extreme form.

Risk Attitudes

Unknown Probabilities: Decisions under Uncertainty

Probabilities are unknown (uncertainty). Rows are actions, columns are states; each criterion reflects a different degree of "pessimism".

Maximin (Wald, plan for the worst): row minima −120, −20, 0 → the largest is 0: No investment.

Maximax (optimistic): row maxima 200, 90, 0 → Big investment.

Laplace (treat states as equally likely): averages 43.3, 36.7, 0.0 → Big investment.

Best achievable result in each state: best = [200, 50, 0]. Regret = best − achieved.

Largest regret per row: Big (bad state, 0 − (−120)) = 120, Small (good state, 200 − 90) = 110, None (good state, 200) = 200.

Savage (minimax regret): the smallest maximum regret is 110 → Small investment.

return (2, 0, 0, 1). Returned indices (0: Big, 1: Small, 2: None): maximin → None, maximax and Laplace → Big, Savage → Small. Three different decisions from one table.

One more criterion, not in the code: Hurwicz sits between the extremes: α·(row max) + (1 − α)·(row min); with optimism coefficient α = 0.5: 40, 35, 0 → Big. If probabilities can be estimated, return to expected utility; if not, the choice of criterion reflects the decision maker's attitude.

def criteria(U):
    A, m = range(len(U)), len(U[0])
    mm = max(A, key=lambda i: min(U[i]))      # maximin
    mx = max(A, key=lambda i: max(U[i]))      # maximax
    lp = max(A, key=lambda i: sum(U[i]) / m)  # Laplace
    best = [max(r[j] for r in U) for j in range(m)]
    reg = [max(best[j] - r[j] for j in range(m))
           for r in U]            # max regret
    sv = reg.index(min(reg))                  # Savage
    return mm, mx, lp, sv

Bayesian Decisions

Medical Test: What Does a Positive Result Mean?

Picture a population of 10,000. Using natural frequencies (head counts) instead of probabilities makes Bayesian reasoning intuitive.

Prior: prevalence 1% ⇒ 100 ill, 9,900 healthy.

Sensitivity 90%: 90 of the 100 ill people test positive, 10 negative.

False positive rate 9%: 891 of the 9,900 healthy people also test positive! Healthy people are so numerous that this number is large.

Everyone testing positive: 90 + 891 = 981 people. Only 90 of them are actually ill.

Posterior: P(ill | +) = 90 / 981 = 9.2%. Even with 90% sensitivity, a positive result is mostly a false alarm (base-rate neglect).

Bayesian Decisions

Code Trace: bayes(p, sens, fp, tests)

Call: bayes(0.01, 0.9, 0.09, ["+", "+", "-"]). p starts at the prior (1%); the three test results are processed in turn.

Test 1 result: +. The current belief p = 0.0100 is this test's prior.

res == "+" ? → True: use the likelihoods of a positive result. Likelihood: the probability of this result given the disease status, e.g. P(+ | ill) = 0.9.

num = P(+|ill)·p = 0.9 × 0.0100 = 0.0090; den = num + P(+|healthy)·(1 − p) = 0.0981 (total probability of a positive).

p = num / den = 0.0917, printed. Despite the positive test, the probability of disease is still below 10%.

Test 2 result: +. The current belief p = 0.0917 is this test's prior.

res == "+" ? → True: use the likelihoods of a positive result.

num = P(+|ill)·p = 0.9 × 0.0917 = 0.0826; den = num + P(+|healthy)·(1 − p) = 0.1643 (total probability of a positive).

p = num / den = 0.5025, printed. Two positives, independent given the disease status, raised the probability to ~50%.

Test 3 result: -. The current belief p = 0.5025 is this test's prior.

res == "+" ? → False: use the likelihoods of a negative result.

num = P(−|ill)·p = 0.1 × 0.5025 = 0.0503; den = num + P(−|healthy)·(1 − p) = 0.91 × 0.4975 + num = 0.5030.

p = num / den = 0.0999, printed. The negative result brought the belief back down to 10%.

The loop ends; the function returns p = 0.0999. Each test uses the previous posterior as its prior (sequential updating).

def bayes(p, sens, fp, tests):
  for res in tests:
    if res == "+":
      num = sens * p
      den = num + fp * (1 - p)
    elif res == "-":
      num = (1 - sens) * p
      den = num + (1 - fp) * (1 - p)
    else: raise ValueError("result must be + or -")
    if den == 0: raise ValueError("impossible result")
    p = num / den
    print(res, round(p, 4))
  return p

Bayesian Decisions

Bayesian Decision Rule: Treat or Wait?

  • Update the prior with the evidence; then compute each action's expected loss under the posterior. Choose the action with the lowest expected loss.
  • Losses: needless treatment of a healthy patient 30, not treating an ill one 200.
  • E[loss | treat] = (1 − p)·30; E[loss | wait] = p·200. Treat if it loses less: (1 − p)·30 < p·200 ⇒ 30 < 230p ⇒ p > 30/230 = 3/23 ≈ 13.0%.

The decision depends not only on the probability but on the ratio of the losses: the costlier a missed disease, the lower the threshold.

In practice: base-rate neglect

In a classic study, physicians and medical students were asked about a test with prevalence 1/1000 and a 5% false positive rate: only 11 of 60 reached the right answer (about 2%); the most common answer was 95% (Casscells, Schoenberger and Graboys, 1978).

The game's "EU-optimal" counter counts not lucky outcomes but decisions that maximize expected utility given the information at hand: a good decision can lead to a bad outcome.

Bayesian Decisions

Bridge to Bayesian Games: Updating the Opponent's Type

  • With incomplete information the unknown "state" is the opponent's type: the belief is the prior, the move is the evidence.
  • Example (poker): the opponent is a bluffer with probability 20%, honest with 80%. A bluffer raises 90% of the time whatever the hand; an honest player raises only with a strong hand (30%).
  • The opponent raised: P(bluffer | raise) = 0.2·0.9 / (0.2·0.9 + 0.8·0.3) = 0.18 / 0.42 ≈ 0.429.
  • Calling earns +30 against a bluffer, −30 against an honest player: E[call] = 0.429·30 − 0.571·30 ≈ −4.3 < 0 ⇒ fold. After a second raise the posterior is 0.692.
ObservationP(bluffer)E[call]decision
prior only0.200−18.0fold
1 raise0.429−4.3fold
2 raises0.692+11.5call

Calling pays only if P(bluffer) > 0.5 (equal gain and loss).

Common mistake: confusing P(raise | bluffer) = 0.9 with P(bluffer | raise). Honest players are more common, so most raises (0.24 / 0.42 ≈ 57%) come from them. A bluff tries to steer this update.

Decision Trees and the Value of Information

Decision Tree: Product Launch and Market Research

  • A firm must decide whether to launch a new product. If the market is good (probability 2/5) it earns +500, if bad (3/5) −200 thousand TL; no launch gives 0.
  • It can first buy market research for 20 thousand TL. The report is imperfect: P(positive | good) = 0.8, P(positive | bad) = 0.1.
  • By Bayes: P(positive) = 0.4·0.8 + 0.6·0.1 = 0.38; P(good | positive) = 0.32/0.38 = 16/19; P(good | negative) = 0.08/0.62 = 4/31.
□ Decision nodeThe decision maker chooses → the largest value among the branches.
○ Chance nodeNature chooses → probability-weighted average.
LeafThe net result of a path.
A decision tree is a game tree in which nature also moves (chance nodes); it is solved from the leaves to the root by backward induction.

Decision Trees and the Value of Information

Backward Induction in a Decision Tree

Square = decision node, circle = chance node. Leaves show net payoffs (20 deducted if research is bought). The two root branches: Research and Direct (decide without research). Backward induction: expected value at chance nodes, maximum at decision nodes.

Launching without research: 2/5·500 + 3/5·(−200) = 200 − 120 = 80.

Decision: max(Launch 80, Skip 0) = 80 ⇒ Launch. The Skip branch is pruned.

After a positive report, P(Good | positive) = 0.32/0.38 = 16/19 (Bayes). Expected: 16/19·480 + 3/19·(−220) ≈ 369.5.

Decision: max(369.5, −20) ⇒ Launch after a positive report.

After a negative report, P(Good | negative) = 0.08/0.62 = 4/31. Expected: 4/31·480 + 27/31·(−220) ≈ −129.7.

Decision: max(−129.7, −20) = −20 ⇒ Skip after a negative report (only the research cost is lost).

Report chance node: 0.38·369.5 + 0.62·(−20) = 128.

Root: max(Research 128, Direct 80) ⇒ Research. Strategy: do research; launch if positive, skip if negative.

def value(n):
    if not isinstance(n, tuple):    # leaf
        return n
    kind, branches = n
    if kind == "C":                  # chance node
        return sum(p * value(c) for p, c in branches)
    return max(value(c) for name, c in branches)  # decision
