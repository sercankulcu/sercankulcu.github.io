Introduction

Why Do Algorithmic Decisions Matter?

  • Algorithms now make or steer high-stakes decisions: lending, hiring, medical diagnosis, social media feeds, criminal justice.
  • Unfair or unethical decisions harm individuals and erode public trust in institutions and technology.
  • The game-theory link: a decision rule is a mechanism, and its stakeholders (applicants, institutions, society) have conflicting interests.
Amazon hiring (2018)A model trained on past résumés penalized applications containing the word "women's" and was scrapped.
COMPAS (2016)A recidivism risk score and the debate over unequal error rates: with different base rates, fairness criteria conflict.
Credit scoringDoes the same score mean the same risk for everyone? Who never gets credit at all?

Introduction

Where Do Bias and Discrimination Come From?

Historical dataPast inequalities end up in the labels, and the model learns them as "truth" (gender bias in hiring).
UnderrepresentationIf a group is rare in the data, the model errs more on it (face recognition fails most on darker-skinned women: Gender Shades, 2018).
Measurement and proxiesMeasure a proxy ("health spending") instead of the target ("health need") and groups with less access look healthier.
Design and feedbackThe wrong objective, proxies for a protected attribute (zip code), and decisions that reshape future data.
Dropping the protected attribute (gender, ethnicity) is not enough: other variables carry it indirectly. That is why outcomes must be measured per group.

100 applicants per group; the model outputs two scores: 0.8 ("low risk") and 0.2 ("high risk")

The share of repayers (the base rate) is 50% in group A and 32% in group B. The model uses a 0.5 threshold.

Score 0.8 is approved: in A, 40 repay (TP) + 10 default (FP); in B, 16 TP + 4 FP.

Score 0.2 is rejected: in A, 10 would-be repayers are rejected (FN) and 40 are correctly rejected (TN); in B, 16 FN + 64 TN.

Both confusion matrices are ready. Accuracy is 80% in both groups ((40+40)/100 and (16+64)/100). Is the model "fair"? Let us check the criteria one by one.

Fairness Criteria

Demographic Parity Step by Step

Demographic parity: P(Ŷ=1 | A) = P(Ŷ=1 | B). Approved: 40 + 10 = 50 in A, 16 + 4 = 20 in B.

Selection rates: A = 50/100 = 0.50, B = 20/100 = 0.20. A gap of 0.30 ⇒ demographic parity fails.

Ratio: 0.20 / 0.50 = 0.40 < 0.8, below even the "four-fifths rule" used in US hiring. On its own this is not a legal finding of discrimination.

But the groups differ: 50 actual repayers in A, 32 in B. Forcing demographic parity means approving defaulters in B or rejecting repayers in A.

Fairness Criteria

Equalized Odds Step by Step

Equalized odds (Hardt et al., 2016): error rates must match conditional on the true outcome: TPRA = TPRB and FPRA = FPRB.

TPR (approval among repayers): A = 40/(40+10) = 0.80, B = 16/(16+16) = 0.50. Half of B's creditworthy applicants are rejected!

FPR (approval among defaulters): A = 10/(10+40) = 0.20, B = 4/(4+64) = 0.059.

TPR gap 0.30, FPR gap 0.141 ⇒ equalized odds fails. Requiring equal TPR alone is called equal opportunity.

Fairness Criteria

Predictive Parity Step by Step

Predictive parity: the repayment rate among approved applicants (PPV) must be equal across groups. This is the lender's point of view.

PPV: A = 40/(40+10) = 0.80, B = 16/(16+4) = 0.80 ⇒ predictive parity holds.

Rejections too (NPV: share of rejected who really default = TN/(TN + FN)): A = 40/50 = 0.80, B = 64/80 = 0.80. "Approve" and "reject" mean the same thing in both groups.

Code Trace: metrics(TP, FP, FN, TN)

The function is called with group A's matrix.

n = 40 + 10 + 10 + 40 = 100 applicants.

Selection rate = (40 + 10) / 100 = 0.5 (compared for demographic parity).

TPR = 40 / (40 + 10) = 0.8.

FPR = 10 / (10 + 40) = 0.2.

PPV = 40 / (40 + 10) = 0.8.

The four rates are returned as a Python tuple and assigned to A.

Same computation for B: selection 20/100, TPR 16/32, FPR 4/68 ≈ 0.059, PPV 16/20.

B = (0.2, 0.5, 0.0588…, 0.8).

No demographic parity: 0.5 ≠ 0.2.

i = 1, 2, 3: TPR differs ⇒ no equal opportunity; FPR differs too ⇒ no equalized odds; PPV equal ⇒ predictive parity holds.

def metrics(TP, FP, FN, TN):
  n = TP + FP + FN + TN
  sel = (TP + FP) / n     # demographic parity
  tpr = TP / (TP + FN)    # equalized odds
  fpr = FP / (FP + TN)
  ppv = TP / (TP + FP)    # predictive parity
  return sel, tpr, fpr, ppv

A = metrics(40, 10, 10, 40)
B = metrics(16, 4, 16, 64)
NAMES = ["sel", "TPR", "FPR", "PPV"]
for i, name in enumerate(NAMES):
  print(name, round(A[i], 3), round(B[i], 3))

Fairness Criteria

Beyond Group Fairness: Individual Fairness

distance between Ece and Nur d = 0.05; L·d = 0.1

  • Dwork et al. (2012): "treat similar people similarly": |f(x) − f(y)| ≤ L · d(x, y) (the Lipschitz condition). f(x) is person x's score; d(x, y) measures how different two people are; L is the largest allowed slope. Two people's scores may differ by at most L times their distance.
  • The red step model gives Ece and Nur, on either side of the threshold, 0.2 and 0.8: |Δf| = 0.6 > 0.1 ⇒ violation. The green model satisfies the condition.
  • Group criteria equalize averages: approving random people from B achieves demographic parity but is arbitrary for individuals.
The catch: who chooses the metric d? "Who counts as similar?" is itself a value judgment. Individual fairness does not remove the problem; it moves it into the choice of d.

A Real Case: The COMPAS Debate

ProPublica (2016), Broward County data: percentages

ProPublicaAmong defendants who did not reoffend, 44.9% of Black and 23.5% of white defendants were labeled "high risk": unequal FPR. For FNR (the share of reoffenders labeled “low risk”) the picture reverses.

Incompatibility

The Fairness–Accuracy Trade-off

  • Accuracy alone does not show on whom the errors fall: in our example accuracy is 80% in both groups, yet TPR is 0.8 vs 0.5.
  • A fairness constraint may raise total error; the real choice is which errors are acceptable (wrongful rejection or wrongful approval?).
Pre-processingReweight the data and fix underrepresentation.
In-processingAdd a fairness constraint to the training loss (giving up some accuracy).
Post-processingGroup-specific thresholds (Hardt et al.): may be legally contested.
The context of use, legal requirements, regulation and the views of affected groups should guide the choice of criterion. Example: credit scoring that treats low-income applicants fairly.

Fair Division

Fair Division: Cake Cutting

  • A divisible resource (the cake = the interval [0, 1]: time, land, compute) is to be shared among n people.
  • Each person has a valuation function vi: the whole cake is worth 1, but people value different parts differently.
  • The algorithm may only ask queries (the Robertson–Webb model): "what is this piece worth to you?" and "where does a piece of a given value end?". Xi is the piece person i receives; vi(Xi) is its value to person i.
ProportionalityEveryone gets at least 1/n by their own measure: vi(Xi) ≥ 1/n.
Envy-freenessNo one prefers someone else's piece to their own: vi(Xi) ≥ vi(Xj).
The game-theoretic view: people may be strategic. A good protocol gives every honest participant a guarantee, whatever the others do.

Fair Division

Cut and Choose Step by Step

Ali values the whole cake uniformly. Ayşe likes chocolate 3 times as much as vanilla: vAyşe(vanilla) = 1/4, vAyşe(chocolate) = 3/4.

1. Ali cuts into two pieces equal in his eyes ⇒ x = 1/2, each piece worth 1/2 to Ali.

2. Ayşe evaluates: left piece 1/4, right piece 3/4.

3. Ayşe chooses the more valuable right piece (chocolate).

Result: Ali 1/2 ≥ 1/2, Ayşe 3/4 ≥ 1/2 ⇒ proportional; Ali 1/2 = 1/2, Ayşe 3/4 > 1/4 ⇒ envy-free.

Swap roles: Ayşe cuts at x = 2/3 (1/2 – 1/2 in her eyes). Ali picks the left piece and gets 2/3; Ayşe gets 1/2. The chooser has the edge over the cutter.

Fair Division

Code Trace: A Cut Query by Bisection

A Robertson–Webb cut query: "where is x so that [0, x] is worth 1/2 to you?" Ayşe's valuation is increasing in x, so bisection finds it.

Query 1: mid = 0.5: all of the vanilla is worth 1/4 = 0.25 < 0.5 to Ayşe ⇒ lo = 0.5. The interval halves (width 1/2).

Query 2: v(0.75) = 0.625 ≥ 0.5 ⇒ the cut is here or to the left: hi = mid. The interval halves (width 1/4).

Query 3: v(0.625) = 0.4375 < 0.5 ⇒ the cut is further right: lo = mid. The interval halves (width 1/8).

Query 4: v(0.6875) = 0.5312 ≥ 0.5 ⇒ the cut is here or to the left: hi = mid. The interval halves (width 1/16).

Query 5: v(0.6562) = 0.4844 < 0.5 ⇒ the cut is further right: lo = mid. The interval halves (width 1/32).

Query 6: v(0.6719) = 0.5078 ≥ 0.5 ⇒ the cut is here or to the left: hi = mid. The interval halves (width 1/64).

Query 7: v(0.6641) = 0.4961 < 0.5 ⇒ the cut is further right: lo = mid. The interval halves (width 1/128).

Query 8: v(0.668) = 0.502 ≥ 0.5 ⇒ the cut is here or to the left: hi = mid. The interval halves (width 1/256).

After 8 queries the interval is 1/256 wide; its midpoint is 0.666. The true cut is 2/3: where Ayşe would cut if she were the cutter.

def v_ayse(x):          # value of [0, x]
  if x <= 0.5: return x / 2
  return 0.25 + 1.5 * (x - 0.5)
def cut_point(v, target, steps):
  lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0
  for _ in range(steps):
    mid = (lo + hi) / 2
    if v(mid) < target: lo = mid
    else: hi = mid
  return (lo + hi) / 2
print(round(cut_point(v_ayse, 0.5, 8), 3))

Fair Division

Why Is Cut and Choose Fair?

  • The cutter does not know which piece will be left, so the guarantee is the smaller piece. It peaks at the cutter's own halfway point (1/2): cutting honestly is the maximin strategy.
  • The chooser takes the piece that is larger in her eyes: worth ≥ 1/2 and no less than the other, wherever the cut is.
  • A cutter who knows the chooser's preferences can shift the cut and get more than 1/2, but the guarantee is 1/2.
With two people, proportionality ⇔ envy-freeness: if v(X1) ≥ 1/2, then v(X2) = 1 − v(X1) ≤ 1/2 ≤ v(X1). With three or more, envy-freeness is stronger.

Fair Division

Last Diminisher Step by Step

Three people; the cake has four quarters. Bars show each person's value per quarter (total 1). Ali is uniform; Ayşe likes the right, Cem the left.

Ali cuts a piece worth 1/3 to him: [0, 1/3].

Ayşe values it at 1/6, less than 1/3, so she leaves it alone ("passes").

To Cem the piece is worth 1/2 > 1/3: he trims it, moving the cut to 5/24 (exactly 1/3 for Cem).

The last trimmer (Cem) takes the piece and leaves. For those who did not trim, the piece is worth ≤ 1/3, so the rest of the cake is worth ≥ 2/3 to them.

The remaining [5/24, 1] is split between two by cut and choose: Ali cuts at 29/48, Ayşe picks the more valuable right piece (23/40).

Everyone got ≥ 1/3 by their own measure ⇒ proportional. O(n²) queries for n people. Not envy-free, though: Cem values Ali's piece at 9/20 (his own 1/3).

Moving Knife (Dubins–Spanier) and Even–Paz

The ruleA knife slides slowly from left to right across the cake. When the piece left of the knife is worth 1/n to someone, that person calls "stop!", takes the piece and leaves; the knife continues with the rest, and the last person takes what remains (Dubins and Spanier, 1961).

Back to the Game: Fair Division

Greedy picking is not a best response

The opening items (P1 / P2 value): Cake 5/1, Book 4/9, Headphones 3/8, Ball 1/1.

P1's planPick orderP1P2
always the bestCake, Book, Phones, Ball810
Book firstBook, Phones, Cake, Ball99

P2 values the Cake at 1, so it will still be there later. In round-robin, taking your favorite item every turn is not always the best strategy.

What about fairness?

Under the second plan, P2 values P1's bundle at 9 + 1 = 10 and its own at 9: envy. Remove the Book and 1 ≤ 9: the allocation is still EF1. The theory's guarantee: if both sides always pick their favorite, round-robin yields EF1.

In practice: data from Harvard Business School's draft-based course allocation showed students making exactly this kind of strategic pick, at a cost in welfare (Budish and Cantillon, 2012).

Even a fair protocol is open to strategic players: the EF1 guarantee holds when everyone picks their favorite on their turn.

Ethics and Governance

Strategic Design and Incentives

  • When an algorithm sets a rule, people respond to it: those being classified are players too (strategic classification).
  • Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure (click counts → clickbait).
  • Giving users the right incentives and preventing malicious manipulation (fake reviews, bot accounts) is part of the design.
Gameable featuresEasy-to-change features such as "number of cards" in a credit score move the score without changing the real risk.
Recommender systemsAn objective that rewards the user's long-term benefit, not engagement.
Unequal costsIf gaming costs differ across groups, strategic behavior creates new unfairness.
The mechanism design question applies here too: honest, rule-following behavior should be in each person's own interest.

Strategic Classification Step by Step

Threshold 0.6; filled circle = truly qualified (score ≥ 0.6). People can raise their score at a cost (e.g. number of cards). A's budget is 0.15, B's 0.03.

Gaming: anyone within their budget of the threshold moves up to it. 3 unqualified people from A and 1 from B get through (orange ring).

The institution responds by raising the threshold to 0.75 (accounting for A's budget). Now no unqualified person gets through.

But only 3 of B's 5 qualified people pass, given B's small budget (red); in A, 5 of 5. The defense against strategic behavior created a new inequality (Hu, Immorlica, Vaughan 2019).

Ethics and Governance

Discussion: Can Algorithms Be Neutral?

  • Algorithms are not independent of the values of the institutions that choose their objectives, data, criteria and thresholds.
  • Historical inequalities can seep into training data and target variables.
  • Different fairness criteria give conflicting verdicts on the same decision (the impossibility theorems).
  • After deployment a model can change behavior and create new feedback loops.
Core challengesFairness depends on context; trade-offs among accuracy, privacy and group fairness; cultural differences.
The responseInstead of claiming perfect neutrality: measure impact, document choices, provide an appeals process.

Code Trace: Self-Confirming Data

The two regions have equal true incident counts; past records show one more in region 0 (6 vs 5), perhaps by chance.

Day 1: the patrol goes to the region with more records ⇒ target = 0.

Incidents are recorded only where the patrol goes: records = [9, 5]. The 3 incidents in region 1 stay invisible. Share 0.64.

Day 2: the patrol goes to the region with more records ⇒ target = 0.

Incidents are recorded only where the patrol goes: records = [12, 5]. The 3 incidents in region 1 stay invisible. Share 0.71.

Day 3: the patrol goes to the region with more records ⇒ target = 0.

Incidents are recorded only where the patrol goes: records = [15, 5]. The 3 incidents in region 1 stay invisible. Share 0.75.

Day 4: the patrol goes to the region with more records ⇒ target = 0.

Share 0.55 → 0.78: the data "confirm" the model because they were collected by the model's own decisions (Ensign et al., 2018).

records = [6, 5]   # past records (region 0, 1)
actual = [3, 3]    # true daily incidents: equal!
for day in range(1, 5):
  target = 0 if records[0] >= records[1] else 1
  records[target] += actual[target]  # only there
  share = records[0] / sum(records)
  print(day, target, records, round(share, 2))
