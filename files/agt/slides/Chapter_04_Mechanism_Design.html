Basic Concepts

What Is Mechanism Design?

  • Game theory “in reverse”: the game is not given — we build it.
  • We design the rules (who gets what, who pays what) to reach a desired outcome.
  • The difficulty: players hold private information (values, preferences, costs) hidden from the designer.
  • Goal: reach the desired outcome even though players are selfish and strategic.
  • Examples: taxes, procurement, voting systems, school admissions, ad auctions.
2007 Nobel Prize in Economics: Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson, “for having laid the foundations of mechanism design theory”. In 2012 Roth and Shapley were honored for stable matching and market design.

Basic Concepts

Game Theory and Mechanism Design

Game theory: Rules → Outcome.   Mechanism design: Outcome → Rules. The designer cannot force the players; it only chooses the rules.

Basic Concepts

Incentives

People respond to incentives

If the rules make some behavior profitable, players move toward it.

A good mechanism

Rewards honesty and deters strategic lying: incentive compatibility.

The free-rider problem

When funding a public good, everyone wants to understate their share and benefit from what others pay.

Strategic voting

Voters may back a candidate with a real chance instead of their true favorite.

Misaligned incentives → misreported information → inefficient or unfair outcomes.

Direct Mechanisms

Social Choice Functions

  • Each player has a type θi ∈ Θi: a value, preference or cost. Types are private.
  • A set of possible outcomes O (e.g. who gets the item, which project is built).
  • A social choice function f : Θ1 × … × Θn → O assigns the “desired” outcome to each type profile.
  • The problem: the designer does not know θ; to implement f(θ), it must get the information from the players.

Efficient choice: f(θ) = the highest-value player → Cem. But what if everyone says “my value is 1000”?

The question of mechanism design: are there rules that implement f even when players pursue their own interests?

Direct Mechanisms

Direct Mechanisms

  • In a direct mechanism, participants report their private information (types): bi (the report; it may be a lie).
  • From the reports, the mechanism computes the outcome x(b) and payments p(b). Quasi-linear utility: ui = vi(x) − pi (vi: the valuation of a player of type θi; for a single item vi = θi).
  • In an indirect mechanism, a strategy is something else: raising a hand in an auction, bidding in turn…

Direct Mechanisms

The Revelation Principle

  • If an outcome can be implemented in equilibrium by some indirect mechanism, then under suitable conditions a direct mechanism in which truthful reporting is an equilibrium implements the same outcome.
  • Why? Lying amounts to playing an off-equilibrium strategy in M, and that was not profitable in equilibrium.
  • Example: in an English auction everyone stays in up to their value. If the designer says “write down your value and I'll stay in for you”, we get a direct mechanism (second price) where writing the truth is best.
  • The principle does not always make truth-telling a dominant strategy: the incentive condition depends on the equilibrium concept used (dominant strategy / Bayes–Nash).

Direct Mechanisms

Incentive Compatibility (IC) and Individual Rationality (IR)

Dominant-strategy incentive compatible (DSIC)

With true type θi, truth is at least as good as any lie bi, whatever others report:

ui(θi; θi, b−i) ≥ ui(θi; bi, b−i)
Bayesian incentive compatible (BIC)

If others are truthful, truth maximizes expected utility. DSIC ⇒ BIC.

Individual rationality (IR)

Participating is at least as good as staying out:

ui(θi; θi, b−i) ≥ 0   (truthful)

Value 10, rival bid 6. Paying your own bid (first price): truth yields 0, a bid of 7 yields 3: not IC. Paying the rival's bid (second price): any bid above 6 yields 4.

Direct Mechanisms

Back to the Game: The Ultimatum Game

Theory's answer

A token-only responder gets x by accepting, 0 by rejecting: accepting any x ≥ 0 is a best response (IR); at x = 0 rejecting is too. The proposer offers “take it or leave it”. Two pure SPE with integer offers: (10, 0) and (9, 1) (rejects at 0).

In practice

Since Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze (1982): the most common offer is 40–50%, and about half of offers below 20% are rejected (Camerer, 2003). People punish unfairness at their own cost.

Direct Mechanisms

Criteria for Mechanism Design

  • Incentive compatibility (IC): revealing the truth is the best option.
  • Individual rationality (IR): participating is no worse than staying out.
  • Efficiency: the outcome maximizes social welfare SW(x) = Σi vi(x) (under truthful reports).
  • Budget balance: total payments ≥ 0 (weak: no outside funding needed) or = 0 (strong: no surplus either).

Sample mechanisms: each gives up one criterion.

These criteria cannot always be met together: in bilateral trade with independent values and costs whose ranges overlap, efficiency + (Bayesian) IC + IR + budget balance are jointly impossible (Myerson–Satterthwaite, 1983). Design means choosing the trade-offs.

Direct Mechanisms

Implementation Theory: Is Truth-Telling the Only Equilibrium?

  • The revelation principle only says truthful reporting is an equilibrium; the same mechanism may have other, undesirable equilibria.
  • Rules can make a good equilibrium possible without guaranteeing that players coordinate on it. Mechanisms whose every equilibrium yields the desired outcome are studied by implementation theory (further reading: Maskin, 1999).
A bad equilibrium in Vickrey

Values: Ayşe 10, Bora 5. Ayşe bids 0, Bora bids 100: Bora wins and pays 0 (utility 5). To win, Ayşe would have to beat 100 and pay 100 (utility −90), so she does not deviate. Bora cannot gain by deviating either, so these bids form a Nash equilibrium — yet the item goes to Bora, the lower-value player.

Truthful bidding is a dominant strategy; this equilibrium rests on weakly dominated bids.

Lesson: under DSIC a player need not predict the opponent, which makes it more robust than designs relying only on Nash equilibrium. Still, it does not guarantee a unique equilibrium.

Direct Mechanisms

Single-Parameter Environments: Myerson's Lemma

  • Each player has one private number: a per-unit value vi. The mechanism chooses an allocation xi(b) and payment pi(b); utility is vi·xi − pi.
  • Myerson's lemma (1981): x can be used in a DSIC mechanism ⇔ x is monotone (xi does not decrease as the bid rises). The payment is then uniquely determined (with pi(0) = 0):

pi(b) = bi·xi(b) − ∫0bi xi(z, b−i) dz   = the shaded area left of the curve, between 0 and x(b) (the b × x(b) rectangle minus the area under the curve)

For 0/1 allocations, payment = critical bid (the lowest bid that still wins): exactly Vickrey's second price. If the payment is not the critical bid, IC fails: with threshold 50 and a fixed payment of 20, a player valued between 20 and 50 overbids past 50 and wins.

The VCG Mechanism

The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) Mechanism

  • Choose the outcome that maximizes social welfare under the reported values: x* = argmaxx Σi bi(x)  (bi(x): i's reported value for x).
  • Each player's payment is the externality their participation imposes on the others (Clarke pivot rule):
pi = maxx Σj≠i bj(x) − Σj≠i bj(x*)
= (others' best welfare without i) − (others' welfare with i)

Bora's externality in the public project example.

  • A report affects the payment only through x*; so, under suitable conditions, truthful reporting is a dominant strategy.
  • A player's utility = social welfare − (a term independent of them): everyone wants to maximize social welfare.

def vcg(v, A):
  N = list(v)            # players
  def w(a, S):           # welfare of S at a
    return sum(v[i][a] for i in S)
  x = max(A, key=lambda a: w(a, N))
  p = {}
  for i in N:
    D = [j for j in N if j != i]
    w_out = max(w(a, D) for a in A)  # i absent
    w_in = w(x, D)                   # i present
    p[i] = w_out - w_in
  return x, p

The VCG Mechanism

Properties and Limits of VCG

Strengths

  • DSIC: truthful reporting is a dominant strategy.
  • Efficient: the welfare-maximizing outcome is chosen.
  • IR with the Clarke pivot and nonnegative values; payments ≥ 0.
  • For a single item, VCG = the Vickrey (second-price) auction.

Limits

  • No strong budget balance: the example leaves a surplus of 25 (never a deficit; weak budget balance holds).
  • Computing the argmax can be NP-hard in combinatorial problems.
  • Vulnerable to collusion and fake identities; revenue can be low.
  • Asking players to reveal all their values raises privacy concerns.
High computational cost and budget-balance issues can limit real deployments. Sponsored search ads mostly use GSP instead of VCG.

Mechanisms Without Money

Mechanism Design Without Money

  • School admissions, dorm room swaps, voting: payments are either banned or inappropriate.
  • Without money, incentives cannot be fixed through payments; only the outcome rule can be designed.
  • Bad news for general preferences: with at least three alternatives, every onto, strategy-proof rule (lying never helps) is a dictatorship — the same single person's preference always decides (Gibbard–Satterthwaite).
  • Ways out: exploit preference structure (single-peaked → median) or change the goal (stable instead of “best” → Gale–Shapley; exchange → TTC).

Mechanisms Without Money

The Stable Matching Problem

  • Two sides: students and capstone projects (each project takes one student). Each side ranks the other.
  • A blocking pair: a student and a project who prefer each other to their current partners.
  • A matching with no blocking pair is stable: nobody wants to break away.

Mechanisms Without Money

Gale–Shapley: Deferred Acceptance (Proposal Rounds)

Start: everyone is free. Students propose in preference order; a project tentatively holds its best applicant and rejects the rest.

Round 1 proposals: A→X, B→X, C→Y (each free student to their best project that has not yet rejected them).

Round 1 decisions — X: holds B, rejects A (B, ranked 1st, is better); Y: accepts C (tentatively).

Round 2 proposals: A→Y (each free student to their best project that has not yet rejected them).

Round 2 decisions — Y: holds A, rejects C (A, ranked 1st, is better).

Round 3 proposals: C→X (each free student to their best project that has not yet rejected them).

Round 3 decisions — X: holds B, rejects C (B, ranked 1st, is better).

Round 4 proposals: C→Z (each free student to their best project that has not yet rejected them).

Round 4 decisions — Z: accepts C (tentatively).

Everyone is matched → stop. A–Y, B–X, C–Z. Check: A wants X, but X prefers B to A; neither X nor Y wants C → no blocking pair, stable.

Mechanisms Without Money

Code Trace: gale_shapley(stu, proj)

Everyone free, projects empty. proj[p][s]: p's rank for s (0 = best).

Each student's next choice is index 0 (their favorite project).

Pop A from the queue; A proposes to its next choice, X.

X is empty (r = None) → A is tentatively accepted.

Pop B from the queue; B proposes to its next choice, X.

X was holding A; X prefers B (rank 0 < 1) → B accepted, A rejoins the queue.

Pop C from the queue; C proposes to its next choice, Y.

Y is empty (r = None) → C is tentatively accepted.

Pop A from the queue; A proposes to its next choice, Y.

Y was holding C; Y prefers A (rank 0 < 1) → A accepted, C rejoins the queue.

Pop C from the queue; C proposes to its next choice, X.

X prefers the B it holds to C → C is rejected and rejoins the queue.

Pop C from the queue; C proposes to its next choice, Z.

Z is empty (r = None) → C is tentatively accepted.

The queue is empty after 6 proposals. Same result as the simultaneous rounds: A–Y, B–X, C–Z.

def gale_shapley(stu, proj):
  free, match = list(stu), {}
  nxt = {s: 0 for s in stu}
  while free:
    s = free.pop(0)
    p = stu[s][nxt[s]]         # next choice
    nxt[s] += 1
    r = match.get(p)           # p's holder
    if r is None or proj[p][s] < proj[p][r]:
      match[p] = s             # accept
      if r is not None: free.append(r)
    else: free.append(s)      # reject
  return match

Mechanisms Without Money

Properties of Gale–Shapley

Always stable

The algorithm terminates with a stable matching (Gale and Shapley, 1962). At most n² proposals → O(n²) (with a constant-time queue, e.g. deque).

Proposer-optimal

Proposers get their best partner among all stable matchings; the receiving side gets its worst.

DSIC for proposers

For the proposing side, reporting the true ranking is a dominant strategy. The receiving side can sometimes gain by lying.

Applications

The US medical residency match (NRMP), school choice in Boston and New York. 2012 Nobel: Roth and Shapley.

Our example has a single stable matching (checking all 3! = 6 matchings); even if the projects proposed, the result would be A–Y, B–X, C–Z.

Mechanisms Without Money

The Receiving Side Can Gain by Lying

A new example. Students propose. The projects' true preferences: X: B ≻ A ≻ C; Y and Z: A ≻ B ≻ C.

With truthful reports the proposals are A→X, B→Y, C→X, C→Y, C→Z → A–X, B–Y, C–Z. X gets A, its 2nd choice.

X lies: it reports B ≻ C ≻ A (pushing A to the bottom).

Proposals A→X, B→Y, C→X, A→Y, B→X, C→Y, C→Z: rejecting A sets off a chain that brings B to X → B–X. X got its true first choice: the lie paid off.

This example has two stable matchings (checking all 3! = 6): the student-optimal A–X, B–Y, C–Z and the project-optimal A–Y, B–X, C–Z. X's lie moves the outcome to the project-optimal one. No stable mechanism is DSIC for both sides (Roth, 1982).

Mechanisms Without Money

Why Is the Median Strategy-proof?

1) xᵢ < m (B = 5, median 9): reports r ≤ 9 leave the median at 9 (distance 4); r > 9 pushes the median right, away from B. No r gets below 4.

2) xᵢ > m (D = 14): the symmetric case. r ≥ 9 leaves the median unchanged (distance 5); r < 9 pulls it left, away from D.

3) xᵢ = m (C = 9): the facility sits exactly at C, distance 0; any lie can only move the median away.

Conclusion: in every case truthful reporting is a dominant strategy → the median mechanism is strategy-proof (Moulin, 1980). The same idea works for voting with single-peaked preferences (Black).

Mechanisms Without Money

Serial Dictatorship and the Boston Mechanism

Serial dictatorship

In turn, each takes their favorite of what remains. No one affects earlier picks → DSIC, Pareto optimal. Random order: random serial dictatorship (RSD).

Boston mechanism (immediate acceptance)

Each round, students apply to their next choice; schools fill free seats by priority, and seats are never taken back. Losing a first choice may mean the second is full.

Preferences A: X ≻ Y ≻ Z, B: X ≻ Y ≻ Z, C: Y ≻ X ≻ Z. Priorities at X and Z: A, B, C; at Y: B, A, C. One seat each.

Mechanisms Without Money

Dorm Room Exchange: Top Trading Cycles (TTC)

  • Each student owns a dorm room, there is no money, and each ranks the others' rooms (Shapley and Scarf, 1974; the algorithm is credited to Gale).
  • TTC: everyone points to the owner of their favorite remaining room. A finite graph has at least one cycle; those in it trade and leave. Repeat.
  • Reporting true preferences is a dominant strategy (Roth, 1982); the outcome is Pareto optimal and in the core: no group can do better on its own (Roth and Postlewaite, 1977).
  • The same rule fits duty or shift swaps; in the real world it is used in kidney exchange (Roth, Sönmez and Ünver, 2004).

Mechanisms Without Money

Code Trace: ttc(pref)

Everyone is still in the list; nobody has traded yet.

Round 1: each remaining person points to the owner of their favorite remaining room (arrows).

Add A to the path; its arrow points to C.

Add C to the path; its arrow points to B.

Add B to the path; its arrow points to A, already on the path → cycle found.

Cycle: A → C → B → A. Everyone on it gets the room they point to.

Trade: A ← C's room, C ← B's room, B ← A's room. They leave.

Round 2: each remaining person points to the owner of their favorite remaining room (arrows).

Add D to the path; its arrow points to E.

Add E to the path; its arrow points to D, already on the path → cycle found.

Cycle: D → E → D. Everyone on it gets the room they point to.

Trade: D ← E's room, E ← D's room. They leave.

Done: A, B, C traded in a 3-cycle, D and E in a 2-cycle. Trying every false list for every person never yields a better room (DSIC); the outcome is Pareto optimal.

def ttc(pref):
  left, assign = list(pref), {}
  while left:
    g = {i: next(j for j in pref[i]
             if j in left) for i in left}
    i, path = left[0], []
    while i not in path:         # follow arrows
      path.append(i); i = g[i]
    cycle = path[path.index(i):]
    for k in cycle:              # trade
      assign[k] = g[k]; left.remove(k)
  return assign

Bayesian Games

Incomplete Information: Bayesian Games

  • So far the designer did not know the types; often the players do not know each other's types either.
  • A Bayesian game: players, type sets Θi, a common prior π over types, payoffs ui(a, θ).
  • First, chance (Nature) draws each player's type; each player sees only their own (the Harsanyi transformation).
  • A strategy is now a rule: which action to take for each type.
  • Bayes–Nash equilibrium: every player, of every type, maximizes their expected payoff against the others' strategies.

Example game: P2's type is S (wants to meet) or A (wants to avoid).

Link to mechanisms: the Bayesian revelation principle → any outcome implementable in Bayes–Nash equilibrium can also be implemented by a BIC direct mechanism.

Bayesian Games

Mechanisms Through a Bayesian Lens

Example: first price

A truthful bid leaves zero utility when it wins; in equilibrium everyone bids below their value. This equilibrium is Bayesian: it rests on beliefs about the rivals' values.

DSIC or BIC?

DSIC is robust and independent of others' beliefs. BIC is weaker but allows more goals: e.g. an efficient, strongly budget-balanced public-good mechanism (d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet, 1979; Arrow, 1979).

Maximum revenue

The mechanism maximizing the seller's expected revenue is also a Bayesian question; when values are i.i.d. from a regular distribution, the answer is a second-price auction with a reserve price (a minimum below which the seller won't sell) (Myerson, 1981).

Poker and bluffing

The opponent's hand (type) is hidden; bluffing is part of a mixed Bayesian strategy.

Discussion: Behavior, Fairness and Ethics

Do people always respond to incentives?
  • Theory: players maximize utility.
  • In reality, emotions, morals, habit and a sense of fairness matter too (e.g. low offers are rejected in the Ultimatum Game).
  • Incentives are powerful but not the only driver; simple, understandable rules matter.
Ethical issues in mechanism design
  • Manipulation and withholding information; powerful players gaining an advantage.
  • Unfair distribution of income; excluding those who cannot pay.
  • Ethical limits should be set by society and institutions: a good mechanism must be fair and ethical, not just efficient.
