    How to play

    In each round both players choose Cooperate (C) or Betray (D). Payoffs: both C → 3/3, both D → 1/1, D against C → 5 for the betrayer and 0 for the cooperator. Choose a scenario, a level and (in the duel) the strategies, then press Run experiment.

    • Duel view: each small block is one round; green = cooperation, red = betrayal; an orange outline marks a move flipped by noise.
    • Cooperation is the share of C among all moves.
    • Show the calculation (bottom left): when on, the duel lists every round’s move and the rule both strategies applied; the league and evolution show the pairing table. When off, the page looks exactly as usual.

    Contribution to game theory

    Repeated interaction changes incentives, because today’s move affects the opponent’s behaviour tomorrow. Repeated games can sustain cooperation through reciprocity, punishment and reputation; noise reveals whether a strategy can recover from accidental betrayals.

    Scenarios

    • Strategy duel: two strategies are compared head to head.
    • Round-robin tournament: every strategy plays every other strategy (and a copy of itself); total payoff decides the ranking.
    • Evolutionary tournament: the average payoff of each pairing is used as fitness in replicator dynamics for 60 generations, starting from equal shares, linking repeated games to evolutionary selection.

    Modes

    • Human vs. AI: you choose strategy A; the computer draws B at random on every run.
    • Choose both: you set A and B.
    • Computer chooses: the computer draws both strategies at random.

    Strategies

    • Always Cooperate / Always Betray: basic strategies that show exploitation and the short-term temptation to betray.
    • Tit-for-Tat: starts with C, then copies the opponent’s previous move; immediately forgiving reciprocity.
    • Grim Trigger: cooperates until the opponent betrays once, then betrays forever.
    • Generous TFT: like Tit-for-Tat but forgives a betrayal with probability 1/4, which makes it more robust to noise.
    • Win-Stay, Lose-Shift: repeats its move after a payoff of 3 or 5, switches after 0 or 1.
    • Random: C or D with probability 1/2.

    Levels

    • Level 1: 20 rounds; a short horizon for understanding the logic of the strategies.
    • Level 2: 100 rounds; reputation and retaliation matter more.
    • Level 3: 100 rounds in which every move is flipped with probability 5%; tests forgiveness.

    What to look for: no strategy is best in every situation. Success depends on the opposing population, the horizon, and whether errors occur.

    The model: one round

    Two strategies move simultaneously, C (cooperate) or D (betray); the code stores C as 0 and D as 1. Payoffs (A, B):

    B: CB: D
    A: C3 , 30 , 5
    A: D5 , 01 , 1

    T = 5 > R = 3 > P = 1 > S = 0 and 2R = 6 > T + S = 5. The first condition makes D tempting in every round; the second makes taking turns exploiting each other worth less than steady cooperation. Axelrod’s tournaments used the same numbers.

    A round works like this:

    1. Each strategy picks its move from its own history, the opponent’s history and its own last payoff (strategyMove). Nobody sees the current round’s move in advance.
    2. Noise: at level 3 each player’s move is flipped independently with probability 5% (C ↔ D); at the other levels the probability is 0. A flipped move counts as really played: the payoff and both players’ histories record the flipped move, so the opponent sees the error and reacts to it.
    3. Payoffs are computed, histories are extended, “last payoff” is updated.

    A match has 20 rounds at level 1 and 100 at levels 2 and 3; the highest possible score is 5 × the number of rounds. The Cooperation readout is the share of C among both players’ moves.

    The three scenarios

    • Duel: the two chosen strategies play one match; the block strip shows every round’s move.
    • League: every pair with i ≤ j plays one match (28 matches for 7 strategies, including the 7 matches against own copies). A strategy’s score is the sum of its payoffs in its own 7 matches; a match against its own copy is counted only once. The ranking uses this sum. Because the random strategies play differently each time, the ranking can change a little from run to run.
    • Evolution: the average payoff per round of the league matches forms the matrix A[i][j]. Shares x start equal (1/7) and for 60 generations are updated with fitness fi = Σj xj·A[i][j] by xi ← xi·fi / f̄ (f̄ is the mean fitness). A strategy above the mean grows, one below shrinks. (For the diagonal A[i][i] the code keeps the B-side average of the match against the own copy; this is an implementation detail.)

    Worked example

    Level 1 (20 rounds), A = Tit-for-Tat, B = Always Betray. Round 1: TFT has no history and plays C; B plays D → (0, 5). Round 2: the opponent’s previous move was D, so TFT plays D → (D, D) = (1, 1). Rounds 2–20 are identical: 19 × 1. Totals: A = 0 + 19 = 19, B = 5 + 19 = 24. TFT loses the duel by 5 points. The same TFT against a copy of itself earns 20 × 3 = 60. Turn on Show the calculation to check this yourself.

    Why Tit-for-Tat? Axelrod’s tournaments

    In 1980 Robert Axelrod asked researchers to submit computer strategies for the Prisoner’s Dilemma: 14 entries plus a Random strategy each played every other one, a copy of itself and Random for 200 rounds. The winner was the shortest program, Anatol Rapoport’s Tit-for-Tat. Axelrod published the results and ran a second tournament: 62 entries, all of whose authors knew the analysis of the first. Rapoport again submitted Tit-for-Tat and again won. Axelrod’s lessons (The Evolution of Cooperation, 1984):

    • Be nice: never be the first to betray. In the first tournament the top eight entries were all nice.
    • Retaliate: do not leave betrayal unanswered; Always Cooperate gets exploited.
    • Forgive: do not prolong punishment; punish once and return to cooperation. Endless punishment (Grim Trigger) is costly in the long run.
    • Do not be envious: the goal is a large total, not beating the partner. Tit-for-Tat never scores more than its opponent in any match, yet wins on the sum over all matches. The game is not zero-sum.

    The 7-strategy field on this page is smaller than Axelrod’s and different, so the outcome need not be the same. In the noise-free league (measured over hundreds of runs) Grim Trigger finishes first every time at level 2 and most of the time at level 1; Tit-for-Tat, Generous TFT and Win-Stay Lose-Shift follow closely, Always Cooperate and Always Betray sit in the middle and Random is last. The reason is the field’s Random and Always Betray: after Random’s first betrayal Grim plays D forever, and D is the best reply to Random (average (5+1)/2 = 3 per round), while Tit-for-Tat copies Random and averages 2.25. Against nice strategies both score 3.

    The evolutionary tournament teaches another lesson: the shares of Always Betray and Random go to zero (the payoff of betrayal falls as there are fewer strategies to exploit), while Always Cooperate survives once the exploiters are gone. At level 3 (5% noise) the picture changes: a single accidental betrayal throws two Tit-for-Tats into an endless “echo of retaliation” (C-D, D-C, …) and drives Grim Trigger into permanent betrayal. Generous TFT cuts the echo by forgiving and is the strategy most often left with the largest share (in line with Nowak and Sigmund’s 1990s findings).

    1. The strategies: strategyMove

    /* move: 0 = C (cooperate), 1 = D (betray)
   id:   0 Always Cooperate, 1 Always Betray, 2 Tit-for-Tat, 3 Grim Trigger,
         4 Generous TFT, 5 Win-Stay Lose-Shift, 6 Random */
function strategyMove(id, myHistory, oppHistory, lastPayoff) {
  if (id === 0) return 0;                          // always C
  if (id === 1) return 1;                          // always D
  if (id === 2)                                    // Tit-for-Tat
    return oppHistory.length ? oppHistory[oppHistory.length - 1] : 0;
  if (id === 3)                                    // Grim Trigger
    return oppHistory.includes(1) ? 1 : 0;
  if (id === 4) {                                  // Generous TFT
    if (!oppHistory.length) return 0;
    return oppHistory[oppHistory.length - 1] === 1 && Math.random() >= .25 ? 1 : 0;
  }
  if (id === 5) {                                  // Win-Stay, Lose-Shift
    if (!myHistory.length) return 0;
    const mine = myHistory[myHistory.length - 1];
    return lastPayoff >= 3 ? mine : 1 - mine;      // after 3 or 5: repeat
  }
  return Math.random() < .5 ? 0 : 1;               // Random
}

    2. One match: duel

    Noise is applied after the strategies decide, so a strategy sees its own error in its history too.

    function duel(a, b) {
  const R = horizon();                             // level 1: 20, others: 100
  const noise = level.value === '3' ? .05 : 0;     // noise only at level 3
  const ha = [], hb = [];                          // moves actually played (after noise)
  let A = 0, B = 0, lastA = 3, lastB = 3;          // lastX: that player's last payoff
  for (let r = 0; r < R; r++) {
    let x = strategyMove(a, ha, hb, lastA);        // both strategies look at earlier rounds only
    let y = strategyMove(b, hb, ha, lastB);
    if (Math.random() < noise) x = 1 - x;          // flip the move (C <-> D)
    if (Math.random() < noise) y = 1 - y;
    const payoff = x === 0 && y === 0 ? [3, 3]
                 : x === 0 && y === 1 ? [0, 5]
                 : x === 1 && y === 0 ? [5, 0] : [1, 1];
    A += payoff[0];  B += payoff[1];
    lastA = payoff[0];  lastB = payoff[1];
    ha.push(x);  hb.push(y);                       // the flipped move is what gets recorded
    /* ... cooperation counter and noise markers omitted ... */
  }
  return { A, B, R, ha, hb /* , flips, coop */ };
}

    3. League and evolutionary tournament

    function tournament() {
  const n = strategies.length;                     // 7
  const scores = Array(n).fill(0);
  const avg = Array.from({ length: n }, () => Array(n).fill(0));
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    for (let j = i; j < n; j++) {                  // every pair once; j = i is a copy of itself
      const d = duel(i, j);
      scores[i] += d.A;
      if (j !== i) scores[j] += d.B;               // a self-match is counted once
      avg[i][j] = d.A / d.R;                       // average payoff per round
      avg[j][i] = d.B / d.R;
    }
  }
  return { scores, avg /* , coop */ };
}

/* evolutionary tournament: replicator dynamics, 60 generations */
let x = Array(n).fill(1 / n);                      // start from equal shares
for (let g = 0; g < 60; g++) {
  const f = x.map((_, i) => x.reduce((s, xj, j) => s + xj * avg[i][j], 0));  // fitness
  const mean = f.reduce((s, fi, i) => s + fi * x[i], 0) || 1;                // mean fitness
  x = x.map((xi, i) => xi * f[i] / mean);          // above-average fitness grows
  const sum = x.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0);
  x = x.map(v => v / sum);                         // keep the shares summing to 1
}

    The same idea in Python

    It uses the names from the course’s Python repo file Chapter13/match.py (PAY, tft, match); noise and the replicator step are added.

    import random

PAY = {"CC": (3, 3), "CD": (0, 5),
       "DC": (5, 0), "DD": (1, 1)}
flip = lambda m: "D" if m == "C" else "C"

tft  = lambda me, opp: opp[-1] if opp else "C"
grim = lambda me, opp: "D" if "D" in opp else "C"
def gtft(me, opp):                      # generous TFT
    if not opp or opp[-1] == "C":
        return "C"
    return "C" if random.random() < 0.25 else "D"    # forgive a betrayal with prob 1/4
def wsls(me, opp):                      # win-stay, lose-shift
    if not me:
        return "C"
    won = PAY[me[-1] + opp[-1]][0] >= 3              # got 3 or 5
    return me[-1] if won else flip(me[-1])

def match(s1, s2, T, noise=0.0):        # Chapter13/match.py with noise added
    h1, h2, p1, p2 = [], [], 0, 0
    for t in range(T):
        a, b = s1(h1, h2), s2(h2, h1)
        if random.random() < noise: a = flip(a)
        if random.random() < noise: b = flip(b)
        u, v = PAY[a + b]
        h1.append(a); h2.append(b)
        p1, p2 = p1 + u, p2 + v
    return p1, p2

def evolve(avg, G=60):                  # replicator dynamics
    n = len(avg)
    x = [1 / n] * n
    for _ in range(G):
        f = [sum(x[j] * avg[i][j] for j in range(n)) for i in range(n)]
        mean = sum(f[i] * x[i] for i in range(n))
        x = [x[i] * f[i] / mean for i in range(n)]
    return x

if __name__ == "__main__":
    always_d = lambda me, opp: "D"
    print(match(tft, always_d, 20))     # (19, 24)
    print(match(tft, tft, 20))          # (60, 60)

    Discussion questions

    • Tit-for-Tat never scores more than its opponent in any duel. How can it still finish first in the league?
      Answer

      The ranking counts total points, not matches won. At best Tit-for-Tat ties its opponent; against Always Betray it ends 5 points behind (19–24). But it collects a high score in every match with nice strategies (3–3) and loses only the first round against exploiters. Axelrod called this “do not be envious”: the game is not zero-sum, and your partner’s points do not reduce yours.

    • In a 20-round match, betraying in the last round always looks more profitable. So why do the strategies keep cooperating?
      Answer

      With a known, finite horizon, backward induction (as in Chapter 2) says the only subgame-perfect equilibrium is (D, D) in every round: D dominates in the last round, hence in round 19, and so on. The strategies do not know the last round; they just play coded reciprocity. If the horizon is uncertain (each round the game continues with probability w), cooperation becomes an equilibrium: for Tit-for-Tat to stay an equilibrium against itself it takes w ≥ max((T−R)/(T−P), (T−R)/(R−S)) = max(1/2, 2/3) = 2/3 (“the shadow of the future”). For Grim Trigger the condition is w ≥ 1/2.

    • Grim Trigger often finishes first in this league. Is it a good idea in real life? What happens at level 3?
      Answer

      It does well because of the field’s Random and Always Betray: against Random, permanent D averages (5+1)/2 = 3 per round, while TFT copying Random averages 2.25. But there is no tolerance for error: under noise a single accidental D by the opponent leads to permanent mutual betrayal, with no way back to cooperation. With 5% noise the chance that one of the two players errs in a given round is about 10%; over 100 rounds an error is certain. That is why its share falls in the evolutionary tournament while Generous TFT rises.

    • Why does Always Betray die out in the evolutionary tournament even though it beats Always Cooperate in a duel?
      Answer

      Fitness is a strategy’s average payoff against the population mix. Always Betray does well only when exploitable strategies (Always Cooperate, Random) are plentiful. As they shrink, Always Betray meets its own kind and the punishing strategies (1 point), while nice strategies earn 3 with each other; its fitness drops below the mean and its share shrinks. This is the same idea as on the Replicator Dynamics page: success depends on who else is around.
