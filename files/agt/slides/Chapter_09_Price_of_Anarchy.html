Basic Concepts

The Price of Anarchy: The Bill for Selfishness

  • The Price of Anarchy (PoA) measures the efficiency lost between the worst equilibrium that selfish behavior produces and the social optimum.
  • In cost problems, the social optimum minimizes total cost.
  • In a traffic network, total delay can rise while each user tries to cut their own travel time.
  • Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou (1999) introduced the idea as the “coordination ratio”; Papadimitriou (2001) named it the “price of anarchy”: the cost of everyone deciding alone (anarchy) instead of a central “dictator”.
Example: in the two-route network the Wardrop equilibrium costs 75,000 and the system optimum 71,250 car·min (ratio 75,000 / 71,250 ≈ 1.053); in the Braess network the new road raised travel time from 65 to 80 min. The Price of Anarchy measures and bounds such gaps with a single number.

Basic Concepts

Nash Equilibrium and Social Optimum

Nash equilibrium (individual)

  • No player can improve their outcome by changing strategy unilaterally.
  • Reflects individual incentives.
  • There may be several (good and bad equilibria).

Social optimum (system)

  • Minimizes total cost or maximizes social welfare.
  • Reflects the system's common goal.
  • Usually not an equilibrium: someone wants to deviate.
Why do they differ? Individual rationality (everyone looks after their own payoff), externalities (costs imposed on others are ignored) and missing information. The ratio between the two outcomes is measured by the Price of Anarchy.

Basic Concepts

Definitions: PoA and PoS

Price of Anarchy
PoA = maxs ∈ NE C(s) / C(OPT)
worst equilibrium: a pessimistic guarantee
Price of Stability
PoS = mins ∈ NE C(s) / C(OPT)
best equilibrium: the optimistic scenario

NE: the game's Nash equilibria; C(s): total cost of state s; OPT: the state of minimum total cost (social optimum). PoA = cost of the worst equilibrium ÷ cost of the optimum; PoS = cost of the best equilibrium ÷ cost of the optimum.

  • With C(OPT) > 0, always 1 ≤ PoS ≤ PoA. With a unique equilibrium they are equal.
  • In payoff (welfare) problems the ratio is flipped: SW(OPT) / SW(equilibrium) (SW: social welfare), so a larger value still means worse efficiency.

Basic Concepts

Measuring and Reading Efficiency

  • Total cost: the sum of all users' delay or resource-use costs.
  • In cost problems, the PoA is the ratio of the worst equilibrium cost to the social optimum cost.
  • A PoA of 1 means no efficiency loss.
  • The larger the ratio, the more strategic behavior costs.

Why the worst case? The PoA is a guarantee that holds without knowing which equilibrium players reach: “whatever happens, the loss is at most this much.” It is the game-theoretic counterpart of an algorithm's approximation ratio.

Pigou's Network

Step by Step: The Price of Anarchy in Pigou's Network

Setup (Pigou, 1920): 1 unit of traffic, many small drivers. Top road: delay x (the share on top); bottom road: always 1.

Equilibrium: the top road is never slower than the bottom (x ≤ 1) → everyone switches to the top. x = 1, everyone takes 1 min: C(eq) = 1.

Total cost: share x on top takes x min, share 1 − x on the bottom takes 1 min → C(x) = x·x + (1 − x)·1 = x² − x + 1.

Minimum: C′(x) = 2x − 1 = 0 → x = 1/2. Half the traffic is “sacrificed” to the bottom road.

C(1/2) = 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4. Top drivers take 1/2 min, bottom drivers 1 min; the bottom drivers want to switch → the optimum is not an equilibrium.

PoA = C(eq) / C(opt) = 1 / (3/4) = 4/3 ≈ 1.33: selfish routing produces 33% more total delay.

Pigou's Network

Why the Difference? Private vs. Marginal Cost

  • A driver looks at their private cost: compares x with 1 → x = 1.
  • Society looks at marginal cost: the top road's total cost is x·x with derivative 2x; 2x = 1 → x = 1/2.
  • The gap is x·ℓ′(x): the delay one driver imposes on the others (an externality).
General principle: at equilibrium the delays of used paths are equal. At the optimum, “my own delay + the delay I add for others” is equalized. With f cars on a road and delay ℓ(f), the added delay is f·ℓ′(f) (ℓ′: the derivative of the delay). This gap is the basis for congestion tolls.

def pigou_poa(p):          # top x**p, bottom 1
  x = (p + 1) ** (-1 / p)  # where C'(x) = 0
  opt = x ** (p + 1) + (1 - x)
  return 1 / opt           # eq. cost is 1

for p in [1, 2, 4, 8, 16]:
  print(p, round(pigou_poa(p), 3))

Linear Latency and Braess

The 4/3 Upper Bound for Linear Latency

Theorem (Roughgarden–Tardos, 2002). If latencies are linear (ℓe(x) = aex + be, a, b ≥ 0), then in every network with every demand: C(eq) ≤ 4/3 · C(opt).
Notation. fe: equilibrium traffic on edge e; feOPT: optimal traffic. Total delay sums “traffic × delay” over edges: C(f) = Σ ℓe(fe)·fe.
1. Equilibrium condition. At equilibrium everyone uses a shortest path. Freeze the delays and move drivers to their optimal paths: nobody finds a shorter one: Σ ℓe(fe)·fe ≤ Σ ℓe(fe)·feOPT.
The left side is the equilibrium's real cost C(f). The right side is a thought experiment: roads as slow as at equilibrium, but traffic spread as in the optimum. It remains to bound this right side by C(fOPT).

Linear Latency and Braess

The 4/3 Bound: Edge Inequality and Summation

2. Edge inequality. For each edge: ℓe(fe)·feOPT ≤ ℓe(feOPT)·feOPT + ¼·ℓe(fe)·fe. The trial cost exceeds the optimum's cost by at most ¼ of the equilibrium's.
Why is it true? Put ℓ = ax + b. The b terms: b·fOPT ≤ b·fOPT + ¼·b·f. The a terms need a·f·fOPT ≤ a·(fOPT)² + a·f²/4, i.e. a·(fOPT − f/2)² ≥ 0: a square.
Example (Pigou, the ℓ = x road): f = 1, fOPT = ½. Left: 1·½ = ½. Right: ½·½ + ¼·1·1 = ½. Equal.
3. Sum. Sum step 2 over edges, use step 1: C(f) ≤ C(fOPT) + ¼·C(f). Move ¼·C(f) left: ¾·C(f) ≤ C(fOPT) → C(f) / C(fOPT) ≤ 4/3.
Pigou attains it: the bound is tight. With constant latencies in the class, the worst case is always a two-link network like Pigou's (Roughgarden, 2003): topology does not raise the PoA.

Linear Latency and Braess

One Recipe: Smoothness

Thought experiment. Let s be a state (each player has a strategy) and sOPT the optimum. What would player i pay if only i switched to their sOPT strategy while the others stay in s? This is i's “trial cost”.
Notation. s−i: the strategies of players other than i. Ci(siOPT, s−i): i's trial cost. C(s) = Σi Ci(s): total cost.
Definition (Roughgarden, 2009). Fix constants λ > 0 and 0 ≤ μ < 1. A game is (λ, μ)-smooth if Σi Ci(siOPT, s−i) ≤ λ·C(sOPT) + μ·C(s) for every s and sOPT.
In words: the trial costs add up to at most λ times the optimum's cost plus μ times the cost of s. The smaller λ and μ, the better the resulting bound.

Linear Latency and Braess

From Smoothness to a PoA Bound: Four Steps

1. Equilibrium. s is a Nash equilibrium: nobody gains by deviating alone. For every i, Ci(s) ≤ Ci(siOPT, s−i).
2. Sum. Add these inequalities over all players: C(s) ≤ Σi Ci(siOPT, s−i).
3. Apply smoothness. The right side is at most λ·C(sOPT) + μ·C(s), so C(s) ≤ λ·C(sOPT) + μ·C(s).
4. Rearrange. Move μ·C(s) left: (1 − μ)·C(s) ≤ λ·C(sOPT). Divide both sides by (1 − μ)·C(sOPT): C(s) / C(sOPT) ≤ λ / (1 − μ). Since s was any equilibrium, PoA ≤ λ / (1 − μ).
Familiar cases. The edge inequality in the 4/3 proof means λ = 1, μ = ¼: 1 / (1 − ¼) = 4/3. In atomic (indivisible-player) linear congestion games λ = 5/3, μ = 1/3: (5/3) / (2/3) = 5/2.
Why does it matter? The proof uses only “nobody deviates alone”, so the same bound holds for weaker equilibrium notions too (e.g. mixed equilibria): the robust PoA.

Linear Latency and Braess

Atomic Congestion Games: PoA ≤ 5/2

Cell: 3 × (right − left), avoiding fractions. No negative cells: it holds (checked for y, z ≤ 60); orange 0s mark equality.

  • Each player is one unit of load; edge cost ce(x) = aex + be.
  • ze, ye: players on e at equilibrium and at the optimum. A lone switcher to their optimal path meets at most ze + 1 players on e.
  • Key inequality (integers): y(z + 1) ≤ 5/3·y² + 1/3·z². With c(x) = x: left is the trial cost; y², z² the optimal and equilibrium costs.
  • ⇒ (5/3, 1/3)-smooth ⇒ PoA ≤ (5/3)/(2/3) = 5/2; the bound is tight (Awerbuch–Azar–Epstein; Christodoulou–Koutsoupias, 2005).
Splittable traffic gives 4/3, indivisible players 5/2: the delay a player adds itself (the "+1") enlarges the loss.

Linear Latency and Braess

Step by Step: PoA in the Braess Network

Once A→B opens everyone zigzags, 80 min each. Total 320,000 car·min.

A common comparison is 80/65 ≈ 1.23. But the PoA's denominator is the optimum of the same network; can the new network do better than 65?

On s→A with 2,000 cars the delay is 20 min; marginal cost ℓ + x·ℓ′ = 20 + 2,000·(1/100) = 40 min. The zigzag uses two such edges: 40 + 0 + 40 = 80; an old path 40 + 45 = 85. 80 < 85: even a planner sends some cars on the zigzag.

Optimum: 1,750 on each old path, 500 on the zigzag (marginal costs equal at 90). Average ≈ 64.69 min; the script searched all splits.

PoA = 320,000 / 258,750 ≈ 1.237 ≤ 4/3. In the classic normalized Braess network (x, 1, 1, x, 0) the ratio is exactly 2 / (3/2) = 4/3.

Load Balancing

The Load Balancing Game

  • n jobs (players), m identical machines; job j has size wj.
  • Each job picks a machine; its cost is that machine's total load (its completion time).
  • Social cost: the makespan (maximum load), i.e. when all jobs are done.
  • Applications: server clusters, cloud task placement, processor cores. Koutsoupias–Papadimitriou first defined the PoA in this model (1999).
This game always has a pure Nash equilibrium: when a job moves to a less loaded machine, the load list sorted in decreasing order gets lexicographically “smaller” (a smaller number at the first difference). This cannot go on forever; the process stops at a Nash equilibrium.

Load Balancing

Step by Step: PoA in the Load Balancing Game

The load balancing game: each job (player) picks a machine and wants to minimize its completion time, i.e. its machine's total load.

Social optimum: total work 6 on two machines → best possible 3 and 3. Makespan 3.

Another assignment: both big jobs on M1 (load 4), both small jobs on M2 (load 2). Can this be an equilibrium?

Deviation check: a big job moving to M2 still waits 4; a small job moving to M1 waits 5. Nobody gains → a Nash equilibrium.

Here PoA = 4/3 and PoS = 1: the optimum is an equilibrium, but so is a bad assignment.

General result (Finn–Horowitz, 1979; Koutsoupias–Papadimitriou, 1999): for pure equilibria PoA = 2 − 2/(m+1) < 2. For mixed equilibria the PoA grows very slowly but without bound in m (Koutsoupias–Mavronicolas–Spirakis, 2003; Czumaj–Vöcking, 2002).

Load Balancing

Code Trace: is_nash(sizes, assign, m)

Call: job sizes and each job's machine (0 = M1, 1 = M2).

The load array is zeroed.

j = 0: i1 (size 2) is added to M1 → load = [2, 0].

j = 1: i2 (size 2) is added to M1 → load = [4, 0].

j = 2: i3 (size 1) is added to M2 → load = [4, 1].

j = 3: i4 (size 1) is added to M2 → load = [4, 2].

j = 0, h = 1: if i1 moves to M2, 2 + 2 = 4; now 4. 4 < 4? No.

j = 1, h = 1: if i2 moves to M2, 2 + 2 = 4; now 4. 4 < 4? No.

j = 2, h = 0: if i3 moves to M1, 4 + 1 = 5; now 2. 5 < 2? No.

j = 3, h = 0: if i4 moves to M1, 4 + 1 = 5; now 2. 5 < 2? No.

No job could escape to a better machine → True: {2, 2} | {1, 1} is a Nash equilibrium (makespan 4).

def is_nash(sizes, assign, m):
  load = [0] * m
  for j, k in enumerate(assign):
    load[k] += sizes[j]
  for j, k in enumerate(assign):
    for h in range(m):
      if load[h] + sizes[j] < load[k]:
        return False      # j moves
  return True             # nobody moves

For h = k (its own machine) the condition is always false; the steps show only h ≠ k.

from itertools import product
def poa_pos(sizes, m):
  opt, eqs = float("inf"), []
  for assign in product(range(m), repeat=len(sizes)):
    load = [0] * m
    for j, k in enumerate(assign):
      load[k] += sizes[j]
    opt = min(opt, max(load))
    if is_nash(sizes, assign, m):    # eq. test
      eqs.append(max(load))
  return max(eqs) / opt, min(eqs) / opt
r = poa_pos([2, 2, 1, 1], 2)
print(round(r[0], 3), r[1])

The Price of Stability

Step by Step: The Price of Stability (The Hn Example)

Each player reaches t by one of two paths: a private path of cost 1/i used only by them, or a shared path of cost 1 + ε = 1.1 (ε: a small extra cost) whose cost users split equally. Optimum: everyone shares, total 1.1, 0.275 each.

Player 4: own path 1/4 = 0.25 < 0.275 → leaves. The remaining three now pay 1.1/3 ≈ 0.367.

Player 3: 1/3 ≈ 0.333 < 0.367 → leaves. The remaining two pay 0.55.

Player 2: 1/2 = 0.5 < 0.55 → leaves. Player 1 pays for the shared path alone: 1.1.

Player 1: own path 1 < 1.1 → leaves. Everyone on their own path: 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 = 25/12 ≈ 2.083.

Script: this is the only equilibrium of the 16 profiles → PoS = PoA = (25/12) / 1.1 = 125/66 ≈ 1.894. As ε → 0 with n players, PoS → Hn = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n.

The Price of Stability

Why PoS ≤ Hn? Potential and Cost

Notation. ce: cost of link e; ne: number of players using e (each pays ce/ne). H(k) = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/k; e.g. H(1) = 1, H(4) = 25/12 ≈ 2.08. Hn = H(n). Total cost C(s): the sum of the costs of the links in use.
Rosenthal's potential. Imagine players joining a link one at a time: the 1st writes ce, the 2nd ce/2, …, the kth ce/k. The link contributes ce·H(ne): Φ(s) = Σe ce·H(ne).
Example (the Hn example, four players). All on the shared path: C = 1.1 and Φ = 1.1·H(4) = 1.1·25/12 ≈ 2.29. All on their own paths: each path has 1 user, H(1) = 1, so Φ = C = 25/12 ≈ 2.08.
The potential is between 1 and Hn times the cost. On every link in use, 1 ≤ H(ne) ≤ Hn (there are at most n users). Multiply each term by ce and sum: for every s, C(s) ≤ Φ(s) ≤ Hn·C(s).

The Price of Stability

PoS ≤ Hn: Walking from the Optimum to an Equilibrium

1. Start at the optimum. Run best-response dynamics from sOPT: at each step one player switches to a path that is cheaper for them.
2. Φ drops at every move. In a potential game the deviator's change in cost equals the change in Φ; if the player gains, Φ falls. There are finitely many profiles, so the dynamics stop at an equilibrium s with Φ(s) ≤ Φ(OPT).
3. Chain it. C(s) ≤ Φ(s) (potential is at least the cost) ≤ Φ(OPT) (step 2) ≤ Hn·C(OPT) (potential is at most Hn times the cost). In the example: 2.08 ≤ 2.08 ≤ 2.29 ≤ 2.29.
Some equilibrium costs at most Hn times the optimum: PoS ≤ Hn (Anshelevich et al., 2004). Hn grows very slowly (H100 ≈ 5.19; roughly ln n). The Hn example (a directed network) shows the bound is tight. The PoA, by contrast, can be as large as n.

Reducing the PoA

Step by Step: A Congestion Toll Shifts the Equilibrium

Two-route network: ℓ₁ = 5 + x₁/100, ℓ₂ = 20 + x₂/200, 3,000 cars in total. No toll (τ = 0): equilibrium ℓ₁ = ℓ₂ → x₁ = 2,000, total 75,000 car·min. Drivers count the toll as an extra cost in minutes.

τ = 2.5: the Route 1 curve shifts up and the crossing moves left: x₁ ≈ 1,833, total ≈ 72.917. A toll is a transfer; it is not added to total delay.

τ = 5: x₁ ≈ 1,667, total ≈ 71.667. Each increase pushes some drivers onto the wide Route 2.

τ = 7.5: x₁ = 1,500 → the system optimum (71,250) is now an equilibrium. Marginal-cost tolls are x₁ℓ′₁ = 15 min on Route 1 and x₂ℓ′₂ = 7.5 on Route 2; what matters is the difference: 7.5.

τ = 10: too high a toll overshoots: x₁ ≈ 1,333, total again ≈ 71.667. The right toll equals the externality; it is not “the more the better”.

Reducing the PoA

Step by Step: A Congestion Toll Fixes Pigou

Start: no toll, everyone on top (x = 1), total delay 1. Goal: make the optimum (x = 1/2) an equilibrium.

Marginal-cost toll τe = fe·ℓ′e(fe): on top ℓ′ = 1 → τ = x; on the bottom ℓ′ = 0 → no toll. A driver on top sees x + x = 2x.

New equilibrium: 2x = 1 → x = 1/2. Selfish drivers choose the optimal split on their own; delay 3/4.

Tolls collected: 1/2 · 1/2 = 1/4. Drivers pay 3/4 + 1/4 = 1 in total: unless the revenue is returned, drivers gain nothing and the gain goes to society.

A fixed toll gives the same result: τ = 1/2 on top → x + 1/2 = 1 → x = 1/2. General theorem: marginal-cost tolls make the optimum an equilibrium in every network.

Reducing the PoA

Other Ways to Reduce the PoA

Congestion pricing

Brings the external cost users impose on others into their decision (London, Stockholm, Singapore).

Central (Stackelberg) routing

A center routes part of the traffic, the rest choose selfishly; as the controlled share grows, the ratio approaches 1.

Capacity planning

Roughgarden–Tardos: the selfish equilibrium for r units of traffic costs no more than the optimal routing of 2r units. Adding capacity can substitute for tolls.

Route advice and information

Recommendations can steer the equilibrium to a lower total cost, but compliance is voluntary.

Mechanism design

Set the rules so that the good outcome is the unique equilibrium.

Counting the cost

Weigh an intervention's information, enforcement and fairness costs against the efficiency it gains.

Real Life and Discussion

Toll lanes in traffic networks, inter-domain route selection on the Internet, time-of-use tariffs in power grids: the question is the same in all three. How bad is the selfish equilibrium, and which intervention brings it closer to the optimum?

Discussion questions

  • Why don't individual interests always reach the social optimum? What externality is at work in Pigou's example?
  • Which incentives could reduce the PoA (tolls, bonuses, central routing)? Which is fairer?
  • Where might Braess's paradox show up in daily life? When does closing a road make sense?
