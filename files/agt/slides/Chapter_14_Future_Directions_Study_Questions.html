Multiple Choice · Strategic Agents

Question 1

What classic problem models the risk that an LLM agent negotiating on a user's behalf pursues some goal other than the user's?

The correct answer is C. The user (principal) delegates the task to the agent; if the agent's goal diverges from the principal's and the principal cannot fully monitor it, the agent may act on its own goal: this is the principal–agent problem. A is a two-player game in which individual interest conflicts with collective interest, B is a probability error, and D is the effect of adding a road to a network making the equilibrium worse.

Multiple Choice · Algorithmic Collusion

Question 2

Four firms share the monopoly profit (100) equally; a deviating firm gets 100 for one period and then 0. For which δ does the grim trigger strategy hold?

The correct answer is A. Under cooperation each firm gets 100/4 = 25 per period; its present value is 25/(1 − δ). A deviating firm gets 100 for one period and then, because of the trigger strategy, always 0. Cooperation holds ⇔ 25/(1 − δ) ≥ 100 ⇔ 1 − δ ≤ 1/4 ⇔ δ ≥ 3/4. B is the threshold for two firms and D for three firms; C solves the inequality the wrong way round. The general rule is δ ≥ 1 − 1/n: the more firms there are, the harder collusion is to sustain.

Multiple Choice · Algorithmic Collusion

Question 3

What did the simulations of Calvano et al. (2020) show about algorithmic pricing?

The correct answer is D. Calvano et al. (2020) showed that pricing agents using Q-learning learn prices above the competitive level without ever messaging one another, and punish deviations with temporary price wars. So B is wrong: collusion does not need explicit communication. A is the opposite of the result; C is behavior not observed in the simulations. This is where the regulatory question arises: if there is no explicit agreement, is there collusion?

Multiple Choice · Learning Prices from Data

Question 4

In the slide example the values come from n = 8 buyers drawn from U[0, 1]; the price with the highest empirical revenue is p = 0.47. Why is this price's empirical revenue (0.294) higher than its true revenue (p(1 − p) ≈ 0.249)?

The correct answer is C. The price is chosen on the same sample to maximize empirical revenue and is also measured on it; lucky fluctuations favor the chosen price, so the measurement is optimistically biased (overfitting). The gap closes as the sample grows: 0.311 versus 0.224 at n = 10, both 0.25 at n = 10,000. A is wrong: the true revenue is computed correctly for U[0, 1]; the gap comes from measuring the price on the data it was chosen on. B has no error; D is the opposite, the bias is large with few samples.

Multiple Choice · Automated Design and Privacy

Question 5

In automated mechanism design that learns an auction with a neural network, how is the incentive compatibility (IC) constraint handled?

The correct answer is D. Dütting et al. (2019) represent the auction with a neural network; the largest extra gain a bidder could get by lying is measured as “regret” and added as a penalty during training. Training pushes the regret toward zero but may not make it exactly zero; the result is approximately incentive compatible. A drops IC entirely; B is the classic hand-design route; C: Myerson's solution is known in the single-item structured case, while automated design is meant precisely for multi-item cases where the optimum is unknown.

Multiple Choice · MEV and the Sandwich

Question 6

What is the simplest protection for a user against a sandwich attack?

The correct answer is D. If the user sets a slippage limit (min_out) such as “I must receive at least this much B”, then when the attacker's front-run pushes the price below this limit the transaction is cancelled and the attack earns nothing. In the sandwich() example, with min_out = 82 a front-run of a = 80 would leave the user only 78.47 B, so the transaction is cancelled; the attacker has to keep the front-run small. B is the opposite: a loose tolerance gives the attacker room. A does not guarantee ordering (the attacker pays more), and C moves the price further, making the attack more profitable.

Multiple Choice · Blockchain Mechanisms

Question 7

Which assumption of mechanism design does a smart contract provide through code?

The correct answer is A. Mechanism design assumes that the designer will keep to the rule announced in advance (commitment); for example, in a Vickrey auction the seller cannot later demand the first price. A smart contract's code is public and runs automatically on the chain; the rule cannot be changed afterwards, so commitment is provided by code. B is wrong: honesty is not assumed, it is pursued through incentives. C is the opposite (pending transactions are visible to everyone, and MEV arises from this); D is wrong too, an auction is needed because block space is scarce.

Multiple Choice · Computation and Scale

Question 8

In a combinatorial auction with m = 12 items, how many non-empty bundles can a bidder report a value for?

The correct answer is B. Each of the m items is either in a bundle or not, so the number of subsets is 2m; removing the empty bundle gives 212 − 1 = 4,096 − 1 = 4,095. A also counts the empty set; C (12² = 144) and D (2·12 = 24) treat the growth as polynomial. At m = 30 the number of bundles exceeds a billion: because the input grows exponentially, bidding languages and approximation algorithms are needed.

Multiple Choice · Computation and Scale

Question 9

Since finding a Nash equilibrium in large games can be hard (PPAD), which approach is used?

The correct answer is A. Because finding a Nash equilibrium in general games is PPAD-complete, large games use approximate equilibria and learning dynamics instead of exact equilibria. Regret-minimizing algorithms drive the average behavior in a repeated game toward a (coarse) correlated equilibrium; the superhuman result in multiplayer poker was reached this way, together with search (Brown and Sandholm, 2019). B is unnecessary, equilibrium is still the target concept; C is wrong, a pure-strategy equilibrium may not exist at all; D is not a systematic method.

Question 11: Trigger Strategy Threshold

Classic Question · Transaction Fee Auction

Question 15: Base Fee and Tip

Five pending transactions have values 12, 9, 8, 6, 3; the block capacity is 3 transactions. (a) In a first-price design, if everyone bids honestly, how much is paid to the producer? (b) Base fee r = 6, tip 1: who gets in, how much is burned, what does the producer receive, and what is the users' net utility? (c) What would happen if r = 4?

Show answer

(a) The three highest bids: 12 + 9 + 8 = 29. But an honest bid is not the best response; users shade their bids.

(b) Those with value ≥ r + tip = 7: 12, 9, 8 (3 transactions: the block is exactly full but demand does not exceed capacity). Burned 3·6 = 18, tip to the producer 3·1 = 3. Net utilities 12 − 7 = 5, 9 − 7 = 2, 8 − 7 = 1.

(c) Four transactions (12, 9, 8, 6) with value ≥ r + 1 = 5 want space and the capacity is 3: the block fills and a tip race begins (first-price behavior returns). A full block raises the base fee by 12.5%: r = 4·1.125 = 4.5; the fee keeps rising until demand falls to capacity.

Classic Question · MEV

Question 16: The Sandwich Attack

Constant-product pool (1000 A; 1000 B), x·y = 1,000,000. A user will sell 200 A and wants at least 150 B (slippage limit). (a) How much B would the user get alone? (b) If the attacker first sells 50 A, and sells back the B received after the user's transaction, how much B does the user get and what is the attacker's profit in A? (c) What would happen if the attacker front-ran with 100 A?

Show answer

(a) New B = 106/1200 ≈ 833.33 → the user would get 166.67 B.

(b) Attacker: B = 106/1050 ≈ 952.38 → receives 47.62 B. User: 106/1250 = 800 → 952.38 − 800 = 152.38 B (≥ 150, the transaction goes through; a loss of 14.29 B). Pool (1250; 800). The attacker sells 47.62 B: B = 847.62, A = 106/847.62 ≈ 1179.78 → receives 70.22 A; profit ≈ 20.22 A.

(c) The attacker receives 90.91 B, the pool is (1100; 909.09); the user would get 909.09 − 106/1300 ≈ 139.86 B < 150, so the transaction is reverted. The slippage limit caps the size of the attack: a tight limit means less MEV.

Classic Question · Winner Determination

Question 17: Greedy Winner Determination

Four bundle bids: t1 = ({a, d}, 9), t2 = ({a}, 5), t3 = ({c}, 4), t4 = ({a, b, c}, 11). (a) Which bids does greedy selection by value take, and what is the total? (b) Greedy selection by value per item? (c) What is the optimum?

Show answer

(a) Order t4 (11), t1 (9), t2 (5), t3 (4). t4 is taken; a, b, c are used up. t1 (a), t2 (a) and t3 (c) conflict. Total 11.

(b) Per item: t2 5, t1 4.5, t3 4, t4 ≈ 3.67. t2 is taken (a); t1 is skipped because of a; t3 is taken (c); t4 conflicts. Total 9.

(c) Non-conflicting pairs: {t1, t3} = 13, {t2, t3} = 9; t4 alone is 11. The optimum is {t1, t3} = 13. Both orderings miss the optimum: value favors large bundles, and value per item is fooled by small ones.

Classic Question · Automated Mechanism Design

Question 18: Two-Type Automated Design

One buyer, one item: type L (value 1) has probability qL = 0.6, type H has value 3. The sale probability to L is xL, H always gets the item; pL = xL·1 (L's participation constraint binds), pH = pL + 3(1 − xL) (H's truthfulness constraint binds). (a) What is the expected revenue for xL = 0, 0.5, 1? (b) What is the best xL? (c) Above what value of qL is “sell to everyone at 1” better?

Show answer

(a) revenue = 0.6·xL + 0.4·(3 − 2xL) = 1.2 − 0.2·xL: xL = 0 → 1.2; 0.5 → 1.1; 1 → 1.0.

(b) Revenue is linear and decreasing in xL: the best is xL = 0, that is, sell only to H at 3 (revenue 0.4·3 = 1.2).

(c) For general qL the revenue is qL·x + (1 − qL)(3 − 2x); the coefficient of x is 3qL − 2. If it is positive, xL = 1 is best: qL > 2/3. The optimum is always at an endpoint (a vertex of the linear program).

Multiple Choice · The Course Map

Question 19

According to the course map, the GSP ad-slot auction in the Online Platforms chapter generalizes the idea of which chapter?

The correct answer is B. On the map, Online Platforms derives from Auctions: in GSP each slot holder pays the bid below, which is the multi-slot generalization of the second-price (Vickrey) auction's “pay the next bid” idea. Auctions in turn come from Mechanism Design: the single-item case of VCG is Vickrey. A is the head of the branch that runs from Social Choice and Voting to Algorithmic Fairness and Ethics. C is the chapter derived from Network Design. D derives from Nash Equilibrium and is about finding equilibria.
