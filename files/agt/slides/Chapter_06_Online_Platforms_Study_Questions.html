Multiple Choice · Platforms and Markets

Question 1

In a two-sided market, what does a cross-side network effect describe?

B is correct: a cross-side (indirect) network effect runs between two different sides; the number of participants on one side sets the value the other side gets. In a marketplace, more sellers mean more choice for buyers, and more buyers mean more customers for sellers. Option A describes the direct, same-side network effect and D describes same-side competition (a negative network effect). C is wrong: the platform chooses its own price structure and often charges the two sides different prices.

Multiple Choice · Platforms and Markets

Question 2

Drivers can work on two ride-hailing apps at once (multi-homing). How does this affect the tendency of the market to concentrate on one platform (tipping)?

B is correct: network effects produce a “winner takes all” outcome when users must pick a single platform. If drivers can work on both apps, the small platform also reaches a large pool of drivers; its value to riders does not fall and tipping weakens. A is what happens under single-homing. C is wrong: the cross-side network effect is a force toward concentration that does not depend on price. D has it backwards: multi-homing lowers switching costs, because users are already on both platforms.

Multiple Choice · Network Effects

Question 3

The more of your friends use WhatsApp, the more valuable the app is to you. Which kind of network effect is this?

A is correct: on WhatsApp it is the number of users on the same side (your friends) that raises the value; this is a direct network effect. A cross-side effect needs two separate sides: on Uber the number of drivers sets the value to riders, and the number of riders sets the value to drivers. A negative network effect is when a crowd lowers the value (congestion); here the value rises instead. A price structure is how a fee is split between the sides, not a kind of network effect.

Multiple Choice · Network Effects

Question 4

In the adoption model, F(x) = 1 − p/x and p = 0.16. If users expect a participation rate of x = 0.4, what fraction actually joins?

D is correct. With expectation x = 0.4, a user joins if θ·0.4 ≥ 0.16, that is, if θ ≥ 0.16/0.4 = 0.4. Since θ ~ U[0, 1], the fraction of such users is F(0.4) = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6. The answer 0.84 = 1 − 0.16 forgets the expectation x; 0.4 is the joining threshold on θ, not the fraction who join. Because participation (0.6) exceeds the expectation (0.4), it keeps growing in later periods.

Multiple Choice · Network Effects

Question 5

With p = 0.16 and an initial expectation x₀ = 0.3, where does the dynamics xt+1 = F(xt) go?

B is correct. For p = 0.16 the equilibria are 0, 0.2 (unstable) and 0.8. Since x₀ = 0.3 is above the threshold: x₁ = 1 − 0.16/0.3 ≈ 0.467, x₂ = 1 − 0.16/0.467 ≈ 0.657, … and the sequence increases at every step and converges to 0.8. A happens only if you start below the threshold (e.g., x₀ = 0.19); staying at 0.2 would need exactly x₀ = 0.2. It cannot reach 1: for users with θ below 0.16 the fee always exceeds the benefit.

Multiple Choice · Platform Pricing

Question 6

In two-sided pricing, which side is charged less, even subsidized (a negative price)?

A is correct. Armstrong's rule has the form pA = markup − the value one A participant adds for side B. The side that adds a lot of value to the other side (or is very price-sensitive) is charged less; if the second term exceeds the markup, the price turns negative and that side is subsidized. The loss is made up by the fee collected from the other side it attracts (on a search engine users pay nothing and advertisers pay). What matters is neither being the crowd (B) nor being the seller (C), but the value added for the other side. The side that brings the most revenue (D) is the side that pays, not the subsidized one.

Multiple Choice · Platform Pricing

Question 7

In the trace of participation(pA, pB), each round first updates nA = 20 + 0.25·nB − pA, then nB = 80 + 0.75·nA − pB; pA = −5, pB = 50, and initially nA = nB = 0. In round 1, nA = 25 comes first. What is nB next?

B is correct. The seller response is nB = 80 + 0.75·nA − pB, and the loop uses the current nA = 25: 80 + 0.75·25 − 50 = 80 + 18.75 − 50 = 48.75 (rounded, 48.8). 18.75 is only the contribution from buyers (80 − 50 forgotten); 30 = 80 − 50 forgets the value buyers add for sellers. 60 is the fixed point the loop approaches only after several rounds, not the value of the first round.

Multiple Choice · Platform Pricing

Question 8

The participation loop updates nA = 20 + eA·nB − pA and nB = 80 + eB·nA − pB every round (eA = 0.25, eB = 0.75, pA = −5, pB = 50). Why does it converge to the fixed point (40, 60)?

C is correct. A deviation from the fixed point is passed on with factor eA = 0.25 in the buyer response and eB = 0.75 in the seller response; applying both in one round shrinks the deviation by a factor of eA·eB = 0.1875. Because the product is below 1, the loop converges to (40, 60); had it exceeded 1, participation would blow up or collapse (tipping). A is wrong: prices stay fixed throughout the loop. The sum in D affects the optimal price calculation; what decides convergence is the product.

Multiple Choice · Sponsored Search

Question 9

Which statement about truthful bidding in GSP is correct?

C is correct. With one slot, GSP is the second-price (Vickrey) auction, where truthful bidding is dominant. With several slots this property breaks: for example, advertiser A with value 10 can bid 6 instead of 10, drop to slot 2, and raise the utility from 300·(10 − 8) = 600 to 200·(10 − 5) = 1,000. B is wrong: truthful bidding can be a best response in some cases, just not always. D is wrong too: with the same bids, GSP revenue is 3,600 and VCG revenue is 2,400.

Multiple Choice · Sponsored Search

Question 10

Clicks are 300/200/100; values are A 10, B 8, C 5, D 2. In a GSP equilibrium the bids are A 10, B 5, C 3.5, D 2. Which statement about this equilibrium is correct?

D is correct. The ranking is A 10, B 5, C 3.5, D 2; each pays the bid below: 5, 3.5 and 2 TL per click. Revenue is 300·5 + 200·3.5 + 100·2 = 1,500 + 700 + 200 = 2,400, the same as the VCG payments and VCG revenue. A is wrong: B (value 8) and C (value 5) bid below their values. C is wrong: even if A bid 6 it would stay above B's 5, pay 5 in slot 1, and its utility would stay at 1,500. B is wrong as well: this profile is the lowest-revenue one among the envy-free equilibria, not the highest.

Classic Question · Network Effects

Question 11: Critical Mass and Tipping

In the adoption model, θi ~ U[0, 1], utility is θi·x − p and p = 0.24; if a fraction x is expected, the fraction who join is F(x) = 1 − p/x (0 if x ≤ p).

(a) Find the equilibria; which one is the critical mass? (b) Trace xt+1 = F(xt) for three steps from x0 = 0.5 and from x0 = 0.3. (c) Above what value of p does the market never form?

Show answer

(a) F(x) = x → x² − x + 0.24 = 0 → x = (1 ± √0.04)/2 = (1 ± 0.2)/2: x = 0.4 and x = 0.6; also x = 0 (nobody joins). 0.4 is unstable: the critical mass is 0.4; 0 and 0.6 are stable.

(b) x0 = 0.5 (above the threshold): x1 = 1 − 0.24/0.5 = 0.52; x2 = 1 − 0.24/0.52 ≈ 0.538; x3 ≈ 0.554 → rises to 0.6.

x0 = 0.3 (below the threshold): x1 = 1 − 0.24/0.3 = 0.2; since 0.2 ≤ p, x2 = 0, x3 = 0: the market collapses.

(c) If x² − x + p = 0 has no real root, there is no positive equilibrium: discriminant 1 − 4p < 0 ⟺ p > 0.25: no positive equilibrium remains (at p = 0.25 the curve is tangent at 0.5).

Classic Question · Platform Pricing

Question 12: Which Side Is Subsidized?

Marketplace model (n: thousands of people, p: TL per month, marginal cost 0):

nA = 10 + 0.25·nB − pA
nB = 70 + 0.75·nA − pB
Π = pA·nA + pB·nB

Find the profit-maximizing participation levels, prices and profit. Which side is subsidized? Verify the result with the rule pA = nA − eB·nB.

Show answer

1. Make participation the decision variable: pA = 10 + 0.25nB − nA, pB = 70 + 0.75nA − nB.

2. Π = 10nA + 70nB + (0.25 + 0.75)nAnB − nA² − nB².

3. Holding the other participation level fixed, the derivatives of Π with respect to nA and nB (partial derivatives) must be 0: ∂Π/∂nA = 10 + nB − 2nA = 0, ∂Π/∂nB = 70 + nA − 2nB = 0 → nA = 30, nB = 50.

4. pA = 10 + 12.5 − 30 = −7.5, pB = 70 + 22.5 − 50 = 42.5; Π = −225 + 2,125 = 1,900.

5. Rule: pA = 30 − 0.75·50 = −7.5 ✓; pB = 50 − 0.25·30 = 42.5 ✓. Because buyers add a lot of value for sellers (eB = 0.75), the buyer side is subsidized; the loss is covered by the seller fee.

Classic Question · Sponsored Search

Question 14: Profitable Deviation in GSP

In a three-slot GSP auction (clicks per 1,000 impressions α = 500, 300, 100; per-click values A 12, B 9, C 6, D 4 TL) everyone bids their value. Assume no ties.

Compute each advertiser's utility. Can anyone raise their utility by changing their bid while the others stay truthful? If so, what are their best response and utility? Is the truthful profile a Nash equilibrium?

Show answer

Truthful utilities: A 500·(12 − 9) = 1,500, B 300·(9 − 6) = 900, C 100·(6 − 4) = 200, D 0.

A: bidding 6 < b < 9 moves A to slot 2, paying C's 6: 300·(12 − 6) = 1,800 > 1,500. Bidding 4 < b < 6 gives slot 3: 100·(12 − 4) = 800. Best response: any bid between 6 and 9, utility 1,800.

B: dropping to slot 3 gives 100·(9 − 4) = 500 < 900; bidding above 12 for slot 1 means paying 12: 500·(9 − 12) < 0. No deviation.

C: moving up costs 9 or 12 (> 6); D: slot 3 costs 6 (> 4). Neither deviates.

Since A can deviate profitably, the truthful profile is not a Nash equilibrium: in GSP, truthful bidding is not dominant.

(a) Find the raw and Bayesian rankings. (b) At least how many fake 5★ reviews must X add for its Bayesian score to reach Y's (≥)?

Show answer

  • X: (35 + 4·5)/(10 + 4) = 55/14 ≈ 3.93
  • Y: (35 + 90·4.5)/(10 + 90) = 440/100 = 4.40
  • Z: (35 + 30·4.6)/(10 + 30) = 173/40 ≈ 4.33

(b) (55 + 5k)/(14 + k) ≥ 4.4 ⟺ 55 + 5k ≥ 61.6 + 4.4k ⟺ 0.6k ≥ 6.6 ⟺ k ≥ 11 (k = 11: 110/25 = 4.4). X already tops the raw average; on the Bayesian score it needs 11 fake reviews.

Classic Question · Reputation Systems

Question 17: Is Fake Reviewing Profitable?

A seller has 15 reviews with an average of 3.8. Scores ≥ 4.2 rank at the top and earn an extra G = 1,000 TL per month. A fake 5★ review costs 20 TL each; the detection probability is ρ, and the loss if caught is L = 4,000 TL.

(a) With the raw average, how many fake reviews are needed, and for which values of ρ is faking profitable? (b) Answer the same questions with the Bayesian score (m = 3.5, C = 10).

Show answer

(a) Σ ratings = 15·3.8 = 57. (57 + 5k)/(15 + k) ≥ 4.2 ⟺ 0.8k ≥ 6 ⟺ k ≥ 7.5 → k = 8 (97/23 ≈ 4.22). Cost 8·20 = 160 TL.

Profitable ⟺ 1,000 − 160 > ρ·4,000 ⟺ ρ < 0.21.

(b) (35 + 57 + 5k)/(10 + 15 + k) ≥ 4.2 ⟺ 92 + 5k ≥ 105 + 4.2k ⟺ 0.8k ≥ 13 ⟺ k ≥ 16.25 → k = 17 (177/42 ≈ 4.21). Cost 340 TL.

Profitable ⟺ 1,000 − 340 > ρ·4,000 ⟺ ρ < 0.165.

The Bayesian score raises the number of fake reviews needed from 8 to 17 and narrows the range of detection probabilities for which faking stays profitable.

Classic Question · Budgets and Online Allocation

Question 18: Greedy or Balance?

Advertiser A has a daily budget of 40 TL, and B has 80 TL. Both bid 1 TL on the query “book”; only A bids 1 TL on “notebook”. First 80 “book” queries arrive, then 40 “notebook” queries; each query goes to an advertiser who still has budget when it arrives.

(a) How much revenue does the greedy rule (highest bid, ties to A) collect? (b) How much does Balance (give to the one with the larger remaining budget) collect? (c) What is the optimum with knowledge of the future? What fraction of the optimum does each rule get?

Show answer

(a) The first 40 “book” queries go to A, whose budget runs out; the remaining 40 go to B. A has no money left for the “notebook” queries: 0. Revenue 80.

(b) Remaining budgets are 40 and 80: the first 40 “book” queries go to B (remaining 40 – 40). The next 40 are split in turn: A 20, B 20 (remaining 20 – 20). A's 20 remaining “notebook” queries are sold. Revenue 80 + 20 = 100.

(c) Optimum: give all 80 “book” queries to B and the 40 “notebook” queries to A: 120. Ratios: greedy 80/120 = 2/3, Balance 100/120 = 5/6.

The greedy rule exhausts the budget of A, the only advertiser bidding on “notebook”, early on “book” queries; Balance preserves budget and cuts the loss.

Multiple Choice · Reputation and Competition

Question 19

A market has tipped to a single platform. A regulator (e.g., the EU's Digital Markets Act, DMA) wants to make it easier for rivals to challenge that platform. Which measure serves this goal?

A is correct. Ratings, history and data are a switching cost that ties users to the platform; once they are portable, moving to a rival gets cheaper. B is loyalty programs: they deliberately raise switching costs. C blocks multi-homing, yet multi-homing weakens tipping. D is self-preferencing; the obligations the DMA places on large “gatekeeper” platforms limit exactly this.

Multiple Choice · Reputation and Competition

Question 20

Fake-review model: k fake reviews at c TL each; the gain from ranking at the top is G, the detection probability is ρ, and the loss if caught is L. Cheating is profitable ⟺ G − k·c > ρ·L. Which measure–effect pairing is correct?

C is correct. The Bayesian average pulls the score of a product with few reviews toward the platform average; more fake reviews are needed to cross the same threshold, so cheating gets more expensive (in the example from the slides, 8 → 13). A is wrong: a verified-purchase requirement makes fake reviews easier to catch, that is, it raises ρ. B is wrong: account closure raises L and leaves G unchanged. D is wrong: anomaly detection also raises ρ; lowering c would make cheating cheaper.
