How to play

  1. Players take turns choosing one of the seven columns (click a column, or use the arrow buttons above the board with Tab and Enter). The numbers on the buttons are the column numbers.
  2. The disc falls to the lowest empty cell in that column. Red always starts.
  3. The first player to connect four discs in a horizontal, vertical or diagonal line wins at once.
  4. A full column cannot be chosen.
  5. If the board fills up without four in a row, the game is a draw.

Computer levels

  • Easy plays at random but never misses a move that wins immediately.
  • Medium wins with or blocks immediate threats and avoids giving the opponent the cell above a threat.
  • Hard looks seven half-moves ahead with minimax (in its negamax form) and alpha-beta pruning; it scores open twos and threes.

With Show move reasoning ticked, in Computer mode the panel on the right shows how Yellow decided every time it moves. The last disc played is marked with a white ring.

Threats and forks

A strong move often creates a threat: a cell that wins on the next turn. Even stronger is a fork: on the next turn there are two separate winning moves and the opponent can block only one. Because discs fall downward, every future threat also depends on whether the supporting cells beneath it can be filled safely.

Strategy tips

  • The middle column takes part in more possible fours than the edge columns.
  • Before chasing your own long plan, check whether the opponent can win right away.
  • Avoid moves that give the opponent the supporting cell needed for a winning diagonal.
  • Against the Hard level, build several threats instead of relying on one obvious line.

Connection to game theory

Connect Four is a deterministic, perfect-information game; it was solved in 1988: with perfect play the first player wins. It is a compact setting for minimax reasoning, tactical threat detection, alpha-beta pruning and heuristic evaluation. The gravity rule makes the action space especially interesting, because not every empty cell is immediately playable.

Three levels, three different methods

  • First step at every level: if Yellow can win at once, it plays that move. Nothing else is looked at.
  • Easy: with no winning move, a random column.
  • Medium: with no winning move it closes the column where Red would win at once; if there is none, it chooses among the "safe" columns that do not hand Red an immediate win (60% of the time the first, i.e. the one closest to the middle; 40% a random one). There is no search.
  • Hard: the search below. There is no randomness: in the same position it always plays the same move.

Hard: what is searched, and why seven half-moves?

Yellow tries every column (1 half-move) and then looks 6 half-moves further ahead, at all possible moves of Red and Yellow in turn: 7 half-moves in total. There are at most 7 moves in each position, so the tree grows fast: 76 ≈ 118 thousand leaves for a single root move and about 800 thousand nodes for the whole position (measured after Red opens in the middle column: 800,477). Searching the whole game is impossible (about 4.5·1012 possible positions). So the depth is limited, and positions at the limit are estimated by an evaluation function. A depth of 7 is a compromise between playing strength and response time; every extra layer multiplies the number of nodes by about 7 (less with pruning).

Negamax: minimax in a single function

Because the game is zero-sum, the value of a position for Yellow is the negative of its value for Red. So instead of separate MAX and MIN functions a single negamax is enough: it always returns the value for the side to move, and the result of the recursive call is negated: v = −negamax(...). For the same reason the alpha-beta window is flipped and passed on as (−beta, −alpha). At every node, in order:

  1. If the board is full the value is 0 (a draw).
  2. If the side to move can win at once by playing some column, the value is 1000 + depth. depth is the number of layers left in the search; the more are left, the earlier the win, so the score is larger. (At a node on the depth limit this check also comes before the evaluation.)
  3. If depth is 0 the position is scored with evaluate.
  4. Otherwise, for every legal column: drop the disc, compute its value with −negamax, take the disc back. The best value is kept and alpha is updated; when alpha ≥ beta the remaining columns are skipped.

So values come from two different worlds: if |value| ≥ 1000 the search found a forced win or loss (within the horizon, with best play from both sides). Small values are only an estimate.

The evaluation function

It counts all lines of four cells on the board ("windows": 24 horizontal, 21 vertical, 24 diagonal, 69 in total) and the middle column. Let p be the side to move and q its opponent:

The opponent's open three (−6) counts slightly more than your own (+5), so the evaluation is mildly defensive. The weights were picked by hand; they have no theoretical justification.

Move ordering and ties

Columns are always tried in the order 3, 2, 4, 1, 5, 0, 6 (middle to edge). The result of alpha-beta does not depend on the order, but if good moves are tried first, tighter bounds are found and more branches are pruned. If two columns get the same value at the root, the > comparison keeps the first one found: the one closer to the middle.

Now a case where the search sees a forced loss: Red played column 4, Yellow column 4 again, Red column 3. Red has two adjacent discs on the bottom row (columns 3 and 4), a Yellow disc sits on top, and it is Yellow's turn. The search returns: column 5 +2, column 2 +1, and every other column −1004. −1004 means "the opponent wins with its 2nd move": if Yellow plays anywhere else, Red plays column 2 or 5 and makes an open three on the bottom row; Yellow can close only one end and Red wins at the other. So only two columns (2 and 5) do not lose, and the AI picks column 5, the one with the larger value.

And one from the winning side: Red has two discs in column 1 and one in column 7, Yellow has columns 3 and 4 on the bottom row, and it is Yellow's turn. Column 5 has value +1005 (1000 + 5): Yellow makes 3-4-5, columns 2 and 6 are both open, Red can close only one, and Yellow's 2nd move wins. The search finds this with only 8 nodes.

What does alpha-beta buy?

For Yellow's answer after Red opens in the middle column, the search examines about 15,800 nodes; without pruning it would need about 800 thousand (50 times more). The result is the same. Each column at the root is searched with the window (−∞, +∞), so the values the panel shows are exact, not bounds. In the sub-branches the returned values are often only bounds, but that does not affect the result at the root.

What does the panel show?

On Hard: the total number of nodes, the chosen column, the parts of the static evaluation of the position after the move (middle column, own and opponent threes and twos), and for every column its value, meaning and node count. The chosen row is highlighted in yellow. The static evaluation is not the value the search found; it is only the score of the board right after the chosen move, and it is there so you can see how the evaluation function works. On Easy and Medium the panel states the rule that was applied. Related lecture: Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning.

The JavaScript on this page

Below is the real code that runs the Hard level (helpers such as dropRow, lineAt and DIRS are not shown for brevity). For the panel only the c4nodes counter and the rows record were added; the search rules did not change.

var ORDER = [3, 2, 4, 1, 5, 0, 6];      /* middle column first: good moves are tried early */

function legalCols(b) { return ORDER.filter(function (c) { return !b[c]; }); }

/* does p make four by playing column c? (only lines through the new disc) */
function winsAt(b, c, p) {
  var r = dropRow(b, c);
  if (r < 0) return false;
  b[r * 7 + c] = p;
  var w = !!lineAt(b, r * 7 + c);
  b[r * 7 + c] = 0;
  return w;
}

/* score of one window of four cells (my disc count, number of empty cells) */
function scoreWindow(cnt, empty, opp) {
  if (cnt === 3 && empty === 1) return 5;
  if (cnt === 2 && empty === 2) return 2;
  if (opp === 3 && empty === 1) return -4;   /* never used: opp is always passed as 0 */
  return 0;
}

/* static evaluation from p's point of view */
function evaluate(b, p) {
  var s = 0, q = 3 - p;
  for (var r = 0; r < 6; r++)            /* middle column: mine +3, the opponent's -3 */
    if (b[r * 7 + 3] === p) s += 3; else if (b[r * 7 + 3] === q) s -= 3;
  for (r = 0; r < 6; r++) for (var c = 0; c < 7; c++) for (var d = 0; d < 4; d++) {
    var er = r + DIRS[d][0] * 3, ec = c + DIRS[d][1] * 3;
    if (er < 0 || er > 5 || ec < 0 || ec > 6) continue;   /* window leaves the board: skip */
    var mine = 0, theirs = 0, empty = 0;
    for (var k = 0; k < 4; k++) {
      var v = b[(r + DIRS[d][0] * k) * 7 + c + DIRS[d][1] * k];
      if (v === p) mine++; else if (v === q) theirs++; else empty++;
    }
    if (!theirs) s += scoreWindow(mine, empty, 0);        /* only my discs in it */
    if (!mine) s -= scoreWindow(theirs, empty, 0) + (theirs === 3 && empty === 1 ? 1 : 0);
  }
  return s;
}

/* negamax + alpha-beta: the value is for p, the side to move */
function negamax(b, depth, alpha, beta, p, filled) {
  c4nodes++;                             /* the panel's node counter */
  var cols = legalCols(b);
  if (!cols.length) return 0;            /* board full: draw */
  for (var i = 0; i < cols.length; i++)
    if (winsAt(b, cols[i], p)) return 1000 + depth;   /* p wins right now */
  if (depth === 0) return evaluate(b, p);
  var best = -Infinity;
  for (i = 0; i < cols.length; i++) {
    var r = dropRow(b, cols[i]);
    b[r * 7 + cols[i]] = p;                           /* try the move */
    var v = -negamax(b, depth - 1, -beta, -alpha, 3 - p, filled + 1);
    b[r * 7 + cols[i]] = 0;                           /* undo it */
    if (v > best) best = v;
    if (best > alpha) alpha = best;
    if (alpha >= beta) break;                         /* cutoff */
  }
  return best;
}

/* the Hard level inside ai() (Yellow = 2, opponent = 1); filled is never used */
var best = -Infinity;
cols.forEach(function (c) {
  var r = dropRow(b, c);
  b[r * 7 + c] = 2;
  var n0 = c4nodes;
  var v = -negamax(b, 6, -Infinity, Infinity, 1, moves + 1);   /* full window */
  b[r * 7 + c] = 0;
  rows.push({ c: c, v: v, n: c4nodes - n0 });                  /* record for the panel */
  if (v > best) { best = v; move = c; }              /* on a tie the first in ORDER stays */
});

The same idea in Python

The same alpha-beta structure and best_move as in the course repository; the board is a flat list of 42 numbers (0 empty, 1 Red, 2 Yellow). The win check here looks at all windows on the board (the JavaScript looks only at lines through the new disc); the result is the same. Try it: after Red opens in the middle column, best_move returns 4.

from math import inf

DIRS = [(0, 1), (1, 0), (1, 1), (1, -1)]
ORDER = [3, 2, 4, 1, 5, 0, 6]          # center first: better move ordering

WINDOWS = [[(r + k*dr) * 7 + c + k*dc for k in range(4)]   # all 69 windows of four cells
           for r in range(6) for c in range(7) for dr, dc in DIRS
           if 0 <= r + 3*dr < 6 and 0 <= c + 3*dc < 7]

def drop_row(b, c):                    # lowest empty row of column c (-1: full)
  for r in range(5, -1, -1):
    if b[r*7 + c] == 0: return r
  return -1

def wins_at(b, c, p):                  # does p win by playing column c?
  r = drop_row(b, c)
  b[r*7 + c] = p
  w = any(all(b[i] == p for i in win) for win in WINDOWS)
  b[r*7 + c] = 0
  return w

def score_window(n, empty):
  if n == 3 and empty == 1: return 5
  if n == 2 and empty == 2: return 2
  return 0

def evaluate(b, p):                    # static score from p's point of view
  q = 3 - p
  s = sum(3 if b[r*7 + 3] == p else -3 if b[r*7 + 3] == q else 0 for r in range(6))
  for win in WINDOWS:
    v = [b[i] for i in win]
    mine, theirs, empty = v.count(p), v.count(q), v.count(0)
    if theirs == 0: s += score_window(mine, empty)
    if mine == 0:   s -= score_window(theirs, empty) + (1 if theirs == 3 and empty == 1 else 0)
  return s

def negamax(b, depth, alpha, beta, p): # value for p, the side to move
  cols = [c for c in ORDER if b[c] == 0]
  if not cols: return 0                # board full: draw
  for c in cols:
    if wins_at(b, c, p): return 1000 + depth   # p wins right now
  if depth == 0: return evaluate(b, p)
  best = -inf
  for c in cols:
    r = drop_row(b, c); b[r*7 + c] = p
    v = -negamax(b, depth - 1, -beta, -alpha, 3 - p)
    b[r*7 + c] = 0
    best = max(best, v); alpha = max(alpha, best)
    if alpha >= beta: break            # cutoff
  return best

def best_move(b):                      # root: the AI is player 2 (Yellow)
  best, move = -inf, None
  for c in [c for c in ORDER if b[c] == 0]:
    r = drop_row(b, c); b[r*7 + c] = 2
    v = -negamax(b, 6, -inf, inf, 1)   # full window: exact value
    b[r*7 + c] = 0
    if v > best: best, move = v, c + 1
  return move

b = [0] * 42
b[5*7 + 3] = 1                         # Red opened in the middle column
print(best_move(b))                    # 4

Discussion questions

  • Why is v = −negamax(...) written in negamax? Why is no separate code needed for MIN?
    Answer

    In a zero-sum game, if a position is worth v to Yellow it is worth −v to Red. The opponent choosing the maximum means, from our side, that the minimum is chosen: min(a, b) = −max(−a, −b). So the side to move always acts as a MAX from its own point of view and negates the value coming from the child. The alpha-beta window is flipped by the same rule: (−beta, −alpha).

  • Why are the root values exact while the values in the sub-branches are often only bounds?
    Answer

    At the root every column is searched with the window (−∞, +∞); since the window never narrows, the value returned is the true (depth-limited) minimax value of that move. In the sub-nodes the window is narrow: as soon as alpha ≥ beta at a node, the remaining children are skipped and the returned value says only "at least / at most this much". These bounds do not spoil the result at the root, because the pruned branch could not have changed the move chosen there. If alpha were carried across the root moves the search would be faster, but the panel values would become bounds such as "≤ v".

  • Where do the evaluation weights (+3, +5, +2, −6, −2) come from? How would the AI play with other choices?
    Answer

    They were chosen by hand: the middle column is rewarded because it takes part in more fours; an open three is worth much more than a two; the opponent's three counts slightly more than our own. If the weight of the opponent's three were very small the AI would notice the opponent's lines late; if it were very large it would defend all the time instead of following its own plan. The weights come from intuition, not from a theory; to find better ones the AI could play against itself and tune them (the learning idea of Chapter 13).

  • Why does depth 7 create a "horizon effect"? What happens if a threat only materializes after 8 half-moves?
    Answer

    At the limit the search scores a position only with the evaluation function and cannot see a forced win or loss beyond it. A threat that will materialize at the 8th half-move shows up at the limit only as something like a "three count" and may not weigh enough. So the Hard level plays very good tactics but can be wrong in very long-term plans; it sees forks that lie inside its horizon.
