    How to play

    1. Four players form two partnerships: Team A = You (P1) + Partner (P3, across from you), against Team B = P2 and P4.
    2. You are the Hakem (the player who chooses trump): after seeing your first five cards, pick a suit as Hokm (trump) and press Declare Hokm. The remaining cards are then dealt and you lead the first trick.
    3. Play goes clockwise: You → P2 → Partner → P4. The first card of a trick sets the lead suit.
    4. If you hold a card of the lead suit you must follow suit (other cards appear dimmed). Otherwise you may play any card, trump included.
    5. The highest trump wins the trick; if no trump was played, the highest card of the lead suit wins. The winner leads the next trick.
    6. The first team to win seven of the thirteen tricks wins the game. The “Games” badge counts the games won by teams A and B.

    Bot levels

    • Easy: random legal cards.
    • Medium: leads with a non-trump card (an Ace if it has one); when following it wins the trick with the cheapest card that beats the current best, plays low if its partner is already winning, and otherwise discards its lowest card.
    • Hard: plays like Medium, but also remembers played cards, leads with a sure winner (all higher cards of the suit already played), and tries to win the trick itself if its partner’s card does not look safe.

    Partnership play

    The goal is not to win every trick yourself. Playing a losing card to your partner can be excellent if it saves a valuable trump or gives the team control of the next lead. Which suits a player cannot follow reveals information about the cards left in their hand.

    Strategy tips

    • Do not spend a high trump on tricks your partner will probably win.
    • If an opponent cannot follow a suit, expect them to ruff it with trump later.
    • Leading a suit repeatedly exhausts it from the opponents' hands and sets up later winners.
    • Indirect coordination is built through the cards led and the strength shown.

    Link to game theory

    Hokm combines partnership incentives, hidden information and sequential play. Players reason from partial observations, update their beliefs about unseen cards and choose actions that help the team. It is a natural setting for signaling, cooperation and coordinated strategy under incomplete information.

    The bots apply a rule; they do not search a tree

    The Hokm bots do not look ahead in a game tree; the function botMove applies a chain of rules when it is a bot’s turn. The bots use only their own hand, the cards on the table and all cards played so far in this deal (played); they cannot see your hand or each other’s. The rules rely on a few helpers:

    • legal: the follow-suit obligation (if the hand has a card of the lead suit, only those can be played).
    • cost(card) = the card’s value (2…10, J=11, Q=12, K=13, A=14) + 20 if the card is trump. The “cheapest” card is the one with the smallest cost; trumps are always expensive.
    • beats / currentWinner: who is winning the trick so far.
    • isTopRemaining(card): have all higher values of the same suit been played? (If so, the card can no longer be beaten in that suit.)

    Hard’s safety test. If the bot plays third and its partner is winning, the fourth player can still beat the partner’s card. The Hard bot counts the partner’s card as safe only if one of these holds: the card is trump; or it is the suit’s highest remaining card (isTopRemaining); or its value is K or A (≥ 13). Otherwise it tries to secure the trick with its own cheapest winning card. The Medium bot makes no such check. (The fourth player always plays last, so its partner is safe; the second player’s partner has not played yet.)

    Small examples

    Cheapest card that wins. Trump ♠. You led Q♥; P2 (a bot) holds K♥, A♥ and 3♥. Its partner (P4) has not played yet, so rule 2 applies: the winning cards are K♥ (cost 13) and A♥ (14); the cheapest, K♥, is played.

    Difference between Medium and Hard. Trump ♠. You led 9♦ and P2 threw 4♦; it is the Partner bot’s turn (its partner is you, and 9♦ is winning for now). Medium: the partner is winning → it discards its lowest ♦. Hard: 9♦ is not trump, not the highest remaining ♦ (10, J, Q, K, A not played), 9 < 13 → not safe; the fourth player might beat it. So it plays its cheapest winning card, such as J♦ (cost 11).

    Is this rule optimal?

    No. “Take the trick with the cheapest sufficient card” is a sensible hunch (each trick is one of 13, and high cards and trumps are limited resources), but:

    • The future is ignored. A card is “cheap” only relative to the current trick; the bot does not work out which card will be worth more in later tricks.
    • The safety test is crude. Hard uses “K or A is safe”; real safety depends on how many higher cards are still out.
    • No void inference. When an opponent fails to follow a suit the bot does not remember it, and does not expect that suit to be ruffed. played stores only the cards played.
    • No trump counting or plan. The bot never leads trump while it has a non-trump card; yet with a long trump holding, leading trump to drain the opponents’ trumps is a strong plan. Bots also give no signals to their partner.
    • “Highest remaining” ignores its own hand. isTopRemaining looks only at cards that were played; if it holds the Ace and the King, it does not anticipate that the King becomes the highest once the Ace is played.

    What would an ideal player do?

    A card counter computes, for each candidate card, the probability of winning the trick and compares it with the future value of that card (what it could win in another trick). For example, in the second example above, if none of the five ♦ higher than 9♦ (10, J, Q, K, A) are in the Partner bot’s hand or have been seen, the probability that P4 holds none of them is

    (26/39) × (25/38) × (24/37) × (23/36) × (22/35) ≈ 0.114

    so P4 holds a ♦ that can beat the 9♦ with probability ≈ 89%. Hard’s “below K is not safe” rule roughly imitates this probability. (The factors assume P4’s 13 cards are drawn from the 39 cards unseen by the Partner bot.) Another probability: that a given player has no card at all in a given suit is C(39,13)/C(52,13) ≈ 1.3%; so leading an Ace in a suit is safe early in the deal, but the danger grows as opponents run out of that suit and use their trumps.

    Game-theory link: the bots are boundedly rational players; they replace the expected-value calculation of Chapter 1 · Decision Theory with a heuristic cost rule. Because of partnership and hidden information, the ideal strategy is more than one player’s calculation: it also requires predicting what your partner will do.

    1. Helpers

    function cost(card) {                 // price of a card: trump is always expensive
  return card.v + (card.s === trump ? 20 : 0);
}

function isTopRemaining(card) {       // have all higher values been played?
  for (let v = card.v + 1; v <= 14; v++) {
    if (!played.some(c => c.s === card.s && c.v === v)) return false;
  }
  return true;
}

function legal(player) {              // follow-suit obligation
  if (!trick.length) return hands[player].map((_, i) => i);
  const lead = trick[0].card.s;
  const matching = hands[player].map((c, i) => c.s === lead ? i : -1).filter(i => i >= 0);
  return matching.length ? matching : hands[player].map((_, i) => i);
}

    2. The bot’s move: botMove

    function botMove() {
  const me = turn;
  const hand = hands[me];
  const ids = legal(me);                        // indexes of the playable cards
  const level = difficultySelect.value;         // 'Easy' | 'Medium' | 'Hard'
  const cheapest = list => list.slice().sort((i, j) => cost(hand[i]) - cost(hand[j]))[0];
  let index;

  if (level === 'Easy') {
    index = ids[Math.floor(Math.random() * ids.length)];
  } else if (!trick.length) {                   // ---- leading ----
    const nonTrump = ids.filter(i => hand[i].s !== trump);
    const pool = nonTrump.length ? nonTrump : ids;      // non-trump cards preferred
    if (level === 'Hard') {
      const winners = pool.filter(i => isTopRemaining(hand[i]));   // sure winners
      index = winners.length
        ? winners.sort((i, j) => hand[j].v - hand[i].v)[0]         // the highest one
        : cheapest(pool);
    } else {
      const aces = pool.filter(i => hand[i].v === 14);
      index = aces.length ? aces[0] : cheapest(pool);              // Ace if it has one
    }
  } else {                                      // ---- following ----
    const lead = trick[0].card.s;
    const best = currentWinner();               // the card winning for now
    const partnerWinning = best.p % 2 === me % 2;
    const lastToPlay = trick.length === 3;
    const winning = ids.filter(i => beats(hand[i], best.card, lead));  // cards that can win
    let partnerSafe = partnerWinning;
    if (level === 'Hard' && partnerWinning && !lastToPlay) {
      partnerSafe = best.card.s === trump           // trump
                 ? true
                 : isTopRemaining(best.card) || best.card.v >= 13;     // highest remaining, or K/A
    }
    if (partnerSafe)         index = cheapest(ids);        // 1. partner is safe: play low
    else if (winning.length) index = cheapest(winning);    // 2. cheapest winning card
    else                     index = cheapest(ids);        // 3. cannot win: cheapest card
  }
  playCard(me, index);
}

    The same idea in Python

    A short version in the style of the course’s Python repository (card = (suit, value), value 2…14). At the end is the probability calculation an ideal player would use; the bots on this page do not use it.

    from math import comb

def cost(card, trump):                   # card = (suit, value), value 2..14
    return card[1] + (20 if card[0] == trump else 0)

def is_top_remaining(card, played):
    suit, value = card
    return all((suit, v) in played for v in range(value + 1, 15))

def beats(a, b, trump):                  # does a beat b?
    if a[0] == trump and b[0] != trump:
        return True
    return a[0] == b[0] and a[1] > b[1]

def best_of_trick(trick, trump):         # trick = [(player, card), ...]
    best = trick[0]
    for entry in trick[1:]:
        if beats(entry[1], best[1], trump):
            best = entry
    return best

def bot_move(hand, legal, trick, played, trump, me, level):
    cheapest = lambda cards: min(cards, key=lambda c: cost(c, trump))
    if not trick:                                    # leading
        pool = [c for c in legal if c[0] != trump] or legal
        if level == "hard":
            sure = [c for c in pool if is_top_remaining(c, played)]
            return max(sure, key=lambda c: c[1]) if sure else cheapest(pool)
        aces = [c for c in pool if c[1] == 14]
        return aces[0] if aces else cheapest(pool)
    best_player, best = best_of_trick(trick, trump)  # winning for now
    partner_winning = best_player % 2 == me % 2
    safe = partner_winning
    if level == "hard" and partner_winning and len(trick) < 3:
        safe = (best[0] == trump
                or is_top_remaining(best, played) or best[1] >= 13)
    winning = [c for c in legal if beats(c, best, trump)]
    if safe:
        return cheapest(legal)                       # 1. play low
    return cheapest(winning) if winning else cheapest(legal)

# The ideal player's calculation: does the last player hold none of the n open cards?
def p_holds_none(n_higher, unseen=39, hand=13):
    return comb(unseen - n_higher, hand) / comb(unseen, hand)

print(1 - p_holds_none(5))        # about 0.886: P4 holds a diamond that beats the 9

    Discussion questions

    • Every trick is one of 13, so why do the bots say “win with the cheapest sufficient card”?
      Answer

      Since all tricks are worth the same, spending a resource (high cards and trumps) more expensively than needed loses compared with taking the same trick cheaply. The marginal idea is that the smallest card that can win secures the same gain at the least cost. Its limit is that “cheap” is defined only relative to the current trick; when the card would be worth more in a later trick, the rule errs.

    • What does Hard’s “K or A is safe” rule approximate?
      Answer

      The chance that the fourth player beats the partner’s card. If n higher cards are still out, the probability that the fourth player holds at least one of them is 1 − C(39 − n, 13)/C(39, 13): about 89% for n = 5, and about 33% for n = 1 (a King led while the Ace is out). Hard reduces this to a two-level rule (“highest remaining, or ≥ K”); it does not compute the real probability.

    • Does the Hard bot cheat, i.e. does it see your hand?
      Answer

      No. The bot uses only its own hand, the cards on the table and the cards played so far in this deal (played); that is information everyone sees. Hard’s edge lies in that record (knowing which cards are gone) and in its more detailed rules; it has no access to hidden information. An ideal player would add the opponents’ voids and beliefs inferred from their plays.

    • The bots almost never lead trump. When is that a mistake?
      Answer

      For a team with a long trump holding, leading trump drains the opponents’ trumps and makes the side-suit winners safe in later tricks. That is a plan that accounts for future tricks, whereas the bot looks only at the current trick. On the other hand, for a team with few trumps, leading trump wastes valuable trumps; so the rule is not “always wrong” but a sensible hunch in most situations.
