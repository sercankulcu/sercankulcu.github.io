Basic Concepts

What Is Game Theory?

  • A mathematical tool for situations in which decision makers interact strategically: my best decision depends on what you will do.
  • The decision makers are called players (agents); the theory tries to explain how they behave and how the outcome takes shape.
  • Players are assumed to act rationally and in their own interest.
  • Widely used in economics, political science, biology, psychology and computer science.
Economics

Price competition between firms, auctions, bargaining.

Computer networks

Selfish routing, where every packet or driver picks its own route.

Artificial intelligence

Multi-agent systems, game-playing programs, learning agents.

Algorithmic game theory adds two questions: How hard is it to compute an equilibrium? How do we design rules (algorithms, protocols, auctions) that lead to good outcomes among selfish agents?

Basic Concepts

Players and Rationality

  • Rational: each player makes the choice that maximizes its own expected utility given its beliefs.
  • Self-interest: a player follows its own utility function; others' payoffs matter only if they enter that utility.
  • Common knowledge: everyone is rational, everyone knows this, everyone knows that everyone knows it, …
Rational ≠ selfish: a utility function may include fairness, altruism or reputation. The outcome results from an interaction that no single player controls.

Basic Concepts

Actions and Strategies

  • Strategy: the plan of action a player chooses. In sequential games it is a complete plan saying what to do in every possible situation.
  • On the right, F2 sees F1's price and responds. F2 has 2 decision nodes with 2 actions each: 2 · 2 = 4 strategies, though only 2 actions (with 3 nodes, 2 · 2 · 2 = 8).
  • A strategy rests on expectations about how others behave.
Pure strategy

Choosing a single plan for sure.

"always Rock"
Mixed strategy

A probability distribution over pure strategies.

(Rock 1/3, Paper 1/3, Scissors 1/3)

Strategy profile: one strategy per player, s = (s1, …, sn). The profile determines the outcome.

Basic Concepts

Payoffs and Utility Functions

  • The value a player obtains, which depends on the strategy profile chosen by all players; measured by the utility function ui.
  • Players choose strategies to maximize their payoffs.
  • It may be money, points, years in prison (with a minus sign) or a level of satisfaction.
  • With mixed strategies a player computes its expected payoff.
The same action (Paper) pays +1, 0 or −1 depending on the opponent's action: a payoff is a function of the profile, u1(Paper, Rock) = +1.

Basic Concepts

Ordinal and Cardinal Utility

Ordinal utility gives only the order (Opera ≻ Football ≻ Home; ≻: "is preferred to"). Order-preserving changes (30, 20, 0 for 3, 2, 0) leave pure-strategy analysis, dominance and pure equilibria intact. Cardinal utility (von Neumann–Morgenstern) also carries the size of differences: lotteries and mixed strategies are compared by expected utility, so the numbers matter.

Example

Does Ayşe prefer Football for sure, or the lottery ½ Opera + ½ Home?

Utility (Opera, Football, Home)LotterySure FootballChoice
(3, 2, 0)½·3 + ½·0 = 1.52Football

Same order, different numbers: the lottery decision can flip; a·u + b (a > 0) preserves it.

Common mistake: saying "4 is twice as good as 2", or using ordinal numbers in mixed-strategy calculations. Expected payoffs assume cardinal (vNM) utilities; with only an order, stick to pure strategies.

Basic Concepts

Information: Who Knows What?

  • What each player knows about the game, the payoffs and the moves made so far; it directly affects strategies and outcomes.
A dashed line is an information set: the player cannot tell which of these nodes it is at. Perfect information means seeing every past move, not knowing the future.

Basic Concepts

Example: Poker and Bluffing Strategies

Setup (Texas Hold'em)
  • Each player has 2 hidden cards and tries to make the best hand with the open cards in the middle.
  • You cannot see your opponents' hidden cards: information is private.
  • Even with a strong hand, you try to infer your opponents' hands from their bets.
The game-theoretic view
  • An opponent's behavior (betting, checking) carries information about them.
  • A bluff is a misleading signal sent to hide the strength of a hand.
  • A player who bets only with strong hands is easy to read.
A bluff is really a mixed strategy: betting with some probability on weak hands too keeps opponents from reading you. Poker programs (e.g. Libratus, 2017) beat professionals by computing such equilibria.

Representing Games

Normal Form (Strategic Form)

  • Models situations where players decide simultaneously (or without knowing each other's choices).
  • A set of players N = {1, …, n}; each player has a strategy set Si.
  • Strategy profile: s = (s1, …, sn).
  • Utility function: ui : S1 × … × Sn → ℝ.
  • With two players, a payoff matrix: row = player 1, column = player 2, cell = (u1, u2).

Representing Games

Example: Rock-Paper-Scissors in Normal Form

  • Two players (n = 2) choose Rock, Paper or Scissors simultaneously. Rock beats scissors, scissors beats paper, paper beats rock.

In no cell are both players happy with their choice: one of them always wants to switch.

Representing Games

Choosing a Strategy in Rock-Paper-Scissors

1. Play at random

Each round, pick each symbol with probability 1/3.

Cannot be exploited: whatever the opponent does, the expected payoff is 0.

2. Play by history

If the opponent played Rock last round, play Paper this round.

Wins against a patterned opponent; but the rule itself is a pattern the opponent can exploit.

3. An unbalanced mix

E.g. Paper half the time, Scissors half the time.

If the opponent always plays Scissors, its expected payoff is +1/2 and yours −1/2: this mix is exploitable.

Even in a simple game, strategy matters. Being unpredictable is not enough; you must be unpredictable with the right probabilities.

Representing Games

Zero-Sum Games

  • Payoffs sum to zero in every profile: u1(s) + u2(s) = 0. One player's gain is the other's loss.
  • A constant sum (e.g. one shared point) works the same: a constant-sum game.
  • Examples: RPS, Matching Pennies, chess (+1 / 0 / −1). Not: the Prisoner's Dilemma, price competition.
  • One matrix suffices: the row player maximizes its worst case (maximin); the column player minimizes the row player's best case (minimax). (Minimax search applies this to game trees, from leaves to root.)
A zero-sum game has no common interest: pure competition. In non-zero-sum games everyone may win (or lose); cooperation and competition coexist, and negotiation can change the outcome (trade talks, climate deals).

Representing Games

Example: A Price Competition Game

  • Two firms sell similar products in the same market and set prices simultaneously: 100 TL or 120 TL.
  • The cheaper one captures the customers; if both are expensive, margins stay high.
  • Cells are profit units: (Firm 1, Firm 2).
Firm 2: 100 TLFirm 2: 120 TL
Firm 1: 100 TL(5, 5)(8, 1)
Firm 1: 120 TL(1, 8)(6, 6)

Both at 100 TL earn 5 each; both at 120 TL earn 6 each.

If the other side picks 100, 5 > 1; if 120, 8 > 6: 100 TL is dominant for each firm. Equilibrium (100, 100) → 5 each, while (120, 120) would give both 6. This is the structure of the Prisoner's Dilemma.

Representing Games

Extensive Form (Game Trees)

  • A game tree shows the order of decisions and who knows what at each point; it models sequential interactions (chess, bargaining, market entry) naturally.

Entry game: the entrant (E) enters the market or stays out; if it enters, the incumbent (I) fights or shares the market. The tree shows the order and who moves at each decision point.

From Normal Form to Game Tree, Step by Step

Start: the normal form of the price competition game. We will draw the same game as a game tree.

Root: Firm 1's decision. Each branch is an action: 100 TL or 120 TL.

At the end of each branch is a decision node for Firm 2; it also picks 100 or 120.

Leaves are outcomes: the four paths match the four cells of the matrix. E.g. path 100–120 is cell (8, 1).

But the firms set prices simultaneously: F2 does not see F1's choice. The dashed information set shows this; the tree is the same game as the matrix.

Remove the information set and F2 sees F1's price first: now the game is sequential, solved by backward induction.

Representing Games

Example: Chess Is an Extensive-Form Game

  • Two players move in turn; each move is a branch, each position a node.
  • Perfect information: both players see the board and all past moves.
  • The result is a win, a loss or a draw (stalemate, threefold repetition, the 50-move rule).
  • Zermelo (1913): in such finite games either one side can force a win or both can force a draw; but the tree is so large that we do not know which.
Chess programs search only part of the tree (minimax + pruning). The trees of small games like tic-tac-toe, Nim and "Don't Say 21" can be solved completely.

Equilibrium Concepts

Equilibrium and Nash Equilibrium

  • Best response: the strategy giving player i the highest payoff when the other players' strategies (s−i for short) are fixed.
  • Nash equilibrium: a profile (written s*) in which every player's strategy is a best response to the others; no one wants to deviate unilaterally (Nash, 1950).
What it says

With the others fixed, deviating does not pay; nobody has regrets. Deviation arrows lead into the equilibrium, never out of it.

What it does not say

That the equilibrium is good for everyone: (120, 120) would give both 6. The Prisoner's Dilemma has the same structure.

Prisoner's Dilemma

Why Is Defection Dominant? Case Analysis

Case 1: John stays silent

Sam silent: −1 (cell a)  ·  Sam defects: 0 (cell c) ⇒ defecting is better (0 > −1).

Case 2: John defects

Sam silent: −3 (cell b)  ·  Sam defects: −2 (cell d) ⇒ defecting is better (−2 > −3).

Whatever John does, defecting brings a lighter sentence: defection is a dominant strategy. No need to know John's probabilities; the argument "it lowers the average sentence" is wrong.
John faces the same dilemma; defection is dominant for him too. The outcome is cell d: (−2, −2), 2 years each. Had both stayed silent (cell a) they would get (−1, −1): confessing is not "best for both"; individually optimal choices make both worse off.

Prisoner's Dilemma

The Lesson: Individual Rationality, Collective Harm

  • The game's unique Nash equilibrium is (Defect, Defect) = (−2, −2).
  • (Silent, Silent) = (−1, −1) is better for both: the equilibrium is not Pareto optimal.
  • Even if they make a deal, it is not binding (a non-cooperative game): each has an incentive to break it.
Lesson: everyone acting rationally in their own interest does not guarantee a good outcome for society.
Where does it appear?

Price wars, arms races, climate agreements, doping in sports, overuse of common resources.

Ways out

Repeated play and reputation (Tit-for-Tat), contracts and incentive design.

Prisoner's Dilemma

Pareto Optimality in Payoff Space

Each outcome is a point (u₁, u₂). The four outcomes of the Prisoner's Dilemma (S: Silent, D: Defect), payoff = −(years in prison).

Nash equilibrium (D, D) = (−2, −2). In the orange region there is an outcome better for both: (S, S).

Pareto optimal: (S, S), (S, D), (D, S) (green). The unique Nash equilibrium (D, D) is Pareto dominated.

  • Pareto dominance: x dominates y ⇔ u(x) ≥ u(y) for everyone, with > for at least one.
  • Pareto optimal: no outcome dominates it; nobody can be made better off without making someone worse off.
  • In the plot: a point is Pareto optimal if no other outcome lies in its upper-right quadrant.
  • Pareto optimality is not fairness: (S, D) is Pareto optimal too, yet one prisoner serves 3 years.
Equilibrium (stability) and efficiency are separate questions; the loss between them is measured by the Price of Anarchy.

Classic 2×2 Games

Six Games: Conflict or Coordination?

Matching PenniesInterests are fully opposed; in every cell the loser deviates. Any predictable tendency is exploited: the only way out is to play at random (a mixed strategy).
ChickenAnti-coordination: players want different actions. Each of the two equilibria favors one driver; whoever credibly shows they cannot back down (commitment) wins.
Battle of the SexesMeeting is a common interest; where to meet is the conflict. No dominant strategy; the fix is communication, convention or taking turns.
Stag HuntA trust dilemma: (Stag, Stag) is Pareto optimal and an equilibrium, but Hare guarantees 3 in every case. Unlike the Prisoner's Dilemma, cooperation is an equilibrium.
Pure CoordinationNo conflict of interest; yet the bad equilibrium (Home, Home) also sustains itself. Players gravitate toward the salient choice, the focal point (Schelling).
Free Money(Heads, Heads) is a strict equilibrium: a deviator loses. (Tails, Tails) is a weak equilibrium: deviating neither gains nor loses; Heads is weakly dominant.

  • "The average will be 45, so 2/3 of it is 30" is only level 1 reasoning.
  • Someone who expects others to say 30 writes lower (e.g. 20); someone who expects everyone to go too low may shift up a bit.
  • The best guess depends on your belief about the levels at which others reason.
  • A level is how many steps a player thinks ahead; do not confuse it with the level of knowledge, which counts how many times "everyone knows" is nested.

Guess 2/3 of the Average

Code Trace: Level-k Reasoning and the Best Guess

Level 0 writes at random (average 45). Level k thinks everyone is level k−1 and writes 2/3 of their guess.

Belief: 30% of the class reasons at level 0, 40% at level 1, 30% at level 2.

Expected average: 0.3·45 + 0.4·30 + 0.3·20 = 31.5.

The winning number is 2/3 of the average: target = 21.

Level 0: guess 45, distance to target 24. Writing the average is far too high.

Level 1: guess 30, distance 9. The classic "average 45 → 30" answer: still far off.

Level 2: guess 20, distance 1. The closest of the levels examined; in the large-population approximation the best response is the target itself: about 21.

Level 3: guess 13.3, distance 7.7. Thinking one level too far loses as well.

Level 4: guess 8.9, distance 12.1. The distance grows as we approach the fully rational answer (0).

Result: against this population, 20 wins; the Nash equilibrium answer 0 loses. The best response depends on beliefs.

def guess(k, L0=45):     # level-k
    g = L0
    for _ in range(k):
        g = g * 2 / 3
    return g
share = {0: 0.3, 1: 0.4, 2: 0.3}   # belief
avg = sum(p * guess(k) for k, p in share.items())
target = avg * 2 / 3
for k in range(5):
    print(k, round(guess(k), 1),
          round(abs(guess(k) - target), 1))

Guess 2/3 of the Average

Iterated Elimination: 90 → 60 → 40 → … → 0

Start: every guess lies between 0 and 90. Nothing eliminated yet.

The average is at most 90, so the target (2/3 × average) is at most 60. A guess above 60 is never better than 60 → eliminated.

Everyone is rational and everyone knows it: nobody exceeds 60. Average ≤ 60 ⇒ target ≤ 40. Above 40 is eliminated.

One more round: average ≤ 40 ⇒ target ≤ 26.7. This needs "everyone knows that everyone knows everyone is rational".

Average ≤ 26.7 ⇒ target ≤ 17.8. Each round multiplies the upper bound by 2/3.

Average ≤ 17.8 ⇒ target ≤ 11.9. The range shrinks geometrically.

Average ≤ 11.9 ⇒ target ≤ 7.9. After k rounds the upper bound is 90·(2/3)k.

The process goes on forever and the upper bound converges to 0: the only profile surviving iterated elimination is everyone saying 0.

hi = 90                # largest possible guess
for t in range(1, 7):
    # avg ≤ hi  ⇒  target ≤ (2/3)·hi
    hi = hi * 2 / 3
    print(t, round(hi, 1))
# hi = 90·(2/3)^k → 0 : unique equilibrium, all 0

Guess 2/3 of the Average

The Unique Nash Equilibrium: Everyone Says 0

Check that it is an equilibrium
  • If everyone says 0, the target is 0: all win (prize shared).
  • A deviation to x > 0 makes the target 2x/(3n); the others' 0 is closer → the deviator loses.
  • No other equilibrium: a loser can move to the target and win a share; if all tie, one can deviate and win alone. It is unique. (With integers only, "everyone 1" is also one for n ≥ 4.)
And in practice?
  • In experiments the winner is not 0: among Financial Times readers (1997, 0–100) it was 13.
  • Full rationality as common knowledge does not quite hold in reality.
Note: the equilibrium is easy to find (unique, pure: everyone 0). The hard part is the best response to real players. With repetition, guesses drift toward 0.

Combinatorial Games

Combinatorial Games and Zermelo's Theorem

  • Two players move in turn; perfect information, no chance, and the game ends in finitely many moves.
  • Normal play: whoever makes the last move wins (Nim; in Don't Say 21, whoever says 21 loses). Tic-tac-toe and chess also allow draws.
  • Zermelo (1913): in such a game either one side can force a win or both sides can force a draw.
  • Strategy stealing (Hex): there are no draws; if the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could play it with an extra stone, so the first player wins.
  • In games without draws (like Nim) every position is N (next player wins) or P (previous player wins): every move from P leads to N, and at least one move from N leads to P.

Two-heap Nim (1 and 2 chips): the player to move takes at least one chip from a single heap; whoever takes the last chip wins.

Labels are computed from the leaves up: (0, 0) has no moves → P. This is a special case of minimax.

Combinatorial Games

Code Trace: Minimax on a Game Tree

At root A, MAX moves (wants large values); at B, C, D, MIN moves (wants small ones). Leaves are final payoffs.

B is a MIN node: start with best = +∞ and evaluate the children in turn.

Leaf 3 (int → returns at once). best = min(+∞, 3) = 3.

Leaf 12: min(3, 12) = 3.

Leaf 8: min(3, 8) = 3.

B returns 3. At A: max(−∞, 3) = 3.

A's next child is C (MIN). Again best = +∞.

Leaf 2 (int → returns at once). best = min(+∞, 2) = 2.

Leaf 4: min(2, 4) = 2.

Leaf 6: min(2, 6) = 2.

C returns 2. At A: max(3, 2) = 3. For A, C is worse than B.

A's next child is D (MIN). Again best = +∞.

Leaf 14 (int → returns at once). best = min(+∞, 14) = 14.

Leaf 5: min(14, 5) = 5.

Leaf 2: min(5, 2) = 2.

D returns 2. At A: max(3, 2) = 3. The 14 under D looks tempting, but MIN picks 2.

The game value is 3: MAX picks B and is guaranteed at least 3 whatever MIN does. All 9 leaves were examined.

T = {"A": ["B", "C", "D"], "B": [3, 12, 8],
     "C": [2, 4, 6], "D": [14, 5, 2]}
def minimax(n, maxi):
  if isinstance(n, int):         # leaf
    return n
  best = float("-inf") if maxi else float("inf")
  for c in T[n]:
    v = minimax(c, not maxi)
    best = max(best, v) if maxi else min(best, v)
  return best
print(minimax("A", True))

Combinatorial Games

Tic-Tac-Toe: The Whole Tree Can Be Searched, and It Is a Draw

  • Tic-tac-toe's tree is small: a program enumerating all games finds 255,168 distinct games and 5,478 reachable positions.
  • The minimax value of the empty board is 0: with perfect play by both sides the game is a draw.
  • In the position on the right, X wins only with the bottom-left square: it blocks O's diagonal and creates two threats at once (+1). Every other square loses to O (−1).
  • In chess a full search is impossible (≈10¹²⁰ games): depth limits, evaluation functions and alpha-beta pruning.

Tic-tac-toe can be played perfectly by computing these values: against such a player you can at best draw. Alpha-beta pruning and depth-limited search: Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning.

Code Trace: Backward Induction in Don't Say 21

Rule: starting from 1, players take turns saying 1, 2 or 3 consecutive numbers; whoever says 21 loses. Base case: if the last number said is 20, the next player must say 21 → loses (L).

c = 19: saying 20 pushes the opponent into an L position → c = 19 is W.

c = 18: saying up to 20 pushes the opponent into an L position → c = 18 is W.

c = 17: saying up to 20 pushes the opponent into an L position → c = 17 is W.

c = 16: 17, 18, 19 are all W (the opponent wins) → c = 16 is L. A new key number!

c = 15: saying 16 pushes the opponent into an L position → c = 15 is W.

c = 14: saying up to 16 pushes the opponent into an L position → c = 14 is W.

c = 13: saying up to 16 pushes the opponent into an L position → c = 13 is W.

c = 12: 13, 14, 15 are all W (the opponent wins) → c = 12 is L. A new key number!

c = 11…9: same pattern; all can reach the next L (12) → W.

c = 8: 9, 10, 11 are all W (the opponent wins) → c = 8 is L. A new key number!

c = 7…5: same pattern; all can reach the next L (8) → W.

c = 4: 5, 6, 7 are all W (the opponent wins) → c = 4 is L. A new key number!

c = 3…1: same pattern; all can reach the next L (4) → W.

c = 0 (start of the game) is L: the first player loses. The second player always completes to 4, 8, 12, 16, 20.

L positions are multiples of 4: always leave the opponent at 4k. If the opponent says x numbers, you say 4 − x.

        for k in (1, 2, 3) if c + k < TARGET)

Combinatorial Games

Nim: A Game Solved Completely by XOR

  • Rules: several heaps of chips; the player to move takes at least one chip from a single heap. Whoever takes the last chip wins.
  • Nim-sum: the bitwise XOR of the heap sizes (binary addition without carry): 3 ⊕ 4 ⊕ 5 = 2.
  • Bouton (1901): a position is losing (P) ⇔ its nim-sum is 0.
  • Why? When the XOR is 0, every move changes at least one bit of one heap → XOR ≠ 0. When XOR = x ≠ 0, reducing a heap h that has x's leftmost 1 bit to h ⊕ x makes the XOR 0.

Many other sequential games can be solved by reducing them to Nim.

Combinatorial Games

Code Trace: The Winning Move in Nim (XOR)

Position [3, 4, 5]. Our move: compute the nim-sum (bitwise XOR).

x = 0 XOR 3 = 3 (binary 000 ⊕ 011 = 011).

x = 3 XOR 4 = 7 (binary 011 ⊕ 100 = 111).

x = 7 XOR 5 = 2 (binary 111 ⊕ 101 = 010).

Heap 0: 3 XOR 2 = 1 < 3 → take 2 chips from this heap. (The XOR's leftmost 1 bit is also set in 3.)

Heap 0: 3 → 1. New position [1, 4, 5]: the nim-sum is now 0.

The opponent faces XOR = 0: every move breaks it, and we restore 0 each time → we take the last chip.

heaps = [3, 4, 5]
x = 0
for h in heaps:
    x ^= h            # nim-sum (XOR)
for i, h in enumerate(heaps):
    if h ^ x < h:     # can it shrink?
        heaps[i] = h ^ x
        print("heap", i, ":", h, "->", h ^ x)
        break
print(heaps)

Sequential Games and Backward Induction

Subgames and Subgame-Perfect Equilibrium

  • Subgame: a part of the game that starts at a single decision node and contains everything below it (without splitting information sets).
  • Subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE): a strategy profile that is a Nash equilibrium in every subgame (Selten, 1965).
  • An ordinary Nash equilibrium may rely on non-credible threats at nodes that are never reached; SPE rules them out.
  • In finite games of perfect information, SPE is found by backward induction (Kuhn/Zermelo).
Backward induction
  1. Find the last decision nodes, those followed only by leaves.
  2. There, pick the best branch for the player who decides.
  3. Replace the node with the payoff of the chosen leaf.
  4. Repeat until you reach the root.

Sequential Games and Backward Induction

Example: A Non-Credible Threat

E (the entrant) moves first; I (the incumbent monopolist) decides only if E enters. The normal form has two pure Nash equilibria (table on the right).

Last node, I: Fight → −1, Share → 1 ⇒ Share. I's node is worth (1, 1).

Root, E: Out → 0, Enter → 1 (knowing I will share) ⇒ Enter. SPE: (Enter, Share), outcome (1, 1).

  • (Enter, Share): the SPE, found by backward induction.
  • (Out, Fight): a Nash equilibrium, but I's threat "if you enter, I fight" is not credible: once E enters, fighting gives I −1 and sharing gives 1.

Sequential Games and Backward Induction

Step by Step: Backward Induction (Investment Game)

Ayşe invests or not. If she invests, Bora shares the profit or runs off with the money. If he runs, Ayşe sues or drops it. The solution starts at the end.

Last decision node (Ayşe, after Bora runs): Sue 1 > Drop 0 → Sue. The node is worth (1, 1).

Bora knows he will be sued if he runs: Share 3 > Run 1 → Share. Bora's node is worth (3, 3).

Root: Invest 3 > Pass 2 → Invest. Subgame-perfect equilibrium: Ayşe (Invest, Sue), Bora Share; outcome (3, 3).

Now suing is costly: Ayşe gets −1 if she sues. Same tree, one leaf changed. Start again from the end.

Last node: Drop 0 > Sue −1 → Drop. The node is worth (0, 4).

Bora: Run 4 > Share 3 → Run. Root: Pass 2 > 0 → Pass. The outcome (2, 0) is worse for both than (3, 3).

With the plan "if you run, I sue", (Invest, Share) is still a Nash equilibrium: nobody deviates alone. But the threat is not credible (−1 < 0): it is not a subgame-perfect equilibrium.

Sequential Games and Backward Induction

Code Trace: solve(d)

Player 1 (index 0) chooses at the root, player 2 (index 1) at x and y. solve(root) is called.

Left branch: solve(x) → first branch a; leaf (3, 1) is returned as is.

No best at x yet: best = (3, 1), choice = a.

Second branch b: (1, 2). Player 2 looks at its own payoff: v[1] = 2 > best[1] = 1.

choice = b; "x b" is printed and x is worth (1, 2). Player 1's favorite, (3, 1), is now out of reach.

At the root, Left is worth (1, 2): best = (1, 2), choice = Left.

Right branch: solve(y). c → (2, 1), d → (0, 0); for player 2, 1 > 0, so choice = c.

"y c" is printed; y returns (2, 1).

At the root player 1 compares: Right 2 > Left 1 → choice = Right. "root Right" is printed.

Result (2, 1). SPE: (Right; b at x, c at y). The unreached choice b at x is part of the strategy too.

def solve(d):
    if isinstance(d, tuple):         # leaf: payoffs
        return d
    name, player, branches = d
    best = None
    for move, child in branches:
        v = solve(child)
        if best is None or v[player] > best[player]:
            best, choice = v, move
    print(name, choice)
    return best
T = ['root', 0, [('Left', ['x', 1, [('a', (3, 1)), ('b', (1, 2))]]),
                ('Right', ['y', 1, [('c', (2, 1)), ('d', (0, 0))]])]]
print(solve(T))

The Centipede Game

  • Two players decide in turn (Rosenthal, 1981). At each node a player either says Take, and the current split is realized, or Pass, and the pot grows.
  • The total payoff grows (1 → 2 → 4 → 6; also 6 at the end), but a player who passes gets less if the opponent takes at the next step.

The number in a node is the player who decides. Leaves show (Player 1, Player 2) payoffs; at the far right, if everyone passes, (3, 3).

Sequential Games and Backward Induction

The Centipede Game: Theory and Experiment

Theory (SPE)Every player takes at every node; the game ends at the first move with (1, 0). Every Nash equilibrium of the game has the same outcome.
Inefficiency(3, 3) is better than (1, 0) for both players: the SPE outcome is not Pareto optimal. The loss grows with the number of nodes.
ExperimentIn the lab most players do not take at the first node; they pass for a few rounds (McKelvey and Palfrey, 1992).
Why?Backward induction requires rationality to be common knowledge and a long chain of reasoning; even a small chance that the opponent is "nice" makes passing profitable.
Centipede shares its logic with the finitely repeated Prisoner's Dilemma: looking back from the last round, cooperation unravels. With an infinite horizon, cooperation can survive in equilibrium.

Sequential Games and Backward Induction

The Ultimatum Game: Backward Induction in Bargaining

  • Rule: Ayşe offers Bora x of 10 TL (whole TL). If Bora accepts: (10 − x, x); if he rejects: both 0 (Güth, Schmittberger and Schwarze, 1982).
  • Backward induction: for x > 0 Bora prefers accepting (x) to rejecting (0), so Ayşe offers the smallest unit: SPE outcome (9, 1). (If Bora also accepts 0, (10, 0) is an SPE too.)
  • Non-credible threat: "I reject anything under 4 TL" supports a Nash equilibrium, but facing 1 TL, rejecting gives 0 < 1.

Sequential Games and Backward Induction

The Infinitely Repeated Prisoner's Dilemma: Trigger Strategies

The grim trigger strategy (GT): start Silent; if the opponent ever defects, Defect forever. When the same one-shot game is repeated, what decides the outcome is whether players know when the game ends.

Finite, known horizon

In the last round there is no future and no punishment: Defect is dominant there. Backward induction carries this to the previous round and on to the first; Defect in every round (as in the centipede).

Infinite horizon (each round continues with probability δ)

Today's gain from defecting is weighed against the lasting loss from future punishment. If δ is large enough (patient players), deviating does not pay and cooperation is sustained in equilibrium.

The punishment is credible because (Defect, Defect) is an equilibrium of the one-shot game: two grim trigger strategies together form a subgame-perfect equilibrium.

Common Mistakes: Sequential Games

Starting at the rootThe first move depends on the responses to later moves; the solution always starts at the last decision nodes.
The wrong player's payoffAt each node compare only the numbers of the player deciding there (the second number at Bora's node).
Confusing a strategy with a pathA strategy specifies a choice at every node, including unreached ones: Ayşe's strategy is (Invest, Sue).
Nash = subgame perfectEvery subgame-perfect equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium, not conversely: some Nash equilibria rest on non-credible threats.
Solving simultaneous games sequentiallyIf moves are not observed (Prisoner's Dilemma), the tree has an information set; backward induction does not apply directly, so solve the matrix.
Words = commitmentSaying "I will sue" is not enough; carrying out the threat must pay at that moment, or be locked in beforehand (contract, deposit).

The Algorithmic View

Why Is Algorithmic Game Theory "Algorithmic"?

Size

Even writing the game down grows exponentially: n players × 2 strategies → 2n profiles. Brute-force search does not scale.

Complexity

A Nash equilibrium always exists, but finding one is computationally hard even in two-player games.

Structure and design

Special structure brings efficiency: linear programming for zero-sum games, XOR for Nim. Rules are algorithms too.

The Algorithmic View

The Four Questions of Algorithmic Game Theory

1. Computation: can equilibria be found?Zero-sum games are solved quickly by linear programming. No fast method is known for Nash equilibria in general games; the problem is PPAD-complete. Solving Hex on large boards is very hard (PSPACE-complete).
2. Efficiency: what does selfishness cost?Price of Anarchy = cost of the worst equilibrium / cost of the best outcome. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, counting prison years as cost, the equilibrium totals 4 years and (Silent, Silent) 2: ratio 4/2 = 2.
3. Design: which rules?Mechanism design seeks rules under which players report private information truthfully (incentive compatible): auctions, matching, voting.
4. Dynamics: how do players reach equilibrium?In repeated and online games information arrives over time; players learn from the past and adapt their strategies.

The common language of the four questions: classifying games, equilibria of classic games and backward induction in sequential games.

A Look at Mechanism Design

Mechanism Design: Game Theory in Reverse

  • Game theory predicts the outcome of a given game; mechanism design designs the rules and incentives that produce a desired outcome.
  • The difficulty: agents' private information (valuations, locations, preferences) is hidden from the designer, and agents may lie.
With money

The designer can charge payments; payments reward telling the truth.

auctions, ad placement
Without money

Incentives come from the rules alone; usually the objective is partly compromised.

elections, room swaps, facility location

A Look at Mechanism Design

Real-World Applications

Search ads

Ad slots are allocated on every search by a generalized second-price (GSP) auction. With several slots, truthful bidding is not dominant in GSP.

Spectrum auctions

Governments sell frequency licenses through designed auctions (in the US since 1994).

School choice

Under student-proposing Gale–Shapley, reporting true preferences is best for each student; the result is a stable matching.

Room and shift swaps

Everyone brings what they hold; trading cycles leave nobody worse off than before, and no money is used.

Traffic and routing

Each driver picks their own fastest route; the cost of selfish choices to total delay is measured by the Price of Anarchy.

Platforms and incentives

Surge pricing draws drivers to peak hours; Bitcoin miners validate transactions for rewards.

The common idea: design the rules so that the system works well even when everyone acts in their own interest.
