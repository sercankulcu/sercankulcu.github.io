Multiple Choice · Behavioral Decisions

Question 1

What does Simon's concept of bounded rationality mean?

The correct answer is C. According to Herbert Simon, a decision maker's information, time and computing power are limited, so instead of comparing every option and finding the best, the decision maker accepts a “good enough” option. A is wrong: deviations are systematic, not random, which is why they can be modeled. B describes the fully rational (even risk-neutral) model; D is a separate and false generalization about risk attitudes.

Multiple Choice · Framing and Probability Weighting

Question 2

In the epidemic example (600 people), the gain frame is: A saves 200 people, B saves all 600 with probability 1/3; the loss frame is: C lets 400 people die, D lets nobody die with probability 1/3. In the gain frame 72% of participants chose the sure option A; in the loss frame 78% chose the risky option D. What does this show?

The correct answer is C. A and C have the same outcome (200 saved = 400 die), and B and D are the same lottery; in all four the expected number of survivors is 200. Only the presentation changes, and the majority switches from the sure option (72%) to the risky one (78%): the framing effect. A is wrong (the expected values are equal anyway), B assumes the outcomes differ, and D is not what the experiment is about.

Multiple Choice · Framing and Probability Weighting

Question 3

How does the probability weighting function w(p) explain the same person buying both a lottery ticket and insurance?

The correct answer is A. With Tversky and Kahneman's (1992) estimate, w(0.01) ≈ 0.055: a 1% probability is perceived as about 5.5%. Because the small chance of winning (lottery) and the small chance of disaster (insurance) are both exaggerated, the same person finds both the ticket and the policy worth buying. B is the opposite: large probabilities are underweighted (w(0.99) ≈ 0.912). A decision maker who follows expected value (C) or is risk neutral (D) cannot explain these two behaviors together.

Classic Question · Prospect Theory

Question 4: Loss Aversion

Prospect theory: v(x) = x0.88 (x ≥ 0), v(x) = −2.25·(−x)0.88 (x < 0). Gamble: 50% +400 TL, 50% −200 TL. (a) What are the expected value and the prospect value; is the gamble accepted? (b) What is the smallest gain G that makes risking a 200 TL loss acceptable?

Show answer

(a) Expected value: ½·400 − ½·200 = +100 TL → a risk-neutral person accepts.

v(400) = 4000.88 ≈ 194.9; v(−200) = −2.25·2000.88 ≈ −2.25·105.9 ≈ −238.3.

Prospect value: ½·194.9 − ½·238.3 ≈ −21.7 < 0 ⇒ rejected.

(b) ½·G0.88 = ½·2.25·2000.88 ⇒ G = 200·2.251/0.88 ≈ 200·2.513 ≈ 503 TL. The ratio 2.51 does not depend on the amount: a loss weighs about 2.5 times as much as an equal gain (the same ratio as 100 → 251).

Classic Question · Tragedy of the Commons

Question 5: Common Pool and the Pigou Tax

5 herders graze animals on a common pasture; the value per animal is 120 − G (G = total animals), and herder i's payoff is ui = gi(120 − G). (a) What are the best response and the symmetric Nash equilibrium g*, G*, and the total payoff? (b) What are the social optimum and the ratio of the equilibrium to the optimum? (c) What per-animal tax t yields the optimum?

Show answer

(a) ui = gi(120 − gi − G−i) ⇒ derivative 120 − 2gi − G−i = 0 ⇒ gi = (120 − G−i)/2. Symmetric: g = (120 − 4g)/2 ⇒ g* = 120/6 = 20, G* = 100. Check: when the others have 80, (120 − 80)/2 = 20. Value per animal 20; 400 per herder, 2,000 in total.

(b) G(120 − G) is largest at G = 60, total 60·60 = 3,600. Ratio 2,000/3,600 = 5/9 ≈ 55.6%.

(c) With the tax, ui = gi(120 − G − t) ⇒ g = (120 − t)/6, G = 5(120 − t)/6. G = 60 ⇒ 120 − t = 72 ⇒ t = 48.

Multiple Choice · Social Welfare Criteria

Question 6

In a society with three positions, the utilities of the policies are P = (10, 6, 2), Q = (7, 6, 4), R = (5, 5, 5). Which policy do a decision maker who assigns equal probability to each position behind the veil of ignorance (Laplace, Harsanyi) and one who applies maximin (Rawls) choose, respectively?

The correct answer is B. The equal-probability expected utilities are 18/3 = 6, 17/3 ≈ 5.67 and 15/3 = 5 ⇒ the Laplace rule picks P, which matches the utilitarian criterion of maximizing the sum (Harsanyi). The worst positions are 2, 4 and 5 ⇒ maximin picks R, the egalitarian criterion (Rawls). A swaps the two criteria; C overlooks that Q is not best under either criterion; in D, Q's average (5.67) is smaller than P's (6).

Multiple Choice · Quantal Response

Question 7

In logit quantal response, P(a) = eλ·u(a) / Σb eλ·u(b). What do the extremes λ = 0 and λ → ∞ correspond to?

The correct answer is B. When λ = 0 all exponents are e0 = 1, so every option is chosen with equal probability. As λ grows, payoff differences are amplified, and as λ → ∞ all probability goes to the option with the highest payoff: the best response. That is why the quantal response equilibrium approaches the Nash equilibrium as λ → ∞. A reverses the extremes; C and D misread the model's parameter.

Multiple Choice · The Endowment Effect

Question 8

The use value of a mug is 15 TL, the value function is linear, and the loss aversion coefficient is λ = 2. What are the lowest price the mug's owner will ask and the highest price a buyer will pay?

The correct answer is A. For the owner, giving up the mug is a loss from the reference point: it feels like −λ·15 = −30, so the owner asks at least 30 TL. For the buyer the mug is a gain: +15, so the buyer pays at most 15 TL. The ratio of the selling price to the buying price equals λ, and no trade happens in between: the endowment effect. C ignores loss aversion; B mixes up the owner and the buyer.

Multiple Choice · Information Cascades

Question 9

In the two-urn cascade model (a signal is correct with probability 2/3), the previous two people said A, and both had followed their own signals. The third person's signal is b. Why does this person still say A?

The correct answer is A. Two a signals can be inferred from the earlier guesses; with the person's own b the difference is f = 2 − 1 = 1, and P(A) = 21/(21 + 1) = 2/3 > 1/2. The person rationally ignores their own signal; a cascade does not require social pressure (B). D is the value obtained without counting the person's own b signal (f = 2 ⇒ 0.8).

Multiple Choice · Punishment and Cooperation

Question 10

In laboratory experiments (Fehr and Gächter, 2000), what is observed when costly punishment is available?

The correct answer is C: contributions stay high. In Fehr and Gächter's (2000) experiments, subjects punished those who did not contribute even when it cost them; because punishment lowered the payoff of free riding, contributions remained high. In the repeated game without punishment, contributions decline round after round because conditional cooperators react to free riders; but an “immediate fall to zero” (A) is not seen in either case. B is wrong, punishment was actually used; D is wrong too, a punished free rider's payoff falls.

Multiple Choice · Second-Order Free Riding

Question 11

In a public goods game, punishing costs the punisher. Which statement is true for players who care only about money?

The correct answer is B. The cost of punishment falls on the punisher, while the benefit (higher contributions) goes to everyone: a cooperator who does not punish is a second-order free rider and earns the most (C is wrong). Reasoning backward from the last round, nobody who cares only about money punishes, the threat is not credible, and contributions fall to 0 (A is wrong). In experiments, punishment occurred even in groups reshuffled every round (D is wrong): altruistic punishment.

Multiple Choice · Ostrom's Principles

Question 12

Which of the following is not one of Ostrom's design principles for successful common-pool resource management?

The correct answer is C. Ostrom's principles (clear boundaries, rules matched to local conditions, participation in rule making, monitoring, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, recognized right to organize, nested governance) describe a third way beyond state control and privatization. Privatization is one of the two solutions Hardin proposed; Ostrom showed that local communities can sustain a resource jointly.

Multiple Choice · The Condorcet Jury Theorem

Question 13

Each voter independently votes correctly with probability p = 0.4. As the (odd) number of voters grows, how does the probability that the majority decides correctly change?

The correct answer is C. When p < 1/2 the jury theorem works in reverse: 0.4 for n = 1, 0.317 for n = 5, and only 0.021 for n = 101. As the crowd grows, individual errors do not cancel but accumulate, and the majority is almost surely wrong. A is the case p > 1/2; B ignores independence; D has no basis.

Classic Question · Inequity Aversion

Question 14: Inequity Aversion

Fehr–Schmidt model, ultimatum and dictator games with 10 tokens. Offers are whole tokens. (a) What is the smallest number of tokens a responder with α = 1.5 accepts? How many does a proposer who knows this and cares only about their own payoff offer? (b) How many tokens do dictators with β = 0.6 and β = 0.3 give in the dictator game?

Show answer

(a) For s < 5 the utility of accepting is s − 1.5·(10 − 2s) = 4s − 15 ≥ 0 ⇔ s ≥ 3.75 (= 10α/(1 + 2α) = 15/4). The smallest whole-token offer is 4: check 4 − 1.5·2 = 1 > 0, whereas 3 − 1.5·4 = −3 < 0. The proposer offers 4 and keeps 6.

(b) When giving s ≤ 5, u = 10 − s − β(10 − 2s) = 10 − 10β + s(2β − 1).

  • β = 0.6: the coefficient 2β − 1 = 0.2 > 0 ⇒ u rises with s, so the dictator gives 5 (u = 5).
  • β = 0.3: the coefficient −0.4 < 0 ⇒ gives 0 (u = 10 − 3 = 7).

Classic Question · Information Cascade

Question 15: Information Cascade

The true urn is A (2/3 of the balls are a). Five people see these signals in turn: b, b, a, a, a. Everyone hears the earlier guesses; f is the number of a's minus the number of b's that can be inferred from the guesses (the public difference). When |f| < 2 in front of a person, that person follows their own signal. (a) What is each person's guess, and the value of f afterwards? (b) What would P(A) be if the five signals were pooled?

Show answer

(a) Person 1: b ⇒ B, f = −1. Person 2: b ⇒ B, f = −2: a B cascade starts.

Person 3: the f in front is −2; sees a, and with their own a the total difference is −2 + 1 = −1, P(A) = 1/3 < 1/2 ⇒ B. Because this guess carries no signal, the public f stays at −2; persons 4 and 5 make the same calculation and guess B. Guesses: B, B, B, B, B.

(b) 3 a's, 2 b's ⇒ f = 1, P(A) = 2/(2 + 1) = 2/3 ⇒ the correct answer is A. Although three of the five saw the correct signal, everyone was wrong: the first two signals determined the whole group's guess.

Classic Question · The Jury Theorem

Question 16: The Jury Theorem

Each voter independently votes correctly with probability p = 0.7. (a) What is the probability that the majority is correct for juries of 3 and 5? (b) What is the probability for a 3-member jury if two members copy the third member's vote?

Show answer

(a) P3 = 0.73 + 3·0.72·0.3 = 0.343 + 0.441 = 0.784.

P5 = 10·0.73·0.32 + 5·0.74·0.3 + 0.75 = 0.3087 + 0.36015 + 0.16807 = 0.8369.

(b) The three votes are always identical, so the majority is one person's vote: 0.7. Dependence reduces the jury to a single voter; the 0.784 gain disappears.

Classic Question · The Wisdom of Crowds

Question 17: The Wisdom of Crowds

A jar holds 100 candies. Four people's guesses: 90, 95, 110, 125. (a) What is the crowd's (the mean's) squared error? (b) What are the average of the individual squared errors and the diversity of the guesses? Verify the diversity prediction theorem.

Show answer

(a) Mean (90 + 95 + 110 + 125)/4 = 105 ⇒ (105 − 100)² = 25.

(b) Individual squared errors: 100, 25, 100, 625 ⇒ average 850/4 = 212.5.

Diversity (squared deviations from the mean): 225, 100, 25, 400 ⇒ 750/4 = 187.5.

Theorem: 212.5 − 187.5 = 25 ✓. The crowd predicts far better than its average member (212.5); the difference is exactly the diversity.

Classic Question · Punishment and Free Riding

Question 18: Punishment and Free Riding

Public goods game: 4 players, 10 tokens each, multiplier 1.6. Contributions are [0, 10, 10, 10]. P2 and P3 each give P1 3 punishment points; P4 does not punish. For each point, the punisher pays 1 and the punished loses 3. (a) What are the payoffs before and after punishment? (b) If punishment is given only to those who contribute less than 10, would contributing 10 have paid off for P1? (c) Who earns the most, and why?

Show answer

(a) Pool 30 × 1.6 = 48 ⇒ 12 per person. Before punishment: (22, 12, 12, 12).

P1: 22 − 2·3·3 = 4; P2 and P3: 12 − 3 = 9; P4: 12. Result (4, 9, 9, 12).

(b) Had P1 contributed 10, everyone would have contributed 10 and there would be no punishment: 16 > 4. If the punishment threat is credible, contributing pays off.

(c) P4 (12): contributed but did not bear the cost of punishment. The benefit of punishment goes to everyone, the cost only to the punisher: second-order free riding.

Classic Question · Threshold Project

Question 19: Threshold Project

Threshold project: 4 players, 10 tokens each; if total contributions ≥ 22, everyone receives 12 extra tokens, otherwise the contributions are lost. (a) Is (8, 8, 6, 0) a Nash equilibrium? What are the payoffs? (b) Is (6, 6, 6, 6) an equilibrium? (c) What is the group's total payoff in equilibria that reach the threshold exactly; compare with the all-zero equilibrium.

Show answer

(a) Total 22 ⇒ payoffs (14, 14, 16, 22). If a contributor lowers their contribution by 1, the total is 21 and the threshold is missed: e.g. if P3 contributes 5, P3 gets 5 < 16. P4 would only lose by contributing. Nobody deviates: a Nash equilibrium (but an unequal one).

(b) Total 24 ⇒ everyone gets 16. If P1 drops to 4, the total is 22, the threshold holds and P1 gets 18 > 16: not an equilibrium.

(c) Total payoff = 40 − 22 + 4·12 = 66, whatever the profile. In the all-zero equilibrium it is 40. The efficient equilibrium is not unique; both which equilibrium to move to and how to split the 22 are coordination problems.

Classic Question · Asymmetric Matching Pennies

Question 20: Asymmetric Matching Pennies

Payoffs (row, column): (Up, Left) = (120, 40), (Up, Right) = (40, 80), (Down, Left) = (40, 80), (Down, Right) = (80, 40). (a) What are p = P(Up) and q = P(Left) in the mixed Nash equilibrium? (b) How would p and q change if the row player's 120 were 320? (c) Logit QRE with λ = 0.05 gives p ≈ 0.60, q ≈ 0.40. Interpret the difference from Nash.

Show answer

(a) The q that makes the row player indifferent: 120q + 40(1 − q) = 40q + 80(1 − q) ⇒ 120q = 40 ⇒ q = 1/3. The p that makes the column player indifferent: 40p + 80(1 − p) = 80p + 40(1 − p) ⇒ p = 1/2.

(b) 320q + 40(1 − q) = 40q + 80(1 − q) ⇒ q = 1/8; p is still 1/2: the row player's probability depends only on the column player's payoffs, so only q changes.

(c) In the QRE the row player shifts toward their own large payoff (p = 0.60 > 0.50). The column player sees this and moves toward Right, so the probability of Left falls below 0.5 (0.40). But it does not fall as far as the Nash 1/3, because in the QRE neither player best-responds exactly. The “own-payoff effect” observed in experiments is absent in Nash and present in the QRE.

Multiple Choice · Reciprocity and Trust

Question 21

In a trust game experiment, senders sent an average of 6 TL out of 10 TL; the amount was tripled and receivers returned an average of 5.40 TL. Which interpretation is correct?

The correct answer is D. The sender gave 6 and got back 5.40: trusting was not profitable, yet the money was sent. The receivers also returned a positive amount instead of the 0 the theory predicts: reciprocity, answering trust with kindness (Rabin, 1993). A is wrong: the theory of purely money-minded players predicts 0 sent and 0 returned. B mistakes the receiver's 18 TL for the sender's gain. C is wrong: reciprocity looks at intentions, while “distribution only” rules that out.

Classic Question · Inequity Aversion

Question 22: Conditional Cooperation in a Public Good

Two players, 10 tokens each; each contributed token is multiplied by 1.6 and split in two: xi = 10 − ci + 0.8(c1 + c2). Player 1's Fehr–Schmidt utility is u1 = x1 − α·max(x2 − x1, 0) − β·max(x1 − x2, 0). (a) If player 2 contributes 10, what is the best c1 for β = 0.3 and for β = 0.1? (b) If player 2 contributes nothing (α = 1)? (c) If both have α = 1, β = 0.3, are (10, 10) and (0, 0) Nash equilibria?

Show answer

(a) c2 = 10, c1 = c: x1 = 18 − 0.2c, x2 = 8 + 0.8c; player 1 is ahead, the gap is 10 − c. u1 = 18 − 0.2c − β(10 − c) = 18 − 10β + c·(β − 0.2).

  • β = 0.3: the coefficient is +0.1 ⇒ c = 10 (u = 16, both get 16).
  • β = 0.1: the coefficient is −0.1 ⇒ c = 0 (u = 18 − 1 = 17).

(b) c2 = 0: x1 = 10 − 0.2c, x2 = 10 + 0.8c; player 1 falls behind, the gap is c. u1 = 10 − 0.2c − 1·c = 10 − 1.2c ⇒ c = 0.

(c) The best response is 10 when the other contributes 10 and 0 when the other contributes 0: both are equilibria. A player with β > 1 − 0.8 = 0.2 is a conditional cooperator: contributes if the other does, does not if the other does not. Which equilibrium is reached depends on expectations.
