Multiple Choice · Definition of Nash equilibrium

Question 1

Which is the necessary and sufficient condition for a profile s* to be a Nash equilibrium?

The correct answer is B: s* is a Nash equilibrium ⇔ for every player i, no unilateral deviation raises their payoff while the others stay at s*−i (ui(s*) ≥ ui(si, s*−i)). C is sufficient but not necessary: if everyone plays their dominant strategy the profile is an equilibrium, but in the Battle of the Sexes there is no dominant strategy and there are still two pure equilibria. A describes Pareto optimality and D describes social welfare (the total payoff); neither is the definition of equilibrium.

Multiple Choice · Marking best responses

Question 2

Cells are (u1, u2). Which are the pure Nash equilibria?
LCR
U(3, 1)(0, 2)(2, 3)
D(1, 0)(4, 2)(1, 1)

The correct answer is A. The row player's best row in each column: L→U (3 > 1), C→D (4 > 0), R→U (2 > 1). The column player's best column in each row: U→R (3), D→C (2). The cells where the two marks coincide are (U, R) and (D, C). In (U, L), option B, the row player does their best but the column player switches to R and gets 3 instead of 1; option C misses the second equilibrium.

Multiple Choice · Counting pure equilibria

Question 3

How many pure-strategy Nash equilibria does the following game have?

Row \ ColumnLeftRight
Up(3, 2)(0, 0)
Down(0, 0)(2, 3)

The correct answer is C. (Up, Left) and (Down, Right) are equilibria: in both, no player gains by deviating alone. In (Up, Right) and (Down, Left) both players get 0 and deviate. In total 2 pure equilibria.

Multiple Choice · Iterated elimination

Question 4

Which statement is correct about strict and weak domination?

The correct answer is D. A strictly dominated strategy can never be a best response to any opposing profile; so deleting it destroys no Nash equilibrium, and the result is independent of the deletion order. A is wrong: deleting a weakly dominated strategy can lose an equilibrium (in a game where Down only weakly dominates Up, deleting Up can lose the (Up, Left) equilibrium). B is wrong: in the Battle of the Sexes no strategy is dominated; C is wrong in the same game, because nobody has a dominant strategy.

Multiple Choice · Dominant strategy and equilibrium

Question 5

Which is the Nash equilibrium of the following game?

Row \ ColumnLeftRight
Up(5, 1)(4, 0)
Down(3, 2)(1, 3)

The correct answer is D. Up is dominant for the row player: 5 > 3 and 4 > 1. Against Up, the column player's best response is Left (1 > 0). The equilibrium is (Up, Left).

Multiple Choice · Weak domination

Question 6

Cells are (u1, u2). How many pure Nash equilibria are there?
LeftRight
Top(1, 1)(0, 1)
Bottom(1, 1)(1, 1)

The correct answer is B. The column player gets 1 in every cell and never wants to deviate; only the row player matters. (Top, Left), (Bottom, Left), (Bottom, Right): the row player's deviation brings no gain → equilibrium; (Top, Right): switching to Bottom gives 1 instead of 0 → not an equilibrium. In total 3. Bottom only weakly dominates Top; if Top were deleted, (Top, Left) would be lost.

Multiple Choice · Computing a mixed equilibrium

Question 7

Cells are (u1, u2); there is no pure equilibrium. In the mixed equilibrium, what is p = P(U)?
LR
U(3, 1)(0, 2)
D(1, 3)(2, 0)

The correct answer is D. p must make the column player indifferent: E[L] = 1·p + 3(1 − p) = 3 − 2p and E[R] = 2p + 0·(1 − p) = 2p. Setting 3 − 2p = 2p gives p = 3/4. The 1/2 in A is actually q = P(L): the row player's indifference gives 3q = q + 2(1 − q) ⇒ q = 1/2. Mixing up the two probabilities is the most common mistake.

Multiple Choice · Mixed strategy

Question 8

In a mixed Nash equilibrium, which statement is correct about the actions a player plays with positive probability?

The correct answer is B. In a mixed equilibrium, all actions played with positive probability give the same expected payoff against the opponent's mixture (the indifference principle). If one were more profitable, the player would shift probability to it and the mixture would stop being an equilibrium; so A is wrong. The 1/3–1/3–1/3 in Rock-Paper-Scissors comes from the symmetry of the game, not from a general rule (C; e.g., in the patrol example p = 2/3). The opponent's payoff being 0 is likewise specific to games such as Rock-Paper-Scissors (D).

Multiple Choice · Mixed equilibrium in Matching Pennies

Question 9

In the unique Nash equilibrium of Matching Pennies, with what probability does each player play Heads?

The correct answer is A. The game has no pure equilibrium. In the unique equilibrium both players play Heads and Tails with probability 1/2; a player who plays any other way has their tendency exploited by the opponent.

Multiple Choice · Nash's existence theorem

Question 10

What does Nash's existence theorem guarantee for every finite game?

The correct answer is D. Nash (1950): in every game with finitely many players and finitely many strategies, the best-response correspondence has a fixed point on the set of mixed profiles, by the Kakutani (Brouwer) fixed-point theorem; this point is at least one mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium. A is wrong: Matching Pennies has no pure equilibrium. B is wrong: Stag Hunt has three equilibria. C is wrong: the only equilibrium of the Prisoner's Dilemma is not Pareto optimal.

Multiple Choice · Focal point

Question 11

In the example "if we are meeting in Istanbul, Taksim at noon", which criterion selects the equilibrium?

The correct answer is C. In a meeting game every common meeting place is an equilibrium; with no communication, players choose "Taksim, noon" thanks to culture and salience: this is Schelling's focal point. D is wrong: in a correlated equilibrium a mediator (a traffic light) gives each player a private recommendation; here there is no mediator. A and B rest on payoff differences, whereas the payoffs of the meeting places are equal.

Multiple Choice · Nash's existence theorem

Question 12

What does Nash's existence theorem (1950) guarantee for every game with finitely many players and finite strategy sets?

The correct answer is A. The theorem guarantees at least one mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium. There may be no pure equilibrium (Matching Pennies), the equilibrium may not be unique (Stag Hunt), and it may not be Pareto optimal (Prisoner's Dilemma).

Multiple Choice · The minimax theorem

Question 13

According to the minimax theorem, if (x, y) and (x′, y′) are two Nash equilibria of a zero-sum game, which statement is correct?

The correct answer is C. By the minimax theorem, in a zero-sum game the equilibrium strategies are exactly the maximin (row) and minimax (column) strategies. Since x is maximin and y′ is minimax, (x, y′) is also an equilibrium (interchangeability), and in every equilibrium the row player gets the same value v. So A and B are wrong: there is no coordination problem. D is wrong: as in the penalty-kick example, the equilibrium can be mixed.

Multiple Choice · PPAD

Question 14

What is the complexity of finding a mixed Nash equilibrium in a general (non-zero-sum) two-player game?

The correct answer is B: finding a mixed equilibrium in a general two-player game is PPAD-complete (Chen–Deng, 2006; for three or more players, Daskalakis–Goldberg–Papadimitriou). C is wrong: since an equilibrium always exists, the answer to "is there one?" is always yes, and NP-completeness does not fit problems of this kind. A holds only for zero-sum games (minimax via LP). D is wrong: support enumeration always finds an equilibrium in finite time.

Classic Question · Best response

Question 15: Pure Equilibria by Best Responses

The row player picks i ∈ {0, 1, 2} and the column player picks j ∈ {0, 1, 2}; each cell is (u1, u2). Find all pure Nash equilibria by marking the best responses for every column and every row.

012
0(3, 1)(2, 4)(0, 2)
1(1, 3)(4, 2)(2, 1)
2(2, 0)(1, 1)(3, 3)
Show answer

Row player (column fixed, the largest u1):

  • Column 0: 3, 1, 2 → row 0
  • Column 1: 2, 4, 1 → row 1
  • Column 2: 0, 2, 3 → row 2

Column player (row fixed, the largest u2):

  • Row 0: 1, 4, 2 → column 1
  • Row 1: 3, 2, 1 → column 0
  • Row 2: 0, 1, 3 → column 2

The only cell marked by both is (2, 2) = (3, 3): the only pure Nash equilibrium. The other marked cells (0,0), (0,1), (1,1), (1,0) form a cycle: in each one a player deviates profitably.

Classic Question · Cournot duopoly

Question 16: Cournot Equilibrium and the Cartel

Two firms choose quantities simultaneously: y₁, y₂ ≥ 0. The price is P = 24 − (y₁ + y₂), and the cost is 0.

a) What is firm 1's best-response function BR₁(y₂)?
b) What are the quantities, price, and profits at the Nash equilibrium?
c) A cartel maximizes total profit and splits output equally. What are the cartel quantity and profit? Why is the cartel not an equilibrium?

Show answer

a) u₁ = y₁(24 − y₁ − y₂); the derivative 24 − 2y₁ − y₂ = 0 ⇒ BR₁(y₂) = (24 − y₂)/2.

b) In the symmetric equilibrium y = (24 − y)/2 ⇒ y₁ = y₂ = 8; the price is 24 − 16 = 8, and each profit is 8·8 = 64.

c) Monopoly: Y(24 − Y) is largest ⇒ Y = 12, 6 each; the price is 12, and each profit is 72 (better than 64).

But BR₁(6) = 9: a deviating firm produces 9, the price is 24 − 15 = 9, and its profit is 81 (> 72); the other drops to 54. Everyone wants to deviate ⇒ the cartel is not an equilibrium.

Classic Question · Dominant strategy elimination

Question 17: Iterated Elimination

Iteratively delete strictly dominated strategies. At each step, write which strategy is deleted, by which strategy, and by which comparisons. What remains?

XYZ
A(3, 3)(6, 6)(5, 3)
B(6, 3)(0, 4)(0, 4)
C(2, 4)(4, 3)(3, 0)

Rows: A, B, C (u1); columns: X, Y, Z (u2).

Show answer

At the start there is only one strict domination: A dominates C. (Y dominates Z only weakly: 4 = 4 in row B.)

  • C is deleted; A dominates it (u1: 3 > 2, 6 > 4, 5 > 3).
  • X is deleted; Y dominates it (u2: 6 > 3, 4 > 3). While C was present (4 > 3), it could not be deleted.
  • B is deleted; A dominates it (u1: 6 > 0, 5 > 0). While X was present (6 > 3), it could not be deleted.
  • Z is deleted; Y dominates it (u2: 6 > 3).

What remains is (A, Y) = (6, 6); it is the game's only pure Nash equilibrium. Strict elimination is independent of order; here there was only one option at each step.

Classic Question · Mixed strategy

Question 18: Expected Payoff in Rock-Paper-Scissors

In Rock-Paper-Scissors (winner +1, loser −1, tie 0) the opponent plays the mixed strategy q = (qR, qP, qS) = (1/2, 1/3, 1/6).

a) Compute the expected payoff of Rock, Paper, and Scissors. What is your best response?
b) What would your expected payoff be if you played (1/3, 1/3, 1/3)?

Show answer

a) Each action beats one action and loses to another:

  • E[Rock] = qS − qP = 1/6 − 1/3 = −1/6
  • E[Paper] = qR − qS = 1/2 − 1/6 = +1/3
  • E[Scissors] = qP − qR = 1/3 − 1/2 = −1/6

The largest is Paper: the best response is always Paper, with an expected payoff of +1/3. The opponent is exploited because they put too much weight on Rock.

b) (1/3)·(−1/6) + (1/3)·(1/3) + (1/3)·(−1/6) = 0. The equilibrium strategy cannot be exploited, but it cannot exploit a weak opponent either.

Classic Question · Chicken

Question 19: Mixed Equilibrium in Chicken

row ╲ columnSwerveStraight
Swerve(0, 0)(−1, 2)
Straight(2, −1)(−6, −6)

a) Find the pure Nash equilibria.
b) Find the mixed equilibrium by the indifference principle; what is each player's expected payoff?
c) In the mixed equilibrium, what is the probability of a collision and the probability that both swerve?

Show answer

a) (Swerve, Straight) and (Straight, Swerve): if the opponent goes straight, swerving (−1 > −6) is the best response; if the opponent swerves, going straight (2 > 0) is.

b) Let q = P(column swerves):

  • u₁(Swerve, q) = 0·q + (−1)(1 − q) = q − 1
  • u₁(Straight, q) = 2q − 6(1 − q) = 8q − 6
  • q − 1 = 8q − 6 ⇒ 7q = 5 ⇒ q* = 5/7

Since the game is symmetric, p* = 5/7. The expected payoff is u₁ = u₂ = 5/7 − 1 = −2/7.

c) Collision: (2/7)² = 4/49 (≈ 8.2%). Both swerve: (5/7)² = 25/49.

Classic Question · Battle of the Sexes

Question 20: A Battle of the Sexes Variant

row ╲ columnAB
A(5, 2)(0, 0)
B(0, 0)(1, 3)

a) Find the pure Nash equilibria.
b) In the mixed equilibrium, what are p = P(row A) and q = P(column A)? What are the expected payoffs?
c) In the mixed equilibrium, what is the probability that they fail to meet?

Show answer

a) (A, A) = (5, 2) and (B, B) = (1, 3).

b) The q that makes the row player indifferent:

  • u₁(A, q) = 5q, u₁(B, q) = 1 − q ⇒ 6q = 1 ⇒ q* = 1/6

The p that makes the column player indifferent:

  • u₂(p, A) = 2p, u₂(p, B) = 3(1 − p) ⇒ 5p = 3 ⇒ p* = 3/5

u₁ = 5·(1/6) = 5/6, u₂ = 2·(3/5) = 6/5: both are below their worst payoffs in the pure equilibria (1 and 2).

c) p(1 − q) + (1 − p)q = (3/5)(5/6) + (2/5)(1/6) = 1/2 + 1/15 = 17/30 (≈ 56.7%).

Classic Question · Mixed equilibrium

Question 21: The Inspection Game

The wage is 6, the cost of effort is 2, the output is 10, and the cost of inspection is 2. A shirker who is caught gets no wage. Row: worker, column: manager.

InspectDon't inspect
Work(4, 2)(4, 4)
Shirk(0, −2)(6, −6)

a) Why is there no pure equilibrium?
b) For p = P(Work) and q = P(Inspect), find the mixed equilibrium and the expected payoffs.
c) What changes if a caught shirker also pays a penalty of 3 (the worker gets −3)?

Show answer

a) The best responses form a cycle: Work → manager Doesn't inspect (4 > 2) → worker Shirks (6 > 4) → manager Inspects (−2 > −6) → worker Works (4 > 0).

b) Worker indifferent: E[Work] = 4, E[Shirk] = 6(1 − q) ⇒ q* = 1/3.

Manager indifferent: E[Inspect] = 2p − 2(1 − p) = 4p − 2; E[Don't inspect] = 4p − 6(1 − p) = 10p − 6 ⇒ p* = 2/3.

Payoffs: worker 4, manager 4·(2/3) − 2 = 2/3.

c) Worker: 4 = −3q + 6(1 − q) ⇒ q* = 2/9; since the manager's payoffs do not change, p* = 2/3 stays the same. The penalty reduces inspection; it does not increase work.

Classic Question · Correlated equilibrium

Question 22: Checking a Correlated Equilibrium

The intersection (Chicken) game: (Swerve, Swerve) = (0, 0), (Swerve, Straight) = (−1, 1), (Straight, Swerve) = (1, −1), (Straight, Straight) = (−10, −10).

a) A mediator draws a cell from the distribution μ(Swerve, Swerve) = 1/2, μ(Swerve, Straight) = μ(Straight, Swerve) = 1/4 and tells each driver only their own recommendation. Is this a correlated equilibrium? What is the expected payoff?
b) What if μ(Swerve, Swerve) = 0, μ(Swerve, Straight) = μ(Straight, Swerve) = 9/20, μ(Straight, Straight) = 1/10?

Show answer

a) Recommendation Swerve: by Bayes the opponent swerves with (1/2)/(3/4) = 2/3 and goes straight with 1/3.

  • Obeying: (2/3)·0 + (1/3)·(−1) = −1/3; deviating (Straight): (2/3)·1 + (1/3)·(−10) = −8/3 ⇒ obey.

Recommendation Straight: the opponent has surely been told Swerve; obeying 1 > deviating 0.

Deviating from no recommendation pays ⇒ a correlated equilibrium. The payoff is (1/2)·0 + (1/4)(−1) + (1/4)(1) = 0; there is no collision.

b) Recommendation Straight: the opponent swerves with 9/11 and goes straight with 2/11. Obeying: 9/11 − 20/11 = −1; deviating (Swerve): (2/11)(−1) = −2/11 > −1. Deviating pays ⇒ not a correlated equilibrium.

Classic Question · Maximin and minimax

Question 23: Saddle Point and the Value of the Game

In zero-sum games the cells are the row player's payoff.

a) For A = [[5, 2, 6], [4, 3, 7], [1, 0, 8]], find the maximin and the minimax. Is there a saddle point? What is the value of the game?

b) Answer the same question for B = [[4, 1], [2, 3]]. If there is no saddle point, find the row player's probability p* of playing row 1 and the value of the game by indifference.

Show answer

a) The row minima are 2, 3, 0 → maximin 3. The column maxima are 5, 3, 8 → minimax 3. They are equal: a saddle point at (row 2, column 2), value v = 3.

b) The row minima are 1, 2 → maximin 2. The column maxima are 4, 3 → minimax 3. 2 < 3: no saddle point.

  • Against column 1: 4p + 2(1 − p) = 2 + 2p
  • Against column 2: p + 3(1 − p) = 3 − 2p
  • Equate: 2 + 2p = 3 − 2p ⇒ p* = 1/4, v = 5/2

The column player, with q on column 1: 4q + (1 − q) = 2q + 3(1 − q) ⇒ q* = 1/2; the value is again 5/2. Maximin (2) < v < minimax (3).

Classic Question · maximin()

Question 24: Maximin in a 2 × 3 Game

Zero-sum A = [[3, 1, 0], [2, 3, 6]]; the rows are row 1 and row 2, the columns are X, Y, Z. The row player plays row 1 with probability p.

a) What are the pure maximin and minimax?
b) Write the line for each column, find the candidate intersections, and compute the largest value of the lower envelope g(p) = minj(…).
c) What is the column player's minimax strategy? Which column is never played?

Show answer

a) The row minima are 0, 2 ⇒ maximin 2; the column maxima are 3, 3, 6 ⇒ minimax 3. No saddle point.

b) X: 2 + p · Y: 3 − 2p · Z: 6 − 6p. Intersections: X∩Y p = 1/3, X∩Z p = 4/7, Y∩Z p = 3/4.

g(0) = 2, g(1) = 0, g(1/3) = 7/3, g(4/7) = 13/7, g(3/4) = 3/2 ⇒ p* = 1/3, v = 7/3.

c) At p* = 1/3, Z = 4 > 7/3: Z is never played. With X at y: 3y + (1 − y) = 2y + 3(1 − y) ⇒ y = 2/3 (X 2/3, Y 1/3); the value is again 7/3.

Classic Question · Domination

Question 25: Domination by a Mixed Strategy

The row player's payoffs (columns Left, Right):

u1LeftRight
Top60
Middle22
Bottom06

a) Does any pure strategy strictly dominate Middle?
b) Does the strategy that mixes Top and Bottom ½–½ dominate Middle?
c) Which mixtures that play Top with p and Bottom with 1 − p strictly dominate Middle?

Show answer

a) No: Top gives 0 < 2 against Right; Bottom gives 0 < 2 against Left. No pure row is better in every column.

b) Yes: the ½–½ mixture gives ½·6 + ½·0 = 3 against Left and 3 against Right; 3 > 2 in every column ⇒ Middle is strictly dominated and deleted.

c) Against Left, 6p > 2 ⇒ p > 1/3; against Right, 6(1 − p) > 2 ⇒ p < 2/3. So every mixture with 1/3 < p < 2/3 dominates Middle.

Conclusion: Middle is not a best response to any belief; a rational player does not play it.

Classic Question · Equilibrium selection

Question 26: Risk Dominance

A version of Stag Hunt: hunting the stag together gives (5, 5); a player who goes for the hare gets 3 in every case; a player who goes for the stag alone gets 0.

row ╲ columnStagHare
Stag(5, 5)(0, 3)
Hare(3, 0)(3, 3)

a) Which are the pure equilibria, and which is Pareto dominant?
b) For each equilibrium, find the product of the deviation losses. Which is risk dominant?
c) In the mixed equilibrium, what is the opponent's probability q* of stag?

Show answer

a) (Stag, Stag) and (Hare, Hare). (5, 5) > (3, 3): (Stag, Stag) is Pareto dominant.

b) Deviating to hare from (S, S) takes 5 → 3, a loss of 2; the product is 2 · 2 = 4. Deviating to stag from (H, H) takes 3 → 0, a loss of 3; the product is 3 · 3 = 9. The one with the larger product, (Hare, Hare), is risk dominant.

c) 5q = 3 ⇒ q* = 3/5: stag is a best response only if one believes the partner will come with probability at least 60%. The Pareto criterion picks stag, the risk criterion picks hare.

Multiple Choice · Bertrand

Question 27

Two firms sell the same good at a unit cost of 10 TL. They choose prices simultaneously; the cheaper one takes the whole market, and at equal prices the market is split in half. Prices can be chosen continuously. Which is the Nash equilibrium?

The correct answer is C. If the common price is above 10 TL, one firm can undercut slightly, take the whole market, and almost double its profit: such a profile cannot be an equilibrium (A). Below 10 means a loss (D). If one is at 10 and the other above, the firm at 10 can raise its price a little, still take the whole market, and make a profit (B). The only equilibrium is p₁ = p₂ = 10 with profit 0: the Bertrand paradox.

Classic Question · Security games

Question 28: A Security Game

An attacker who hits an unprotected target gains that target's value; one who hits a protected target gets 0. The attacker observes the protection probabilities and picks the best target.

a) How many pure strategies does a defender who protects k = 3 of n = 8 targets have?
b) One guard, three targets: A has value 6, B 3, C 2. What protection probabilities cA, cB, cC minimize the attacker's payoff, and what is the attacker's payoff?
c) If the guard always protects A, what does the attacker gain?

Show answer

a) C(8, 3) = 8·7·6 / (3·2·1) = 56 pure strategies.

b) The payoff from attacking a target is value·(1 − protection). Indifference: 6(1 − cA) = 3(1 − cB) = 2(1 − cC) = V and cA + cB + cC = 1.

ci = 1 − V/value ⇒ 3 − V(1/6 + 1/3 + 1/2) = 1 ⇒ V = 2; cA = 2/3, cB = 1/3, cC = 0.

The attacker gets 2 from every target: 6·(1/3) = 3·(2/3) = 2·1. V < 2 is impossible: the protections needed would sum to 3 − V > 1.

c) B (3) and C (2) are left open: the attacker hits B and gains 3. A randomized patrol lowers this to 2.

Multiple Choice · Three-player game

Question 29

Three students simultaneously say Join or Don't join. The assignment succeeds only if all three join, and then everyone gets 5 points; joining costs 2. How many pure Nash equilibria are there?

The correct answer is D. k = 3: everyone gets 5 − 2 = 3; a player who drops out ruins the assignment and gets 0 (0 < 3) ⇒ equilibrium. k = 2: those who join get −2; if one drops out, 0 > −2 ⇒ not an equilibrium. k = 1: the one who joins gets −2, and 0 if they drop out ⇒ not an equilibrium. k = 0: a player who joins alone gets −2 < 0 ⇒ equilibrium. Two pure equilibria: (Join, Join, Join) and (Don't join, Don't join, Don't join). A is the answer to the lecture's "at least two people" game; here, exactly two who join pay the cost for nothing (C is wrong too). B forgets the coordination failure in which nobody joins.

Multiple Choice · mixed_2x2()

Question 30

The lecture's mixed_2x2(A, B) is called with A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]] (row player) and B = [[1, 3], [4, 0]] (column player). What does the function return? (Fractions are written in short form.)

The correct answer is A. da = 4 − 1 − 2 + 3 = 4 ≠ 0; q = (3 − 1)/4 = 1/2. db = 1 − 3 − 4 + 0 = −6 ≠ 0; p = (0 − 4)/(−6) = 2/3. Since 0 < p, q < 1, the check passes; a negative denominator is no problem (D is wrong; the game also has no pure equilibrium, as the best responses form a cycle). u1 = 4·(1/2) + 1·(1/2) = 5/2. The result is (2/3, 1/2, 5/2). B mixes up p and q: q is the column probability that makes the row player indifferent. C writes the column player's payoff (3p = 2).
