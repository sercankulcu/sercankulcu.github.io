Multiple Choice · Social choice

Question 1

What is the difference between majority and plurality?

The correct answer is C. Majority requires more than half of the votes to win; if the threshold is not reached, there is no winner. Plurality picks the candidate with the most first choices, who need not pass one half: in a profile of 13 voters, for example, A wins the plurality with 6/13 votes but has no majority. B reverses the definitions, and so does A, which is therefore wrong. D is wrong: in both rules a voter names just one candidate.

Multiple Choice · Scoring and IRV

Question 2

Which of the following is not a positional scoring rule?

The correct answer is A. A positional scoring rule gives a fixed score to each rank, s = (s1 ≥ s2 ≥ … ≥ sm), and the candidate with the highest total wins. Plurality (1, 0, …, 0), veto (1, …, 1, 0) and Borda (m − 1, …, 0) belong to this family. Instant-runoff voting, by contrast, eliminates the candidate with the fewest votes round by round and transfers votes; a candidate's count depends on which candidates have been eliminated, so it cannot be written with a single score vector. One more difference: every scoring rule is monotone, IRV is not.

Multiple Choice · Rules and criteria

Question 3

Of instant-runoff, Borda, plurality and approval voting, which can violate monotonicity (moving a candidate up on a ballot can make them lose)?

The correct answer is A. For example, in the profile 6 × A≻B≻C, 6 × B≻C≻A, 5 × C≻A≻B, IRV first eliminates C and A wins 11–6. If 2 voters with B≻C≻A move A to the top, the first choices become A 8, B 4, C 5; this time B is eliminated, its votes go to C, and C wins 9 – 8 over A. A lost because it gained support: instant-runoff voting violates monotonicity. Borda, plurality and approval add up points; moving a candidate up only raises that candidate's own score.

Multiple Choice · Rules and criteria

Question 4

Which rule can pick the Condorcet loser as the winner?

The correct answer is D. For example, in the profile 4 × A≻D≻B≻C, 4 × C≻B≻D≻A, 3 × D≻B≻C≻A, 2 × A≻B≻C≻D, plurality picks A with 6 first choices; yet A loses 6–7 to B, to C and to D in pairwise comparisons: plurality can pick the Condorcet loser. Borda never does this: the Borda score of a Condorcet loser is always below the average score of the candidates. In Copeland the loser's score is 0, and Kemeny puts it at the end of the ranking; neither picks it.

Multiple Choice · Strategic voting

Question 5

9 voters: 4 × A≻B≻C, 3 × C≻B≻A, 2 × B≻C≻A. The truthful Borda winner is B. What happens if the A voters bury B and report A≻C≻B?

The correct answer is B. Truthful Borda (2, 1, 0): A = 4 · 2 = 8, B = 4 · 1 + 3 · 1 + 2 · 2 = 11, C = 3 · 2 + 2 · 1 = 8 ⇒ B wins. When the A voters report A≻C≻B, C gains 4 points and B loses 4: A 8, B 7, C 12 ⇒ C wins. The true order of the A voters is A≻B≻C, so C is their worst candidate: burying backfired. A is wrong, because A's score never changes (8).

Multiple Choice · Arrow and IIA

Question 6

Which of these is not one of the conditions in Arrow's impossibility theorem?

The correct answer is D. Arrow's theorem sets four conditions for rules that produce a social ranking: universal domain, Pareto (unanimity), independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) and no dictator. With at least 3 candidates, no rule satisfies all four together. Strategy-proofness is not an Arrow condition; it is the subject of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, which studies the incentive to lie under rules that pick a single winner. A, B and C are among Arrow's conditions.

Multiple Choice · Arrow and IIA

Question 7

Profile: 4 × A≻D≻B≻C, 4 × C≻B≻D≻A, 3 × D≻B≻C≻A, 2 × A≻B≻C≻D. Under Borda, B 22 > D 21. If C drops out, what is the Borda (2, 1, 0) result?

The correct answer is A. When C drops out, the rankings become 4 × A≻D≻B, 4 × B≻D≻A, 3 × D≻B≻A, 2 × A≻B≻D. Borda (2, 1, 0): D = 4 + 4 + 3 · 2 = 14, B = 4 · 2 + 3 + 2 = 13, A = 4 · 2 + 2 · 2 = 12. D 14 > B 13: although no voter's B–D order changed, the social order flipped, so Borda violates IIA. Option C forgets that the score scale also shrinks when there are fewer candidates; B and D contradict the calculation.

Multiple Choice · May and single-peakedness

Question 8

Which condition of May's theorem does the rule “the status quo changes only with a ⅔ vote” violate?

The correct answer is B. Neutrality requires the two candidates to be treated symmetrically: if their names are swapped, the outcome must swap too. Under “the status quo changes only with a ⅔ vote”, a change needs ⅔, while keeping the status quo takes just over ⅓ of the votes; the rule favors the status quo and violates neutrality. Anonymity is preserved (who voted how does not matter), and so is positive responsiveness. Surjectivity (every candidate can win) is a condition of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, not of May's.

Multiple Choice · May and single-peakedness

Question 9

Five voters with single-peaked preferences have peaks at 2, 3, 7, 8, 9 on the axis. Which position is the Condorcet winner?

The correct answer is C. For single-peaked preferences the median voter theorem holds: the peaks are 2, 3, 7, 8, 9, and the median of 5 voters is the 3rd peak, that is, 7. Against any position to its left, the 3 voters with peaks 7, 8, 9 choose 7; against any position to its right, the 3 voters with peaks 2, 3, 7 choose 7: 7 beats every rival 3–2. A (the mean, 5.8) is a trap: against 7 it gets only the votes of the voters with peaks 2 and 3. B and D are extreme peaks and lose to positions in the middle.

Multiple Choice · Approval voting

Question 18

9 voters: 4 × X≻Y≻Z, 3 × Y≻Z≻X, 2 × Z≻Y≻X. In approval voting, who wins if everyone approves only their first choice, and if everyone approves their top two?

The correct answer is C. When only the first choice is approved, approval voting reduces to plurality: X 4, Y 3, Z 2 → X. When the top two are approved (2-approval): X 4, Y 4 + 3 + 2 = 9, Z 3 + 2 = 5 → Y. In approval voting the outcome depends on the voters' approval threshold, and the threshold is itself a strategic choice: even if the 4 voters who support X under top-two approval stopped approving Y, Y would drop to 5 and tie with Z, and X (4) would still not win. Here Y is also the Condorcet winner (5–4 over X, 7–2 over Z).

Multiple Choice · Gibbard–Satterthwaite

Question 19

The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that with at least 3 candidates every strategy-proof surjective rule is a dictatorship. Which change escapes this result?

The correct answer is B. The theorem rests on three conditions: at least three candidates, every candidate able to win (surjectivity), and every preference ranking being allowed. Once the domain is restricted to single-peaked preferences, the rule that picks the median voter's peak is strategy-proof (Black, Moulin). Having only two candidates is another way out (majority rule). A is wrong: the theorem holds for any number of voters; in C plurality can be manipulated too; D ballot secrecy does not remove the incentive to lie.

Multiple Choice · Judgment aggregation

Question 20

A three-member committee rejects a project if p or q is true (c = p ∨ q, that is, p or q). Member 1: p yes, q no; member 2: p no, q yes; member 3: p no, q no. What is the decision under premise-based (p and q each by majority) and conclusion-based (c directly by majority) procedures?

The correct answer is D. When the premises are voted on one by one, p is rejected 1–2 and q is rejected 1–2, so p ∨ q comes out false: no rejection. When the conclusion is voted on directly, members 1 and 2 find c true (2–1): reject. Each member is consistent, but the majority says “p false, q false, p ∨ q true”, which is an inconsistent collective judgment; the decision depends on the procedure (the doctrinal paradox, Kornhauser and Sager 1986).

Multiple Choice · Proportional representation

Question 21

5 seats; votes A 46,000, B 29,000, C 16,000, D 9,000. What is the seat allocation under the D'Hondt method?

The correct answer is A. Quotients: A 46,000, 23,000, 15,333.3; B 29,000, 14,500; C 16,000, 8,000; D 9,000. The five largest quotients are 46,000 (A), 29,000 (B), 23,000 (A), 16,000 (C), 15,333.3 (A) → A 3, B 1, C 1. For the fifth seat, A's third quotient (15,333.3) narrowly beats B's second (14,500). With 46% of the votes, A takes 60% of the seats: D'Hondt favors large parties.

Classic Question · Single-peaked preferences

Question 23: Lying under the Median Rule

Five board members vote on a budget amount (million TL, between 0 and 100). Preferences are single-peaked; the peaks are 20, 35, 50, 60, 90. Each member's loss is the distance from the chosen amount to their peak. Rule: the median of the reported peaks is chosen.

(a) What is the outcome if everyone is truthful? (b) What happens if the member with peak 90 reports 100 or 40? (c) For every possible report r by the member with peak 20, find the outcome; does lying help? (d) What happens if, under the mean rule, the member with peak 20 reports 0?

Show answer

(a) Sorted peaks 20, 35, 50, 60, 90: the median is 50.

(b) 100: order 20, 35, 50, 60, 100, the median is still 50. 40: order 20, 35, 40, 50, 60, the median is 40; the distance grows from 40 to 50. Exaggerating in one's own direction does not change the outcome, and going the opposite way moves the outcome further from the true peak.

  • (c) r ≤ 50: the others are 35, 50, 60, 90 ⇒ the median is 50 (loss 30)
  • 50 < r ≤ 60: the median is r; r > 60: the median is 60 (the loss rises from 30 up to 40)

No report brings the loss below 30: truthfulness is a dominant strategy.

(d) The truthful mean is 255/5 = 51; reporting 0 gives 235/5 = 47, and the loss falls from 31 to 27: the mean can be manipulated.
