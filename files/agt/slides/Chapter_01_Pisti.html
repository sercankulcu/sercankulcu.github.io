Chapter 1 · Game

Pisti

← Chapter 1
You 0 Computer 0 Deck 52

    How to play

    1. Four cards are placed on the table (only the top one is face up) and each player is dealt four cards. Players take turns putting one card on top of the pile.
    2. If the card played matches the rank of the top card, the whole pile is captured.
    3. A Jack captures the pile whatever the top card is.
    4. Capturing a single card with a card of the same rank is a Pisti: 10 points (a Jack on a lone Jack is 20 points).
    5. When both hands are empty, four more cards are dealt to each player until the deck runs out. The player who made the last capture takes the cards left on the table.
    6. Scoring: each Ace and each Jack is 1 point, 2♣ is 2 points, 10♦ is 3 points; the player who captures the most cards gets 3 points. The score badges show the Pisti and card points collected so far; the most-cards bonus is added at the end.

    Modes

    • Human vs Computer: the bottom hand is yours; the computer's hand stays face down.
    • Human vs Human: two people share the screen. After each move the next hand stays hidden until its owner presses “Show my hand”.

    Computer styles

    • Random: plays any card.
    • Opportunist: captures with a card matching the top card, otherwise with a Jack; if it cannot capture, it discards its least valuable card.
    • Cautious: captures with a matching card, uses a Jack only on a valuable pile, and discards ranks that have often been seen, so the opponent is less likely to hold a match.

    What to watch for

    The important information is not only the cards in hand but the visible history of the cards already played. A card that looks safe now may give your opponent an easy capture next turn. Because a Jack captures any exposed pile, using it too early can sacrifice future value.

    Strategy tips

    • Capturing a valuable pile is good, but do not spend a Jack on a small pile.
    • Remembering which ranks have already appeared helps you estimate whether a matching card can still come.
    • If you cannot capture, play the card that gives your opponent the least attractive opportunity.

    Link to game theory

    Pisti combines strategic interaction with incomplete information. Players see the shared pile and their own cards, but not the opponent's hand or the cards to come. This makes hidden information, belief formation, tactical sacrifice and the value of remembering revealed moves concrete.

    The computer applies a rule; it does not search a tree

    In Pisti the computer does not look ahead like Minimax or Alpha-Beta: it cannot even build a game tree, because it does not know the opponent’s hand or the cards still in the deck. Instead the function botPlay tries fixed, ordered rules from top to bottom and plays the first one that applies. There are three styles:

    • Random: one of the cards in its hand, with equal probability.
    • Opportunist and Cautious: the three rules below, in this order.
    1. Match. If the table is not empty and it holds a card of the same rank as the top card, it plays the first such card in its hand and captures the pile. (If the top card is a Jack the match is a Jack; with a single card in the pile this is a Pisti.)
    2. Jack. If it holds a Jack and the table is not empty: the Opportunist always plays it; the Cautious player plays it only if pile value ≥ 2 or if it has nothing but Jacks left.
    3. Discard. If it cannot capture, it discards the card with the lowest score among the non-Jacks (all cards if only Jacks are left).

    discard score = 4 × cardPoints  (− 2 × seen[rank], Cautious only)  +  random number in [0, 0.5)

    pile value (pileValue) = the card points in the pile + 0.25 × number of cards + (10 if the pile has exactly one card, for the Pisti chance). cardPoints: 10♦ → 3, 2♣ → 2, Ace and Jack → 1, all others 0. seen[rank] is the number of cards of that rank that have been played face up so far (including the first top card of the table and the computer’s own discards; its hand and the three face-down table cards are not counted). The random number only decides between exactly equal scores, because different scores differ by at least 2.

    Is this rule optimal?

    No. The rule is simple and usually plays sensibly, but it has gaps:

    • Counting is approximate. seen looks only at ranks and ignores the cards in the computer’s own hand. The chance that the opponent holds a card of a rank can be computed from how many cards of that rank are still unknown (4 − seen − in my own hand).
    • It ignores the size of the pile. The discard score does not consider what the card leaves on top. Discarding onto an empty table gives the opponent a single-card pile, a 10-point Pisti chance; discarding onto a big pile can hand over many cards and points. The same score is used for both.
    • The Jack rule is crude. The pile value adds +10 for a single card (the Pisti chance), but a Jack does not make a Pisti on a single card (only a Jack on a Jack does). So the Cautious player may spend a Jack on a lone worthless card.
    • No deck or key-card counting. It does not track how many Jacks, 2♣, 10♦ remain, or how many cards are left in the deck (who will take the cards left on the table).
    • No inference from the opponent. If the opponent does not take a pile, it may not hold that rank; the computer never uses this.

    What would an ideal player do?

    A card counter computes the expected loss of every candidate discard and picks the smallest. Let U be the number of cards unknown from its own point of view, k the number of unknown cards of the rank it would throw, and the opponent’s hand 4 cards. The probability that the opponent holds at least one card of that rank is

    P = 1 − C(U − k, 4) / C(U, 4)

    (the Jack probability is added in the same way). Expected loss ≈ P × (value of the pile the card leaves). In the example above let U = 29: for 7s there is 1 unknown card (4 − 2 seen − the 7♥ in hand) → P = 4/29 ≈ 14%; for Kings there are 3 unknown (the K♣ in hand is counted) → P ≈ 37%; for Aces 2 unknown → P ≈ 26%. Here the order agrees with the computer’s (the 7♥ goes). But the computer’s counter does not count the K♣ in its own hand, so it sees the Kings as 4 unknown cards (P ≈ 47%), and its score gaps do not correspond to a probability. The rule only imitates this computation with the hunch “a rank that has been seen often is safe”.

    Game-theory link: this is the expected value computation of Chapter 1 · Decision Theory; the difference is that the probability comes from hidden information (unseen cards). The computer’s seen counter is a very crude version of that belief.

    1. Pile value: pileValue

    function pileValue() {
  return table.reduce((s, c) => s + cardPoints(c), 0)  // points in the pile
       + table.length * .25                            // every card is worth a little
       + (table.length === 1 ? 10 : 0);                // single card: Pisti chance
}

    2. The computer’s move: botPlay

    Three rules from top to bottom: match, Jack, discard. seen counts the face-up cards per rank.

    function botPlay() {
  const hand = hands[1];
  const style = aiStyle.value;     // 'Random' | 'Conservative' | 'Opportunistic'
  const top = table[table.length - 1];
  let idx;                         // index of the card to play
  if (style === 'Random') {
    idx = Math.floor(Math.random() * hand.length);
  } else {
    const matches = hand.map((c, i) => top && c.r === top.r ? i : -1).filter(i => i >= 0);
    const jacks = hand.map((c, i) => c.r === 'J' ? i : -1).filter(i => i >= 0);
    const discardOrder = hand.map((c, i) => i).filter(i => hand[i].r !== 'J');
    if (matches.length) {
      idx = matches[0];            // 1. match: capture the pile
    } else if (jacks.length && top &&
               (style === 'Opportunistic' || pileValue() >= 2 || hand.length === jacks.length)) {
      idx = jacks[0];              // 2. Jack: capture with the Jack
    } else {                       // 3. discard: the lowest-scoring card
      const pool = discardOrder.length ? discardOrder : hand.map((c, i) => i);
      const score = i => {
        const c = hand[i];
        let v = cardPoints(c) * 4;
        if (style === 'Conservative') v -= (seen[c.r] || 0) * 2;  // an often-seen rank is safe
        return v + Math.random() * .5;                            // tie-breaker
      };
      idx = pool.slice().sort((a, b) => score(a) - score(b))[0];
    }
  }
  const card = hand.splice(idx, 1)[0];
  playCard(1, card);
  ...
}

    The same idea in Python

    A short version in the style of the course’s Python repository (card = (rank, suit)). At the end is the expected loss that an ideal player would use; the computer on this page does not use it.

    import random
from math import comb

def card_points(c):                      # c = (rank, suit)
    if c == ("10", "♦"):
        return 3
    if c == ("2", "♣"):
        return 2
    return 1 if c[0] in ("A", "J") else 0

def pile_value(table):
    return (sum(card_points(c) for c in table)
            + 0.25 * len(table)
            + (10 if len(table) == 1 else 0))       # single card: Pisti chance

def choose_card(hand, table, seen, style):
    top = table[-1] if table else None
    if style == "random":
        return random.choice(hand)
    matches = [c for c in hand if top and c[0] == top[0]]
    jacks = [c for c in hand if c[0] == "J"]
    if matches:                                     # 1. match
        return matches[0]
    if jacks and top and (style == "opportunist"
                          or pile_value(table) >= 2
                          or len(jacks) == len(hand)):
        return jacks[0]                             # 2. Jack
    pool = [c for c in hand if c[0] != "J"] or hand # 3. discard
    def score(c):
        s = 4 * card_points(c)
        if style == "cautious":
            s -= 2 * seen.get(c[0], 0)
        return s + random.random() * 0.5
    return min(pool, key=score)

# The ideal player's criterion (the computer on this page does not use it)
def opponent_holds(k_unknown, u_unknown, hand_size=4):
    return 1 - comb(u_unknown - k_unknown, hand_size) / comb(u_unknown, hand_size)

def expected_loss(card, table, seen, own_hand, u):
    left = 4 - seen.get(card[0], 0) - sum(1 for c in own_hand if c[0] == card[0])
    return opponent_holds(left, u) * pile_value(table + [card])

# instead of choose_card: min(hand, key=lambda c: expected_loss(c, table, seen, hand, u))

    Discussion questions

    • What is the Cautious computer’s “seen” counter trying to estimate, and why is it only approximate?
      Answer

      Whether the opponent holds a card of that rank: the more cards of a rank that have been seen face up (4 − seen), the less likely the opponent has one and the safer it is to discard that rank. It is approximate because the computer does not count its own hand, cannot know the three face-down table cards, and puts the count straight into a score instead of computing a probability. An ideal player computes P = 1 − C(U − k, 4)/C(U, 4).

    • Why does the computer try the match before the Jack? Is that always right?
      Answer

      If you capture with the matching card, the Jack stays in your hand for a more valuable pile; most of the time that is right. Its limit: if the pile is nearly worthless and you expect your opponent to build a big pile on the next turn, using the Jack at once or waiting for a matching card may be better; the computer does no such calculation. “Cheap first” is a hunch, not a proven optimum.

    • The Jack rule adds +10 to the pile value for a single card. How does that mislead the computer?
      Answer

      The +10 is meant to account for the chance of a Pisti with a matching card on a single-card pile. But a Jack does not make a Pisti on a single card (a Jack only makes the 20-point Pisti on a Jack). Because the value therefore exceeds 10, the Cautious player spends a Jack even on a lone card; the gain is just 1–2 cards and the Jack’s own 1 point. In expected-value terms a Jack saved for a valuable pile usually earns more.

    • The Opportunist follows “capture every pile with the Jack”. How can a predictable opponent be exploited?
      Answer

      Because the rule is deterministic, the opponent knows in advance what the computer will do: you can leave a small card on the table to make the computer burn its Jack cheaply, after which it cannot take a big pile without one. This is the opposite of the mixed strategy idea in Chapter 3: a predictable player is exploited; randomizing (or computing according to the situation) is the defense.
