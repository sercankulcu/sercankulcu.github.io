    How to play

    You play the black pieces from the bottom, the computer plays the red pieces from the top. You move first.

    • Click one of your pieces; the squares it can move to are marked with dots. Then click the target square.
    • Men move one square diagonally, forward only, on the dark squares.
    • Capturing is mandatory: if you can jump over an opponent's piece to an empty square, you must. Pieces that can move are marked with a ring.
    • If the same piece can jump again after a jump, you must continue (multi-jump); click the landing square of each jump in turn.
    • A piece that reaches the far row becomes a king (gold ring): a king moves one square diagonally forward or backward and can also capture backward.
    • The side that cannot move or has lost all its pieces loses. If 50 moves pass with no capture and no promotion, the game is a draw.
    • Undo takes back your last move and the computer's reply.
    • The Difficulty menu sets how many half-moves (plies) the computer looks ahead: 2, 4, 6 or 8.

    If you tick Show move reasoning, each time the computer picks a move, the moves it evaluated and their scores are listed in the panel beside the board, and the squares where its last move started and ended get a blue frame. The panel is explained in the How the AI decides tab.

    What it shows

    Checkers is a sequential, zero-sum, perfect-information game. Because the game tree is huge, the computer does not search the whole tree but only to a limited depth, scoring the leaves with a heuristic evaluation. The deeper the search, the stronger the opponent, but the computational cost grows exponentially.

    The computer uses an iterative-deepening version of the method from the Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning lecture. To refresh it: Chapter 2 · Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning.

    What happens on every move?

    1. Legal moves are generated (legalMoves). Captures are collected first; if there is at least one capture, only captures are legal (mandatory capture). A multi-jump is a single move: path holds the squares landed on, caps the pieces taken.
    2. If there is only one legal move the computer plays it; there is nothing to search. The panel says so.
    3. Iterative deepening (chooseAiMove): depth 1, 2, 3, … up to the chosen limit (Easy 2, Medium 4, Hard 6, Very hard 8); for each root move search is called.
    4. After each depth the moves are sorted by score; the next depth starts with the move that looked best, so alpha-beta cuts earlier. Equal scores are ordered randomly (Math.random() - 0.5); that is why, in a position with two equally good moves, which one is picked can change from game to game.
    5. Time limit: when 1.5 seconds run out, the unfinished depth is thrown away and the best move of the last completed depth is played. If a win or loss is found (|score| > 9000), the search stops early.

    Search: minimax + alpha-beta

    search(b, depth, alpha, beta, side, ply) looks for the largest value on the computer's (red) moves and the smallest on yours (black); when alpha >= beta the remaining sibling moves are pruned. Scores are always from the computer's side: a large number favours the computer.

    • Terminal positions. The side with no legal move loses: −10000 + ply if the computer loses, 10000 − ply if you do. Thanks to the ply term, a quicker win scores higher and a later loss scores higher.
    • Capture extension. When the depth runs out and the side to move has a forced capture (moves[0].caps.length), one more ply is searched. So leaves are not scored in the middle of an exchange.
    • Window at the root. Root moves are searched in turn and the best value so far is kept in alpha (beta = +∞). A root move that is no better than the best so far is cut off early; its score in the panel is only an upper bound and is written ≤ v.

    Evaluation function

    A leaf is scored by adding up the pieces on the board (evaluate):

    TermPoints
    Man100 + 3 × advancement (0–7 rows beyond its starting row)
    King160
    Edge column (a or h)+2 (for men and kings)

    score = Σ (red pieces) − Σ (black pieces)

    Example. The starting position is symmetric: score 0. If you play c3–d4, one black man advances a row (3 points) and no edge changes: the score becomes −3. If red answers h6–g5, its man advances a row (+3) but leaves the edge column (−2); the total is −3 + 1 = −2. A king is worth far more than a man (160, against at most 120 for a man), which is why the search hunts for sequences that crown a piece.

    What the panel shows

    The same opening at three depths (move: c3–d4; the numbers are real output of this page, and node counts vary a little with the random tie-break in the ordering):

    The move that scores +8 at depth 2 drops to −1 at depth 4: a shallow search can be optimistic because of replies it does not see. This is also why the other rows in the panel are marked ≤: alpha-beta proves they are “no better than the chosen one” and stops there. Depth d/D in the panel header is the completed depth over the requested depth; if time runs out, d stays below D.

    Why these depths?

    • The opening has 7 legal moves; in randomly played games the average is about 5, and when a capture is available often only 1–3 remain. A branching factor of b ≈ 5–7 is a fair estimate.
    • An unpruned tree has bd leaves: for b = 6 about 1.7 million at depth 8. With good move ordering alpha-beta cuts that to roughly bd/2. In practice, from the opening position the search counts about 210 nodes for depth 4, 4500 for depth 6 and 55,000 for depth 8: every two extra plies multiply the node count by about 12–20.
    • A node costs only a few thousandths of a millisecond, so depth 8 fits within 1.5 seconds in most positions; in a middlegame with many moves it may not, and then the limit kicks in. That is why Very hard can finish at different depths depending on the computer's speed.

    1. Move generation: legalMoves and jumpsFrom

    First every piece's jumps are collected. If any jump exists only jumps are returned: mandatory capture is that one line.

    function legalMoves(b, side) {
    const captures = [];
    const steps = [];
    for (let i = 0; i < 64; i++) {
      const p = b[i];
      if (!p || colorOf(p) !== side) continue;
      const saved = b[i];
      b[i] = null;                              // so the piece does not block itself
      jumpsFrom(b, i, saved, [i], [], captures);
      b[i] = saved;
      if (captures.length) continue;            // a capture exists: skip plain steps
      const [r, c] = rc(i);
      for (const [dr, dc] of dirsFor(p)) {
        const tr = r + dr, tc = c + dc;
        if (inside(tr, tc) && !b[idx(tr, tc)])
          steps.push({ from: i, path: [i, idx(tr, tc)], caps: [] });
      }
    }
    return captures.length ? captures : steps;  // capturing is mandatory
  }
  
  // Recursive jump: the move is recorded at the end of the chain (multi-jump).
  function jumpsFrom(b, start, p, path, caps, out) {
    const [r, c] = rc(path[path.length - 1]);
    let extended = false;
    for (const [dr, dc] of dirsFor(p)) {
      const mr = r + dr, mc = c + dc, tr = r + 2 * dr, tc = c + 2 * dc;
      if (!inside(tr, tc)) continue;
      const mid = idx(mr, mc), to = idx(tr, tc);
      const victim = b[mid];
      if (!victim || colorOf(victim) === colorOf(p) || b[to] || caps.includes(mid)) continue;
      extended = true;                          // a jump is possible in this direction
      const nextPath = path.concat(to);
      const nextCaps = caps.concat(mid);
      const promotes = !isKing(p) && tr === promoteRow(colorOf(p));
      if (promotes) out.push({ from: start, path: nextPath, caps: nextCaps });  // crowning ends the chain
      else jumpsFrom(b, start, p, nextPath, nextCaps, out);
    }
    if (!extended && caps.length) out.push({ from: start, path, caps });
  }

    2. Evaluation: evaluate

    function evaluate(b) {
    let s = 0;
    for (let i = 0; i < 64; i++) {
      const p = b[i];
      if (!p) continue;
      const [r, c] = rc(i);
      // king 160; man 100 + 3 × advancement (r for red, N-1-r for black)
      let v = isKing(p) ? 160 : 100 + (colorOf(p) === 'w' ? r : N - 1 - r) * 3;
      if (c === 0 || c === N - 1) v += 2;       // edge column
      s += colorOf(p) === 'w' ? v : -v;         // red (computer) +, black −
    }
    return s;
  }

    3. Search: search (minimax + alpha-beta)

    function search(b, depth, alpha, beta, side, ply) {
    // time check: once every 1024 nodes
    if ((++nodes & 1023) === 0 && performance.now() > deadline) aborted = true;
    if (aborted) return 0;
    const moves = legalMoves(b, side);
    // the side with no move loses; ply: a quicker win is better
    if (!moves.length) return side === 'w' ? -10000 + ply : 10000 - ply;
    if (depth <= 0) {
      if (moves[0].caps.length) depth = 1;      // capture extension
      else return evaluate(b);                  // quiet position: score it
    }
    const other = side === 'w' ? 'b' : 'w';
    if (side === 'w') {                         // MAX node (computer)
      let best = -Infinity;
      for (const m of moves) {
        const v = search(applyMove(b, m), depth - 1, alpha, beta, other, ply + 1);
        if (v > best) best = v;
        if (best > alpha) alpha = best;
        if (alpha >= beta) break;               // cutoff
      }
      return best;
    }
    let best = Infinity;                        // MIN node (you)
    for (const m of moves) {
      const v = search(applyMove(b, m), depth - 1, alpha, beta, other, ply + 1);
      if (v < best) best = v;
      if (best < beta) beta = best;
      if (alpha >= beta) break;                 // cutoff
    }
    return best;
  }

    4. Root: chooseAiMove (iterative deepening)

    function chooseAiMove() {
    const maxDepth = parseInt(depthSelect.value, 10) || 4;
    let moves = legalMoves(board, 'w');
    if (moves.length === 1) { ...; return moves[0]; }   // one move: no search
    deadline = performance.now() + TIME_LIMIT;          // TIME_LIMIT = 1500 ms
    aborted = false;
    nodes = 0;
    let bestMove = moves[0];
    for (let d = 1; d <= maxDepth; d++) {               // depth 1, 2, ..., maxDepth
      let alpha = -Infinity;
      let iterBest = null;
      const scored = [];
      for (const m of moves) {
        const v = search(applyMove(board, m), d - 1, alpha, Infinity, 'b', 1);
        if (aborted) break;                             // time is up
        const better = iterBest === null || v > alpha;  // true: value is exact, not just a bound
        scored.push([v, m, better]);
        if (better) { alpha = v; iterBest = m; }
      }
      if (aborted) { ...; break; }                      // the unfinished depth is dropped
      bestMove = iterBest;                              // best of the completed depth
      ...
      // best first; random among equals (ordering for the next depth)
      scored.sort((x, y) => y[0] - x[0] || Math.random() - 0.5);
      moves = scored.map(s => s[1]);
      if (Math.abs(alpha) > 9000) break;                // a win/loss was found
    }
    ...
    return bestMove;
  }

    The same idea in Python

    The same pattern as alphabeta in the course's Python examples; the differences are the capture extension, the ply term and iterative deepening at the root. The board object is assumed to provide the move generation above and play to apply a move.

    from math import inf
  
  def evaluate(board):
      s = 0
      for p in board.pieces:
          v = 160 if p.king else 100 + 3 * p.advance
          if p.on_edge:
              v += 2
          s += v if p.red else -v              # + favours the computer (red)
      return s
  
  def alphabeta(board, d, a, b, maxi, ply):
      moves = board.legal_moves(maxi)          # only captures if any exist
      if not moves:                            # the side with no move loses
          return -10000 + ply if maxi else 10000 - ply
      if d <= 0:
          if moves[0].captures:
              d = 1                            # capture extension
          else:
              return evaluate(board)
      best = -inf if maxi else inf
      for m in moves:
          v = alphabeta(board.play(m), d - 1, a, b, not maxi, ply + 1)
          if maxi:
              best = max(best, v); a = max(a, best)
          else:
              best = min(best, v); b = min(b, best)
          if a >= b:
              break                            # cutoff
      return best
  
  def best_move(board, max_depth):
      moves = board.legal_moves(True)
      if len(moves) == 1:
          return moves[0]
      best = moves[0]
      for d in range(1, max_depth + 1):        # iterative deepening
          a, scored = -inf, []
          for m in moves:
              v = alphabeta(board.play(m), d - 1, a, inf, False, 1)
              scored.append((v, m))
              if v > a:
                  a, best = v, m
          scored.sort(key=lambda x: -x[0])     # best move first next round
          moves = [m for _, m in scored]
          if abs(a) > 9000:
              break
      return best

    Try it yourself

    1. Predict first, then look. Tick Show move reasoning and play a move. Before you see the reply, guess which move red will choose. Then look at the panel: is your guess the top row? What is the score gap between the first and second row? Why can't we know the exact value of a row marked ≤?
    2. Change the depth. Play the same opening (c3–d4) on Easy, Medium, Hard and Very hard, starting each time with New game. Write down the completed depth, the node count and the score of the chosen move from the panel. By roughly what factor does the node count grow for every two extra plies? At which depth does the score change, and does the move change?
    3. Watch the forced capture. Deliberately push a piece in front of the computer so that red has exactly one capture. What does the panel say? Why is no node count shown? Now set up a position where the computer has two different captures: how many rows are in the panel, and which ones say captures?
    4. Compute a score by hand. Play c3–d4 in a new game. Work out the static score of the position with the table in the How the AI decides tab: you should get −3. Then work out the score after red's b6–a5 (moves to the edge) and after d6–c5. Which helps the computer more, and why?

    Discussion questions

    • The depths in the Difficulty menu are all even (2, 4, 6, 8). Why would odd depths be less reliable?
      Answer

      With an even depth the search ends on the opponent's reply, so when the leaf is evaluated the opponent has had the last word. With an odd depth the search ends on the computer's own move: it sees a piece won but not the reply that takes it back, so it is optimistic (the horizon effect). The capture extension repairs this partly, but only for forced capture chains. The b6–c5 example in the panel shows a similar optimism: +8 at depth 2, −1 at depth 4.

    • The evaluation only looks at piece count, king value, advancement and the edge. What does it not know? How does that relate to search depth?
      Answer

      It knows nothing about mobility, control of the centre, protecting the back row, pieces backing each other up, or tempo. A deeper search makes up for some of this: if leaving the back row loses a piece a few moves later, that shows up in the score. So a weak evaluation can be covered by deep search, and a shallow search by a good evaluation. Serious checkers programs (such as Chinook) use both, plus opening books and endgame databases; English draughts was solved completely in 2007, and shown to be a draw with perfect play from both sides.

    • Iterative deepening runs shallow searches first. Why is that not a waste of time?
      Answer

      Because the tree grows exponentially, the last depth carries nearly all the cost: from the opening, about 4500 nodes for depth 6 against about 55,000 for depth 8. The earlier depths together are a small fraction of the last one. In return you get two things: ordering (the best move of the previous round is searched first, so alpha-beta cuts far more) and a time guarantee (even if time runs out, a completed depth's move is in hand).

    • On Very hard you may notice the computer choosing a different move in the same position. Why could that happen?
      Answer

      Two reasons. First, among equally scored moves the ordering is random (Math.random() - 0.5) and the first best one found is chosen. Second, the time limit: the search depends on wall-clock time, so a slow computer or a busy browser may not reach depth 8 and will play the depth-6 move. A program playing at different strengths on different machines is a natural consequence of time-limited search.
