How to play

A game built for this course: territory control and adversarial search in one.

  1. Red and Blue compete for territory on a 6×6 map. Red starts in the top-left corner, Blue in the bottom-right (each starts with two cells); Red moves first. In Human vs. AI mode you are Red and the AI is Blue.
  2. The player to move clicks an empty cell (dashed) that is horizontally or vertically adjacent to territory they control. Every move adds a single cell.
  3. Conversion: if the new cell touches at least two of the player’s cells, an adjacent, weakly supported enemy cell (touching at most one cell of its own colour) is captured and turns to the player’s colour. At most one cell is captured per move; if several qualify, the first one in the order below, above, right, left is taken.
  4. A player with no valid expansion passes; the other player moves again.
  5. The match ends when the board is full or neither player can expand. The player controlling more cells wins; equal counts are a draw.

The last move is shown with an amber outline, the captured cell with an outline in the text colour. The left panel shows both colours’ cell counts and whose turn it is.

AI difficulty

  • Easy: picks a random valid expansion.
  • Medium: a greedy positional heuristic: prefers cells with many friendly neighbours (3 points each) and few enemy neighbours.
  • Hard: adversarial search: minimax with alpha-beta pruning looks three plies ahead (its own move, your reply, its next move) and evaluates the territory difference and mobility (number of valid expansions).

Reading the territory

A cell is worth more than its immediate point. Central positions can open several expansion directions in the future, while narrow corridors can keep the opponent from reaching a whole region. Border cells have both present value and option value: they determine which moves will be possible later.

Strategy tips

  • Expand toward flexible central areas before committing too much to a single edge.
  • Build connected regions so that blocking one border does not stop all growth.
  • Watch for bottlenecks where a single move can cut off access to several cells.
  • Compare the immediate territory gain with the future moves each expansion creates or removes.

Link to algorithmic strategy

This version focuses on spatial control, heuristic evaluation and adversarial search. The small board makes it easy to observe how an evaluation function can reward territory, mobility, connectivity and future expansion instead of merely counting filled cells; Medium (one-step heuristic) and Hard (search) can be compared.

Tick Show move reasoning in the left panel and, each time the AI (Blue) moves, the moves it evaluated and their scores are shown under the board.

Three difficulties, three methods

The AI is always Blue. The difficulty selector decides the method; this tab is mostly about Hard, where the real search happens.

  • Easy: no evaluation at all; one of the valid expansions is chosen at random.
  • Medium: scores each candidate cell without looking ahead: score = 3 × (Blue neighbours of the cell) − (Red neighbours) + small noise. The highest-scoring cell is taken. Like the Backgammon AI, this is a one-step greedy heuristic.
  • Hard: minimax search with alpha-beta pruning, described below.

Hard: what is searched?

  1. Root. Each of Blue’s valid expansions is tried in turn (ply 1).
  2. Search. For each one, minimax(…, depth = 2) is called: Red tries all of its replies (ply 2), then Blue again tries all of its moves (ply 3). The search stops at a depth of three plies.
  3. Leaf. When the depth is used up, the position is scored by evaluate. Blue wants a large score (MAX), Red a small one (MIN); values travel from the leaves to the root by the minimax rule.
  4. Choice. The root move with the largest value is played.

The evaluation function

value = 10 × (Blue cells − Red cells) + 2 × (Blue’s valid expansions − Red’s valid expansions)

TermWhat it countsWeight
TerritoryBlue cells − Red cells (the real score)× 10
MobilityBlue’s valid expansions − Red’s (future options)× 2

A positive value means Blue is ahead, a negative one Red. Every move adds one cell, so the territory term often swings by ±10; mobility is the fine adjustment. If an expansion opens new border cells for Blue and cuts down Red’s options, the mobility term rewards it.

How is a finished game scored?

There is no separate large win/loss score. If the board is full or neither player can move, the search calls the same evaluate; since neither side has a valid move, the mobility term is zero and the value is exactly 10 × the final cell difference. If only one player cannot move, that player passes; a pass also counts as one ply (the depth drops by one) and the search continues with the other player.

Alpha-beta pruning and randomness

  • Inside the tree the alpha-beta rule from Chapter 2 is used: when alpha ≥ beta the remaining siblings are skipped. Moves are not ordered; they are tried in cell-number order (left to right, top to bottom). There is no transposition table.
  • Each root move enters the search with the full window (−∞, +∞); that is, the best value so far is not passed on as alpha at the root. So every root value in the panel is an exact value, not a bound (≤ or ≥). The price is slightly less pruning.
  • Math.random()*0.5 is added to each root value. All values are even integers (multiples of 10 and 2), so the smallest gap between different values is 2; the noise therefore only chooses among equal values and never reorders different ones.

Why depth 3?

A game lasts at most 32 moves (4 cells are filled at the start, 32 are empty) and each position has several moves. Against a randomly playing Red, in sample games Blue had about 6 valid expansions on average (at most 12). With a branching factor b ≈ 6, a search of d plies looks at roughly bd leaves in the worst case:

  • d = 3: 6³ ≈ 220 leaves. With alpha-beta, sample games needed on average about 100 evaluated positions per move (at most about 550); the answer is instant.
  • Searching to the end of the game (d ≈ 32) would need 6³² ≈ 10²⁵ leaves: impossible.

So the depth is a compromise that limits strength and makes the move instant; the search horizon is three plies, and anything beyond it is only guessed by the evaluate heuristic. Three is an odd number: the last ply is Blue’s, so Red’s reply to that move is never seen.

An example with numbers

Say Red opens at (1,2) (row 1, column 2). Blue has three valid expansions. As in the panel:

Blue moveNow (territory · mobility)3-ply search
(4,6)0 · +2 = 22
(5,5)0 · +2 = 24
(6,5)0 · 0 = 00

(5,5) and (4,6) have the same “now” score, but the search tells them apart. After (5,5), whatever Red replies (it has three replies), Blue can play (5,4). For example, if Red plays (1,3), each side has 4 cells: territory 10 × 0 = 0; Blue has 6 valid expansions and Red 4: 2 × (6 − 4) = 4. Total 4. After (4,6), by contrast, the value of the search is only 2. That is why (5,5) is chosen.

1. Move generation and applying a move

A valid move is an empty cell touching one of the player’s cells. Applying it also runs the capture rule. The search works on a copy of the board at every try.

const directions=[[1,0],[-1,0],[0,1],[0,-1]];     // down, up, right, left (tried in this order)

function adjacentOn(b,index,player){             // how many neighbours belong to player?
  return neighbors(index).filter(n=>b[n]===player).length;
}
function legalOn(b,player){                       // empty cells player may expand into
  const moves=[];
  for(let i=0;i<36;i++)
    if(!b[i]&&adjacentOn(b,i,player))moves.push(i);
  return moves;
}
function applyOn(b,index,player){                 // applies the move to b in place
  b[index]=player;                                // 1) take the cell
  const enemy=3-player;
  if(adjacentOn(b,index,player)>=2){              // 2) with at least two own neighbours
    for(const n of neighbors(index)){
      if(b[n]===enemy&&adjacentOn(b,n,enemy)<=1){ //    a weakly supported enemy cell
        b[n]=player;return n;                     //    is captured (at most one)
      }
    }
  }
  return -1;                                      // no capture
}

2. Evaluation and alpha-beta: evaluate, minimax

Blue is MAX, Red is MIN. At a leaf the formula is computed; at inner nodes the value is passed up and the loop is cut when alpha ≥ beta.

function evaluate(b){                             // Blue (2) is MAX, Red (1) is MIN
  evalCount++;                                    // counter only (for the reasoning panel)
  const c=counts(b);                              // c[0]: Red cells, c[1]: Blue cells
  return 10*(c[1]-c[0])+2*(legalOn(b,2).length-legalOn(b,1).length);
}

function minimax(b,player,depth,alpha,beta){
  const moves=legalOn(b,player);
  const empty=b.some(x=>!x);
  if(depth===0||!empty)return evaluate(b);        // depth used up, or board full
  if(!moves.length){                              // pass
    if(!legalOn(b,3-player).length)return evaluate(b);   // neither can move: game over
    return minimax(b,3-player,depth-1,alpha,beta);       // a pass also costs one ply
  }
  if(player===2){                                 // MAX node (Blue)
    let v=-Infinity;
    for(const m of moves){
      const nb=b.slice();applyOn(nb,m,2);
      v=Math.max(v,minimax(nb,1,depth-1,alpha,beta));
      alpha=Math.max(alpha,v);
      if(alpha>=beta)break;                       // cutoff
    }
    return v;
  }
  let v=Infinity;                                 // MIN node (Red)
  for(const m of moves){
    const nb=b.slice();applyOn(nb,m,1);
    v=Math.min(v,minimax(nb,2,depth-1,alpha,beta));
    beta=Math.min(beta,v);
    if(alpha>=beta)break;
  }
  return v;
}

3. The root: chooseAiMove

On Hard, each root move gets a full-window search, noise is added and the largest value is kept: an argmax.

function chooseAiMove(){
  const moves=legal(2);                           // Blue's valid expansions
  if(!moves.length)return -1;
  const diff=difficulty.value;
  /* ... recording for the reasoning panel: whyInfo ... */
  if(diff==='Easy')return moves[Math.floor(Math.random()*moves.length)];
  if(diff==='Medium'){
    const score=m=>adjacent(m,2)*3-adjacent(m,1)+Math.random()*.5;
    return moves.map(m=>[m,score(m)]).sort((a,b)=>b[1]-a[1])[0][0];
  }
  let best=-1,bv=-Infinity;                       // Hard: the root, a MAX node
  for(const m of moves){
    const nb=board.slice();applyOn(nb,m,2);       // ply 1: Blue
    const v=minimax(nb,1,2,-Infinity,Infinity)    // ply 2: Red, ply 3: Blue, then evaluate
            +Math.random()*.5;                    // small noise that breaks ties
    if(v>bv){bv=v;best=m;}                        // each root move gets the full window
  }
  return best;
}

The same idea in Python

It has the same structure as the alphabeta and best_move functions in the course’s Python repository (Chapter 2); here the game is this board, kept in a list of 36 elements. It is a runnable script; its result is the example above.

from math import inf
import random

def neighbors(i):                       # up/down/left/right cells inside the 6x6 board
  r, c = divmod(i, 6)
  return [r2 * 6 + c2 for r2, c2 in ((r+1, c), (r-1, c), (r, c+1), (r, c-1))
          if 0 <= r2 < 6 and 0 <= c2 < 6]

def adjacent(b, i, p):                  # how many neighbours of cell i belong to p
  return sum(b[n] == p for n in neighbors(i))

def legal(b, p):                        # empty cells touching p's territory
  return [i for i in range(36) if b[i] == 0 and adjacent(b, i, p) > 0]

def play(b, i, p):                      # returns a NEW board
  b = b[:]; b[i] = p
  if adjacent(b, i, p) >= 2:            # capture rule
    for n in neighbors(i):
      if b[n] == 3 - p and adjacent(b, n, 3 - p) <= 1:
        b[n] = p
        break                           # at most one capture
  return b

def evaluate(b):                        # Blue (2) is MAX
  return 10 * (b.count(2) - b.count(1)) + 2 * (len(legal(b, 2)) - len(legal(b, 1)))

def alphabeta(b, p, d, a, beta):
  moves = legal(b, p)
  if d == 0 or 0 not in b:
    return evaluate(b)
  if not moves:                         # pass
    if not legal(b, 3 - p):
      return evaluate(b)
    return alphabeta(b, 3 - p, d - 1, a, beta)
  if p == 2:                            # MAX
    v = -inf
    for m in moves:
      v = max(v, alphabeta(play(b, m, 2), 1, d - 1, a, beta))
      a = max(a, v)
      if a >= beta: break               # cutoff
    return v
  v = inf                               # MIN
  for m in moves:
    v = min(v, alphabeta(play(b, m, 1), 2, d - 1, a, beta))
    beta = min(beta, v)
    if a >= beta: break
  return v

def best_move(b):                       # Blue's root move (Hard)
  best, bv = None, -inf
  for m in legal(b, 2):
    v = alphabeta(play(b, m, 2), 1, 2, -inf, inf) + random.random() * 0.5
    if v > bv:
      bv, best = v, m
  return best

b = [0] * 36
b[0] = b[1] = b[6] = 1                  # Red after opening at row 1, column 2
b[35] = b[29] = 2
print(best_move(b))                     # 28 = row 5, column 5

Try it yourself

  1. Predict first, then look. Set the difficulty to Hard and tick Show move reasoning. After each of your moves, decide which cell Blue will choose before you look at the panel. Then compare with the ▶ row. In how many moves were you right? When you were wrong, which reply had the search seen that you had not?
  2. Recompute a value yourself. Press New battle, play (1,2) as Red and find the (5,5) row in the panel. Check the “now” column on the board: count the cells of both colours (territory); count each colour’s valid expansions, that is, the empty cells touching that colour’s cells (mobility). Then explain why the search finds 4: for each of Red’s three replies (1,3), (2,2), (3,1), set up Blue’s answer (5,4) and score the position by hand.
  3. Compare the levels. Play your first three moves against Medium, then (in a new battle) the same three against Hard. Compare Blue’s replies in both panels: why did Medium choose that cell (3 × friendly − enemy)? Where does Hard’s choice rank in Medium’s list? How did the final cell counts compare?
  4. Find where the AI goes wrong. Try to beat Hard. If you win (or find a position where Blue misses a capture), go back: which move looked good in the panel but turned out badly? Does the error come from the 3-ply search horizon, or from evaluate counting only territory and mobility?

Discussion questions

  • At the root, each move enters the search with the window (−∞, +∞). How does this affect the values in the panel? What would change if alpha-beta also passed the best value so far on at the root?
    Answer

    With the full window every root value is exact, so ranking and comparing them in the panel is meaningful. If alpha were updated with the best value at the root and passed to later root moves, there would be more pruning and the search would be faster; but the values of the non-best moves would only be bounds (≤ v): the search stops as soon as it finds that a move is worse than the best, without computing its exact value. The best move’s value would still be found correctly; what changes is the detail for the losers and the work done. This page gives up some pruning in exchange for exact values you can look at.

  • In the evaluation, the territory weight is 10 and the mobility weight 2. Why is there a mobility term, and how would the AI play if the weights changed?
    Answer

    Territory is the real score; mobility is a signal about the future: with many valid expansions you are more likely to capture cells later and to avoid being blocked. With weight 2, mobility decides when territory is equal, and only a very large mobility gap can outweigh a short-term cell gain (±10). With a large mobility weight the AI would chase options instead of cells and could lose the real score at the end. With no mobility weight at all the AI looks only at the current cell count and tends to get blocked at its borders. When the game ends, mobility is zero and the value is just 10 × the cell difference: the weights do not affect the end, only the way there.

  • The search depth is 3, an odd number: the last ply is Blue’s. Why might that be a problem? What would change with depth 2 or 4?
    Answer

    The leaf comes right after Blue’s own move; Red’s reply to it (for example a capture) is not seen. This horizon effect can make Blue judge its own last move too optimistically. At an even depth (2 or 4) the leaf comes after Red’s reply, so the evaluation is more cautious, but the search ends before it sees the future effect of Blue’s own move. Each extra ply multiplies the work by about b; here b ≈ 6. Which is better is settled by experiment (play the two versions against each other).

  • What is Math.random()*0.5 added to the values for? What if it were 3 instead of 0.5?
    Answer

    Because values are even integers (different values differ by at least 2), noise below 0.5 can never reorder different values; it only picks at random among moves with equal value. So the AI does not always choose the equal move with the lowest cell number, and it is harder to memorise and exploit; this is a simple form of the mixed-strategy idea from Chapter 3. With noise 3, moves valued 2 lower would sometimes be chosen: the AI would play worse at random.
