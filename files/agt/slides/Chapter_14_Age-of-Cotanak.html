How to play

A classroom-oriented territory-control recreation inspired by the original student project: territory control plus adversarial search.

  1. Red and Blue compete to control territory on the 6×6 map. Red starts in the top-left corner, Blue in the bottom-right corner; Red moves first.
  2. On your turn, click an empty cell that is orthogonally adjacent to territory you already control (dashed cells).
  3. Conversion: if the new cell touches at least two of your cells, one adjacent enemy cell that is weakly supported (touching at most one cell of its own colour) is captured and turns your colour.
  4. A player with no legal expansion passes; the other player moves again.
  5. The match ends when the board is full or neither player can expand. The player controlling more cells wins.

The last move is outlined in amber; a captured cell is outlined in the text colour.

AI difficulty

  • Easy — picks a random legal expansion.
  • Medium — greedy positional heuristic: prefers cells with many friendly neighbours (3 points each) and few enemy neighbours.
  • Hard — adversarial search: looks three plies ahead (its move, your reply, its next move) with minimax and alpha-beta pruning, evaluating territory difference and mobility (number of legal expansions).

Reading territory

A cell is valuable for more than its immediate score. Central positions may open several future expansion directions, while narrow corridors can block an opponent from reaching an entire region. Frontier cells have both current value and option value: they determine what moves become possible later.

Strategy tips

  • Expand toward flexible central areas before committing too deeply to one edge.
  • Build connected regions so that one blocked frontier does not stop all future growth.
  • Watch for bottlenecks where a single move can deny access to several cells.
  • Compare immediate territorial gain with the future moves that each expansion creates or removes.

Algorithmic strategy connection

This edition focuses on spatial control, heuristic evaluation and adversarial search. The compact board makes it easy to observe how an evaluation function can reward territory, mobility, connectedness and future expansion rather than merely counting occupied cells — compare Medium (one-step heuristic) with Hard (search).
