How to play

  • You play White; the computer plays Black. Click one of your pieces (or focus it and press Enter), then a marked square. Dots show quiet moves, rings show captures.
  • All standard rules apply (castling, en passant, check, checkmate, stalemate, draws by threefold repetition and the fifty-move rule, insufficient material). Pawns always promote to a queen.
  • Undo takes back your last move and the AI's reply. New game starts over.
  • The last move is highlighted in yellow; a king in check is marked in red.
  • The letters and digits around the board name the squares (a1 to h8). Moves are written with these names: Nf6 moves a knight to f6, exd5 means the e-file pawn captures on d5.
  • The Show move reasoning box shows, in the side panel, what the AI computed before each move (see the How the AI decides tab).

Connection to game theory

Chess is a finite, two-player, zero-sum, perfect-information game. By Zermelo's theorem it has a definite value found by backward induction (White wins, Black wins, or a draw); but the game tree is far too large to solve, so programs search to a limited depth and estimate the value with heuristic rules.

What is searched?

The AI plays Black. On its turn it runs the algorithm from the Minimax and Alpha-Beta Pruning lecture: minimax with alpha-beta pruning. The depth is the choice in the side panel: 1, 2 or 3 plies (half-moves). At depth 1 the AI looks only at its own move; at depth 2 it also considers your reply; at depth 3 it also sees its own next move.

  • Root. The AI tries every legal move in turn; for each it computes a value that assumes your best reply, and plays the one with the largest value. At the root the window is (−∞, +∞), so all root values are exact; pruning only works below the root.
  • Max and min. All values are written from the AI's side: the AI's turn is a max node, yours is a min node.
  • Move ordering. Below the root, captures (moves with an “x” in their name) are tried first. If a good move is found early, α rises quickly and later branches are cut sooner.
  • Randomness. The root moves are shuffled before each search and only a strictly larger value replaces the current best. So moves with equal scores are chosen at random: it may play differently in the same position, but it never picks a lower-scoring move.
  • Terminal positions. A checkmated side gets ±(100000 + remaining depth): a larger remaining depth means the mate came earlier, so the AI prefers mating early and avoids being mated early. Stalemate scores 0. Threefold repetition and the fifty-move rule are not part of the search; they are only checked during the game.
  • What is not there. No transposition table, iterative deepening, quiescence search or time limit; the depth is fixed.

Why not search to the end of the game?

A position has about 35 legal moves on average. A full tree of d plies has about 35d leaves: 353 ≈ 43 thousand, 355 ≈ 52 million; for an average game (about 80 plies) ≈ 10123. In the best case alpha-beta shrinks this to about its square root, but reaching the end is still impossible. Zermelo's theorem says solving the tree by backward induction is possible, not that it is feasible. So the search stops at some depth and an evaluation function estimates the value of that position. Depth 3 being “slow” is the result of this growth. For example, after 1.e4 the panel shows about 20, 620 and 2900 positions searched at depths 1, 2 and 3; the tree at depth 3 without pruning would have about 20·30·30 = 18,000 leaves.

What does the evaluation function count?

The function (evaluateBoard) gives every piece on the board a score: its material plus the placement score of the square it stands on.

position = ΣWhite pieces (material + table[square]) − ΣBlack pieces (material + table[square])

  • Material: pawn 100, knight 320, bishop 330, rook 500, queen 900. The king's 20000 is on both sides all the time and cancels out.
  • Piece-square tables: for each piece type an 8×8 table says whether a square is good or bad. For Black the rows of the same table are flipped. Examples: a pawn is −20 on e2, +20 on e4 and +50 on the seventh rank; a knight is −50 in the corner and +20 in the centre; the king is between −40 and −50 in mid-board and +30 on the castling square (g1); a rook on the seventh rank gets +10.

The total is computed as White − Black. Because the AI is Black, the sign is flipped at the search leaves (−evaluateBoard) and the AI wants the larger value.

This function only looks at the board: there are no threats, exchanges, king safety or pawn structure. If a piece is under attack at the point where the search stops, the function cannot see it (the horizon effect); this error shrinks as the depth grows.

A small example (with real values)

Set the depth to 1 and play e4 as White. The e2 pawn moved from a −20 square (total 80) to e4 (+20, total 120): the position is +40 for White. Black's moves change this difference as follows:

What does the panel show?

With Show move reasoning on, after each AI move the panel lists: the search depth, the number of positions evaluated, the exact value of every candidate move (in algebraic notation) from the AI's side, largest first, the chosen move (yellow), and the material and placement totals of the position after the move. While the panel is on, the “Recent moves” list is hidden to make room.

Decision code (JavaScript)

// Root: try each legal move of the AI (Black) and compute its value
function minimaxRoot(depth, g) {
  const moves = g.moves({ verbose: true });
  let bestValue = -Infinity;
  let bestMove = null;
  const rows = [];                       // record for the reasoning panel
  nodeCount = 0;                         // counter of searched positions
  moves.sort(() => Math.random() - .5);  // random choice among equal scores
  for (const m of moves) {
    g.move(m);                           // try the move
    const value = minimax(depth - 1, g, -Infinity, Infinity, false);
    g.undo();                            // take it back
    rows.push({ san: m.san, value });
    if (value > bestValue) { bestValue = value; bestMove = m; }
  }
  lastWhy = { depth, nodes: nodeCount, rows, chosen: bestMove ? bestMove.san : null, parts: null };
  return bestMove;
}

// Minimax with alpha-beta pruning; isMax = it is the AI's turn
function minimax(depth, g, alpha, beta, isMax) {
  nodeCount++;
  if (depth === 0) return -evaluateBoard(g.board());   // from the AI's (Black's) side
  const moves = g.moves();
  if (!moves.length) {                                  // checkmate or stalemate
    if (g.in_check()) return isMax ? -100000 - depth : 100000 + depth;
    return 0;
  }
  // captures (moves containing "x") first: pruning happens earlier
  moves.sort((a, b) => (b.indexOf('x') >= 0) - (a.indexOf('x') >= 0));
  if (isMax) {
    let best = -Infinity;
    for (const m of moves) {
      g.move(m);
      best = Math.max(best, minimax(depth - 1, g, alpha, beta, false));
      g.undo();
      alpha = Math.max(alpha, best);
      if (beta <= alpha) break;                         // pruning
    }
    return best;
  }
  let best = Infinity;
  for (const m of moves) {
    g.move(m);
    best = Math.min(best, minimax(depth - 1, g, alpha, beta, true));
    g.undo();
    beta = Math.min(beta, best);
    if (beta <= alpha) break;                           // pruning
  }
  return best;
}

// Evaluation: White's total minus Black's total
function evaluateBoard(position) {
  let total = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < 8; i++)
    for (let j = 0; j < 8; j++)
      total += getPieceValue(position[i][j], j, i);
  return total;
}

function getPieceValue(piece, x, y) {
  if (!piece) return 0;
  const w = piece.color === 'w';
  let v = 0;
  switch (piece.type) {
    case 'p': v = 100 + (w ? pawnEvalWhite : pawnEvalBlack)[y][x]; break;
    case 'r': v = 500 + (w ? whiteRookEval : blackRookEval)[y][x]; break;
    case 'n': v = 320 + knightEval[y][x]; break;
    case 'b': v = 330 + (w ? whiteBishopEval : blackBishopEval)[y][x]; break;
    case 'q': v = 900 + (w ? whiteQueenEval : blackQueenEval)[y][x]; break;
    case 'k': v = 20000 + (w ? whiteKingEval : blackKingEval)[y][x]; break;
  }
  return w ? v : -v;                     // Black pieces count with a minus sign
}

// Piece-square table (row 0 = rank 8, row 7 = rank 1, from White's side)
const pawnEvalWhite = [
  [0,0,0,0,0,0,0,0],
  [50,50,50,50,50,50,50,50],
  [10,10,20,30,30,20,10,10],
  [5,5,10,25,25,10,5,5],
  [0,0,0,20,20,0,0,0],
  [5,-5,-10,0,0,-10,-5,5],
  [5,10,10,-20,-20,10,10,5],
  [0,0,0,0,0,0,0,0]
];
const pawnEvalBlack = pawnEvalWhite.slice().reverse();   // flipped for Black
// ... the tables for knight, bishop, rook, queen and king look the same

The same idea in Python

The same pattern as the course's Python examples (alphabeta, maxi, pruning with break); a state object supplies the chess rules.

from math import inf

VALUE = {'p': 100, 'n': 320, 'b': 330, 'r': 500, 'q': 900, 'k': 20000}

def evaluate(pieces):                  # pieces: [(color, kind, x, y), ...]
  total = 0
  for color, kind, x, y in pieces:
    row = y if color == 'w' else 7 - y    # the table is flipped for Black
    v = VALUE[kind] + TABLE[kind][row][x]
    total += v if color == 'w' else -v    # White - Black
  return total

def alphabeta(state, depth, a, b, maxi):    # maxi: it is the AI's turn
  if depth == 0:
    return -evaluate(state.pieces())        # from the AI's (Black's) side
  moves = state.legal_moves()
  if not moves:
    if state.in_check():
      return -100000 - depth if maxi else 100000 + depth
    return 0                                 # stalemate
  moves.sort(key=lambda m: 'x' not in m)     # captures first
  best = -inf if maxi else inf
  for m in moves:
    v = alphabeta(state.play(m), depth - 1, a, b, not maxi)
    if maxi:
      best = max(best, v); a = max(a, best)
    else:
      best = min(best, v); b = min(b, best)
    if b <= a:
      break                                  # pruning
  return best

def best_move(state, depth):
  return max(state.legal_moves(),
             key=lambda m: alphabeta(state.play(m), depth - 1, -inf, inf, False))

Try it yourself

  1. Predict first, then look. Tick Show move reasoning and play at depth 2. After each of your moves, guess which move the AI will choose before it answers. Then compare with the move at the top of the panel: is it the same? If not, what score did your guess get in the panel?
  2. Recompute a value by hand. Press New game, set the depth to 1 and play e4. Find the rows Nf6, Nc6, e5 and d6 in the panel and repeat the calculation from the How the AI decides tab on your own. Use the letters and digits around the board to find the squares in the tables. Does the “material / placement” line below the panel agree with your calculation?
  3. Change the depth and count. Each time start a new game and play the same e4 at depth 1, 2 and 3. Write down the “positions searched” number in the panel header. By what factor does it grow when the depth rises by one? What does that tell you compared with the number of legal moves (~30)? Did the top move change with the depth?
  4. Find the AI's mistake. Set the depth to 1. Catch a moment when the AI captures a piece and then loses its own piece in return (hint: a queen taking a protected pawn). How many points did the panel give that move? Can you read from the panel why the AI could not see the loss? Then raise the depth to 2 and offer the same chance again: did the decision change?

Discussion questions

  • Is chess a solvable game? If so, why doesn't the program solve it?
    Answer

    In theory yes: by Zermelo's theorem chess is finite, two-player, zero-sum and perfect-information, so it has a definite value found by backward induction (White wins, Black wins, or a draw). But the tree has about 10120 leaves; no computer can walk through it. So the program stops at a fixed depth and estimates the value of the position with an evaluation function. The theory says “an answer exists”; the algorithm cannot reach it and settles for an approximate one.

  • At depth 1, why does the AI take a protected pawn with its queen? How can this error be reduced?
    Answer

    The AI scores only the board right after its own move. Capturing the pawn looks like +100 of material; losing the queen on the very next move lies beyond the search horizon. This is called the horizon effect. There are two remedies: search deeper (depth 2 sees the recapture) and quiescence search: at a leaf, keep searching until the captures are over, so the evaluation happens only in “quiet” positions. This page has no quiescence search.

  • The piece-square tables are written by hand. What could replace them?
    Answer

    The numbers in a table are a feature-weight sum; the weights can be learned from real game results. Modern programs leave the evaluation to a neural network trained on millions of positions (for example Stockfish's NNUE); AlphaZero learned the value entirely by playing itself. Finding which features matter from data instead of asking a human is the learning idea of Chapter 13.

  • Why does trying captures first speed up the search?
    Answer

    If a good move is found early, alpha-beta cuts many more branches: α rises at once and sibling branches that cannot catch up are skipped. With moves ordered from best to worst, the tree would shrink from about bd to bd/2 (twice the depth in the same time). Captures are often good moves, so “captures first” is a cheap but effective guess. With the worst possible ordering, no pruning happens at all.
