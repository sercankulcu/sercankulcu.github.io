Multiple Choice · Mechanism design and incentives

Question 1

Why is mechanism design an “inverse” approach compared with game theory?

The correct answer is B. Game theory is analysis: the rules are given and we predict what happens in equilibrium (option A is exactly this). Mechanism design is synthesis: first the desired outcome (the social choice function f) is fixed, then we look for rules that produce this outcome in equilibrium even when selfish players pursue their own interests. C and D are wrong: players are treated as strategic and their types are hidden from the designer, which is the source of the difficulty.

Multiple Choice · Revelation principle and incentive compatibility

Question 2

What does the revelation principle say?

The correct answer is A. If s(θ) is an equilibrium of an indirect mechanism M, a direct mechanism M* asks only for a type and plays s(b) for the player. Lying in M* equals deviating in M, so it is not profitable: the outcome is implemented by a direct mechanism in which truthful reporting is an equilibrium. B is wrong: the principle concerns the constructed M*, and under Bayes–Nash it gives only BIC. C and D are not part of the principle.

Multiple Choice · DSIC and BIC

Question 3

Which statement gives the relation between DSIC and BIC?

The correct answer is C. Under DSIC, truthful reporting is best whatever the others report; so it is also best when the others are honest and types come from a distribution, that is, best in expected utility: DSIC ⇒ BIC. The converse is false: the direct mechanism built by the revelation principle from the Bayes–Nash equilibrium of a first-price auction (everyone bids below their value) is BIC, but when a rival reports differently, honesty need not be best. So A reverses the direction; D is wrong, since DSIC is also defined for mechanisms without money (median, TTC).

Multiple Choice · Myerson–Satterthwaite theorem

Question 4

By the Myerson–Satterthwaite theorem, which properties cannot all hold together in two-sided trade?

The correct answer is D. Myerson–Satterthwaite (1983): in trade between a buyer and a seller whose values come from overlapping ranges, efficiency, Bayesian incentive compatibility, individual rationality, and budget balance cannot all hold; a design must give up one of them. A is incomplete: if IC is not required, efficiency and budget balance are easy to get together. In C, a monotone allocation and a critical-bid payment together give DSIC in Myerson's lemma, so they do not conflict.

Multiple Choice · Limits of VCG and Vickrey

Question 5

Which of the following is a limitation of the VCG mechanism?

The correct answer is D. In VCG the collected payments cannot be handed back to the residents (redistributing them would break the incentives); in the public project example a surplus of 25 units remains, so strong budget balance fails; since payments are non-negative, weak budget balance (no deficit) holds. A and B reverse VCG's strengths: VCG is DSIC and maximizes social welfare. C is wrong: a loser who does not change the choice at the Clarke pivot (creates no externality) pays 0. Other limits: an NP-hard argmax, collusion, and false-name bids.

Multiple Choice · Winner and payment in Vickrey

Question 6

In a second-price (Vickrey) sealed-bid auction the bids are Ayşe 10, Bora 7, Cem 5 TL. Who gets the item and how many TL does the winner pay?

The correct answer is A. Ayşe, the highest bidder, wins and pays the second-highest bid: 7 TL. Paying her own bid (10) is the first-price rule.

Multiple Choice · Dominant strategy in Vickrey

Question 7

In a second-price (Vickrey) auction, which is a bidder's dominant strategy?

The correct answer is B. The amount paid depends not on the winner's own bid but on the rival's bid. So bidding the value truthfully is the dominant strategy: bidding less can miss a profitable sale, and bidding more can lead to paying above the value.

Multiple Choice · Utility in first-price

Question 8

In a first-price auction, where the winner pays their own bid, a bidder values the item at 20 TL and the rival bids 12 TL. What is the bidder's utility if they bid 15 TL? And if they bid their value (20 TL) truthfully?

The correct answer is C. The winner pays their own bid: with a bid of 15 the utility is 20 − 15 = 5, with a truthful bid it is 20 − 20 = 0. The answer is 5 and 0: in the first-price auction a truthful bid is not incentive compatible. The 8 comes from the second-price rule (20 − 12).

Multiple Choice · Design without money and stability

Question 9

Where money cannot be used, in which mechanism is truthful reporting a dominant strategy (DSIC) for the relevant participants?

The correct answer is A. In TTC, reporting preferences truthfully is a dominant strategy for every house owner, and the outcome is also in the core. B is wrong: in the Boston mechanism a student who loses on a first choice also falls behind at the later schools, so ranking strategically can pay. C is wrong: a village at the edge exaggerates its location to pull the average toward itself. D is wrong: Gale–Shapley is DSIC for the proposing side; the proposed-to side can reject some offers and reach a better partner. Without money, incentives come only from the structure of preferences or from the choice of goal (stability, exchange).

Multiple Choice · Gale–Shapley

Question 10

Which statement about Gale–Shapley (deferred acceptance) is true?

The correct answer is A. In Gale–Shapley the proposing side gets its best partner among all stable matchings and truthful reporting is dominant for it; the proposed-to side gets its worst stable partner and can gain by lying. B is wrong: Roth (1982) showed that no stable mechanism can be DSIC for both sides at once. C reverses the direction; D is wrong, since the algorithm returns a stable matching on every input.

Multiple Choice · Gale–Shapley

Question 11

Which statement about the matching produced by the Gale–Shapley algorithm is always true?

The correct answer is D. Gale–Shapley always produces a stable matching. Among stable matchings, only the proposing side gets its best; for the proposed-to side the result is the worst. Not everyone needs to get their first choice.

Multiple Choice · Bayes–Nash equilibrium, P(S) = 1/4

Question 12

P1 chooses concert (C) or match (M). P2 is type S (wants to meet) or type A (wants to avoid); P1 does not know the type. Now P(S) = 1/4, P(A) = 3/4. Which statement is true?

Type SP2: CP2: MType AP2: CP2: M
P1: C(2, 1)(0, 0)P1: C(2, 0)(0, 2)
P1: M(0, 0)(1, 2)P1: M(0, 1)(1, 0)

The correct answer is C. Against a pure P1 action each P2 type has a clear best response, so there are two candidates. P1 = C: P2 plays (S→C, A→M); C gives P1 0.5, M gives 0.75, so P1 deviates (A is wrong). P1 = M: P2 plays (S→M, A→C); C gives 1.5, M gives 0.25, so P1 deviates (B is wrong). In D, type A against C gets 2 by choosing M, so always C is not a best response. With type S unlikely, P2 runs away from P1, so no pure equilibrium remains.

Multiple Choice · A DSIC mechanism without money: TTC

Question 13

Among VCG, proportional cost sharing, TTC, and the first-price auction, which is DSIC without using money and gives a Pareto-optimal outcome in the core?

The correct answer is C. TTC uses no money, reporting true preferences is a dominant strategy (Roth, 1982), and the outcome is Pareto optimal and in the core: no group can do better by swapping their own rooms among themselves. A is also DSIC but uses money (payments). B is not IC (free riding), and D is not DSIC: a bidder gains by shading the bid below the value.

Classic Question · Incentive compatibility

Question 14: Proportional Cost Sharing

A park costs 72 thousand TL. True values: Ayşe 36, Bora 30, Cem 24. Rule: if the reports sum to at least 72, the park is built and the cost is shared in proportion to the reports; otherwise nobody pays.

a) If everyone is honest, what are the payments and utilities? Does IR hold?
b) With the others honest, what is Cem's most profitable report? Is the rule IC?
c) What happens if everyone reports half their value?

Show answer

a) The total 90 ≥ 72 ⇒ the park is built. Payments are 72·36/90 = 28.8, 72·30/90 = 24, 72·24/90 = 19.2. Utilities are 7.2; 6; 4.8: all ≥ 0, so IR holds.

b) If Cem reports b, his payment is 72·b/(66 + b), which grows with b; the park needs 66 + b ≥ 72. The most profitable is b = 6: the total is exactly 72, Cem pays 6, and his utility is 24 − 6 = 18 (> 4.8). Lying pays ⇒ not IC.

c) 18 + 15 + 12 = 45 < 72: the park is not built, and total utility drops from 18 to 0. Free riding causes inefficiency; the fix is to make the payment independent of the person's own report (VCG).

Classic Question · Myerson's lemma

Question 15: Myerson Payment Formula

In a single-parameter setting, with the others' bids fixed, a player's allocation as a function of the bid is:

bid bb < 22 ≤ b < 5b ≥ 5
x(b)01/21

a) Can this allocation be used in a DSIC mechanism?
b) Compute p(4) and p(7) with p(b) = b·x(b) − ∫₀ᵇ x(z) dz. What is the utility at b = 7?
c) What if x(b) = 1 (b < 3), 0 (b ≥ 3)?

Show answer

a) Yes: x is monotone (it does not decrease as the bid grows). By Myerson's lemma the payment is uniquely determined (p(0) = 0).

b) ∫₀ᵇ x(z) dz = the area under the curve = the player's utility; payment = b·x(b) − this area (the shaded area to the left of the curve):

  • ∫₀⁴ x = 0·2 + (1/2)·2 = 1 ⇒ p(4) = 4·(1/2) − 1 = 1
  • ∫₀⁷ x = (1/2)·3 + 1·2 = 3.5 ⇒ p(7) = 7·1 − 3.5 = 3.5

Check: jumps × thresholds = (1/2)·2 + (1/2)·5 = 3.5. Utility 7 − 3.5 = 3.5 = ∫₀⁷ x (the area under the curve).

c) The allocation drops from 1 to 0 as the bid grows: not monotone. No payment rule can make it DSIC (a player above 3 gets the item by lowering the bid).

Classic Question · VCG

Question 17: VCG with Two Identical Items

Two identical items, four bidders; each bidder wants at most one item. Values: 12, 9, 7, and 4. Everyone reports honestly.

a) Who wins under VCG? Find each bidder's payment and utility.
b) What would the utility of the bidder with value 9 be if they reported 13 or 6?

Show answer

a) Maximum welfare: the two highest values ⇒ 12 and 9 win (total 21).

  • Value 12: best without them = 9 + 7 = 16; with them the others get 9 ⇒ p = 16 − 9 = 7, utility 5
  • Value 9: best without them = 12 + 7 = 19; with them the others get 12 ⇒ p = 19 − 12 = 7, utility 2
  • Values 7 and 4: the outcome is the same without them ⇒ p = 0

Each winner pays the highest value among the losers (the 3rd highest bid): a generalization of the single-item Vickrey rule.

b) Reporting 13 still wins and still pays 7: utility 2. Reporting 6 lets the 7 win, and the utility of the bidder with value 9 is 0. Lying does not pay.

Classic Question · Gale–Shapley

Question 19: Gale–Shapley Rounds

Students propose; projects tentatively hold their best candidate.

StudentPreferenceProjectPreference
AY ≻ X ≻ ZXA ≻ C ≻ B
BY ≻ Z ≻ XYC ≻ B ≻ A
CX ≻ Y ≻ ZZA ≻ B ≻ C

a) Write the proposals and decisions in each round. What is the result?
b) Show that the result is stable by looking for a blocking pair.

Show answer
  • Round 1: A→Y, B→Y, C→X. Y: B is held (B ≻ A), A is rejected. X: C is held.
  • Round 2: A→X. X: A ≻ C ⇒ A is held, C is rejected.
  • Round 3: C→Y. Y: C ≻ B ⇒ C is held, B is rejected.
  • Round 4: B→Z. Z is free ⇒ B is accepted. Everyone is matched, stop.

Result: A–X, B–Z, C–Y.

b) A prefers Y to X, but Y prefers C to A. B wants Y, but Y prefers C to B. C wants X, but X prefers A to C. B does not prefer X to Z. No blocking pair ⇒ stable. (Of the 6 matchings, this is the only stable one.)

Classic Question · Median mechanism

Question 20: Mean or Median?

Five villages lie on a road at 3, 8, 10, 15, and 24 km. One facility will be built; each village's cost is its distance to the facility.

a) Where is the facility under the mean and median mechanisms, and what is the total distance?
b) Under the mean rule, what must the village at 24 report to pull the facility exactly to its own location?
c) Under the median rule, can the village at 3 move the facility closer whatever it reports?

Show answer

a) The mean is 60/5 = 12: total 9 + 4 + 2 + 3 + 12 = 30. The median is 10: total 7 + 2 + 0 + 5 + 14 = 28. The median minimizes the total distance.

b) (3 + 8 + 10 + 15 + x)/5 = 24 ⇒ x = 84. The village's distance falls from 12 to 0: the mean can be manipulated. Under the median, a report of 84 leaves the median at 10.

c) No. The village at 3 is to the left of the median; any report below 10 leaves the median at 10. If it moves to the right of 10 (say 12), the order becomes 8, 10, 12, 15, 24, the median shifts to 12, and its distance grows from 7 to 9. Truthfulness is a dominant strategy.

Classic Question · TTC

Question 21: Room Swapping with TTC

Five students each own a room. Preferences are written by room owner (rooms not on the list are worse than the student's own room).

PersonPreference
AB ≻ D ≻ A
BC ≻ A ≻ B
CA ≻ E ≻ C
DB ≻ E ≻ D
EA ≻ E ≻ D

Run TTC round by round. Who gets which room?

Show answer

Round 1: A→B, B→C, C→A, D→B, E→A. Cycle A → B → C → A: A gets B's room, B gets C's, C gets A's. The three leave.

Round 2: D and E remain. D→E (B is gone), E→E (A is gone; the next is E's own room). Cycle E → E: E keeps their own room and leaves.

Round 3: D→D (B and E are gone): D keeps their own room.

Result: A ← B, B ← C, C ← A, D ← D, E ← E. A, B, and C got their first choices. D would have liked E's room, but E prefers their own room to D's: no swap. The outcome is Pareto optimal; truthful reporting is dominant.

Classic Question · Bayes–Nash equilibrium

Question 22: Bayes–Nash Equilibria

P1 chooses concert (C) or match (M). P2 is either type S (wants to meet) or type A (wants to avoid); P1 does not know the type. This time P(S) = 3/4, P(A) = 1/4.

Type SP2: CP2: MType AP2: CP2: M
P1: C(2, 1)(0, 0)P1: C(2, 0)(0, 2)
P1: M(0, 0)(1, 2)P1: M(0, 1)(1, 0)

Find all pure Bayes–Nash equilibria. For general P(S) = π, under what condition is each candidate an equilibrium?

Show answer

Candidate 1: P1 = C. Type S chooses C (1 > 0), type A chooses M (2 > 0).

  • P1: C → (3/4)·2 + (1/4)·0 = 3/2; M → (3/4)·0 + (1/4)·1 = 1/4 ⇒ C ✓

Candidate 2: P1 = M. Type S chooses M (2 > 0), type A chooses C (1 > 0).

  • P1: C → (3/4)·0 + (1/4)·2 = 1/2; M → (3/4)·1 + (1/4)·0 = 3/4 ⇒ M ✓

Two pure Bayes–Nash equilibria: (C; S→C, A→M) and (M; S→M, A→C).

General π: Candidate 1 ⇔ 2π ≥ 1 − π ⇔ π ≥ 1/3. Candidate 2 ⇔ π ≥ 2(1 − π) ⇔ π ≥ 2/3. For example, at π = 1/2 only Candidate 1 is an equilibrium.

Multiple Choice · Boston mechanism

Question 23

Three schools (X, Y, Z) have one seat each. Preferences: A: Y ≻ X ≻ Z, B: Y ≻ X ≻ Z, C: X ≻ Y ≻ Z. Priorities: Y: A, B, C; X: B, C, A; Z: A, B, C. In the Boston mechanism (in each round, unplaced students apply to their next choice; a school gives its free seats to those who chose it in that round, in priority order, and a seat once given is not taken back), which school does B get when reporting honestly, and which when reporting “X ≻ Y ≻ Z”?

The correct answer is A. With honest reports, in round 1 A and B apply to Y and A, who has priority, gets it; C applies to X and gets it. In round 2, B's second choice X is already full; in round 3 B is placed at Z. If B reports “X ≻ Y ≻ Z”, in round 1 B and C apply to X and B, who has higher priority at X, wins: B gets the second choice X instead of Z, so lying pays. That is why the Boston mechanism is not DSIC. On the same profile, Gale–Shapley, in which students propose, also puts B at X with honest reports. D is wrong: A, who has priority, gets Y.

Multiple Choice · Individual rationality

Question 24

In an auction every participant first pays a 5 TL entry fee, then the second-price rule applies. Ayşe, whose value is 8, bids honestly; the highest other bid is 6. Which statement is true?

The correct answer is C. The entry fee does not depend on the bid; once in, a truthful bid is still dominant under the second-price rule, so IC is preserved (B is wrong). But Ayşe wins and pays 6, and with the fee her utility is 8 − 6 − 5 = −3: staying out would give 0, which is more, so IR fails. Winning is not enough for IR (A is wrong); the fee is subtracted from utility (D is wrong). Every losing bidder also has utility −5.

Multiple Choice · Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem

Question 25

In a setting without money there are at least three alternatives, and voters can report any preference ranking. The rule is onto (every alternative can win) and strategy-proof. By the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, which statement is true?

The correct answer is D. With general preferences, at least three alternatives, every onto and strategy-proof rule is a dictatorship: the outcome is always decided by the same person's preference. A is wrong: a dictatorship is strategy-proof, because the dictator already gets their favorite and the others' reports do not affect the outcome. B is wrong: the median rule is strategy-proof only for single-peaked preferences; restricting the preference domain is a way out of the theorem. C is wrong: with two alternatives, simple majority is strategy-proof.

Multiple Choice · Serial dictatorship

Question 26

Four students share four rooms by serial dictatorship; the order drawn by lot is C, A, D, B. Preferences: A: 2 ≻ 1 ≻ 3 ≻ 4, B: 2 ≻ 3 ≻ 4 ≻ 1, C: 1 ≻ 2 ≻ 4 ≻ 3, D: 2 ≻ 4 ≻ 1 ≻ 3. Which statement is true?

The correct answer is B. C picks first and gets 1; A takes 2 from what is left, D (2 is gone) takes 4, and B is left with 3. No one can affect those who pick before their turn, and when the turn comes they take the best of what remains, so truthful reporting is dominant (DSIC). A is wrong: when B's turn comes, 2 is already with A; whatever list B writes, nothing better than 3 is left. C ignores the order: A, who picks before D, has already taken 2. D is wrong: giving 2 to B would make A worse off; serial dictatorship is Pareto optimal.

Multiple Choice · Implementation theory

Question 27

In a Vickrey (second-price) auction the values are Ayşe 12, Bora 4. Which of the following bid profiles (Ayşe, Bora) is a Nash equilibrium that gives the item to Bora?

The correct answer is D. To win, Ayşe would have to bid above 20 and pay 20: utility 12 − 20 = −8 < 0, so she does not deviate. Bora pays 0 and gets the highest utility (4). The item goes to the lower-value bidder: although truthfulness is dominant, there is a bad equilibrium; this is exactly what implementation theory asks about. A is an equilibrium, but Ayşe wins. In B, Ayşe bids above 8 and pays 8 (utility 4 > 0); in C, above 10 and pays 10 (utility 2 > 0): in both, deviating pays.

Classic Question · BIC and DSIC

Question 28: BIC but not DSIC

Two bidders' values are independent U[0, 1]. In the equilibrium of the first-price auction, everyone bids v/2. The direct mechanism built by the revelation principle: everyone reports a value r, the highest report wins, and the winner pays r/2.

a) Write the expected utility of a bidder with value v who reports r while the rival is honest. What is the best r? Is the mechanism BIC?
b) Let v = 0.8 and the rival's report be 0.3. What is the utility of the honest report and of the report r = 0.4? Is the mechanism DSIC?

Show answer

a) Since the rival is honest with value U[0, 1], a report r wins with probability r, and the winner pays r/2:

  • E[u] = r·(v − r/2) = rv − r²/2
  • Derivative v − r = 0 ⇒ r = v; at v = 0.8, E[u] = 0.32 (for r = 0.4 it is 0.24)

The honest report maximizes expected utility ⇒ BIC.

b) Honest: wins, pays 0.4, utility 0.4. r = 0.4: still wins (0.4 > 0.3), pays 0.2, utility 0.6. When the rival's report is known, lying pays ⇒ not DSIC. BIC holds only on average, when the rival is honest.

Output Question · Code trace: ttc(pref)

Question 29

The ttc function from the slides (slightly shortened) is called with the input below. What does it return?

def ttc(pref):
    left, assign = list(pref), {}
    while left:
        g = {i: next(j for j in pref[i] if j in left) for i in left}
        i, path = left[0], []
        while i not in path: path.append(i); i = g[i]
        for k in path[path.index(i):]: assign[k] = g[k]; left.remove(k)
    return assign
ttc({'A': ['B','D','A'], 'B': ['C','A','B'], 'C': ['B','C'], 'D': ['A','D']})

The correct answer is B. Round 1: A→B, B→C, C→B, D→A; the cycle is [B, C], so B and C swap; A is on the path, not on the cycle (A is wrong). Round 2: A→D, D→A, so A and D swap (C is wrong). D is the round-1 arrows.
