Chapter 5: Auctions

A seller does not know to whom to sell an item, or at what price, because the seller cannot see how much the buyers value it. An auction solves this problem with rules: bidders submit bids, and the rules determine the winner and the payment. But when the rules change, so does the behavior of a clever bidder. These notes begin by running the four basic auction formats on a small numerical example. They then prove why truthful bidding is best under the second-price rule, derive how much a bidder shades the bid under the first-price rule, and show why different rules bring the seller the same revenue on average. The last topics are the reserve price, the winner’s curse, and two-sided markets.

5.1Basic concepts

An auction is a method of sale in which one or more items are distributed according to the bids of the participants, under rules announced in advance. In this chapter the participants who want to buy the item are called bidders. The rules answer two questions: who gets the item, and what does that person pay? These two questions are exactly the allocation rule and the payment rule of mechanism design. Auctions are therefore the oldest and most widespread application of mechanism design.

A small game sets out the main question of the chapter. The place of auctions in everyday life, the basic terms, the probability tools needed for the examples, and three models of how bidders value an item frame this question.

5.1.1A first game: the Vickrey auction

The rule of the game is short. Everyone writes a bid in a sealed envelope, and the envelopes are opened at the same time. The highest bidder gets the item. But the winner pays not their own bid but the second-highest bid. This rule is called the Vickrey auction, or the second-price sealed-bid auction, after the economist William Vickrey, who studied it in 1961 (Vickrey, 1961).

Three questions naturally arise for someone playing the game:

  • If the item is worth 70 TL to a player, should the player write less than 70, exactly 70, or more in the envelope?
  • Would the best bid change if the rivals’ bids were known?
  • If the winner paid their own bid (the first-price rule), would writing 70 still be a good idea?

Suppose that in one round the player writes 70, the first rival 60, and the second rival 45. The highest bid is the player’s, and the player gets the item. The amount paid is the second-highest bid, that is, 60 TL. Since the item is worth 70 TL to the player, the player comes out ahead by 70 − 60 = 10 TL. Figure 5.1 shows this round.

The important detail in the figure is this: the 60 TL the winner pays is not the number written in the winner’s own envelope. Had 65 or 100 been written instead of 70, the winner would not change and the amount paid would still be 60. The first big result of the chapter rests on this observation: under the second-price rule the bid determines only whether you win, not how much you pay. Why this observation makes the truthful bid the best strategy is proved step by step in 5.3.2. The answer to the third question is different: if the winner pays their own bid, a player who writes 70 and wins pays 70 and gains nothing. Under the first-price rule the bid is therefore shaded below the value; how far it is shaded is the subject of 5.3.5.

5.1.2Why do auctions matter?

There are three reasons why auctions are so widespread.

First, they are efficient in allocating resources. In a well-designed auction the item tends to go to the person who values it most. This means that the total benefit society gets from the item is large. A painting going to the person who values it at 85 TL creates more welfare than its going to the person who values it at 55 TL.

Second, they provide price discovery. The seller does not know the buyers’ values; if the seller did, the seller would sell directly to the highest-value buyer at that price. An auction pours the buyers’ private information into bids, and the price emerges from these bids. Because the rules apply to everyone in the same way, the market becomes transparent.

Third, the auction is the most widespread application of mechanism design. A mechanism is a set of rules that takes information from participants (here, bids) and produces an outcome (who gets what, who pays what). An auction is the simplest combination of an allocation rule and a payment rule.

In public tenders the direction is reversed: the state is the buyer and firms compete to offer the lowest price. The analyses in this chapter are set in the simplest case, with a single item and a single seller; but the ideas that come out of them (truthful bidding, shading, revenue equivalence, the reserve price) are also the foundation of the large applications.

5.1.3Basic terms and the model

The analysis of auctions rests on four concepts. Telling them apart prevents most of the mistakes made throughout the chapter. The pair most often confused is value and bid.

Definition 5.1
  • Value vi: the true worth that bidder i places on the item; the highest amount the bidder is willing to pay for it. The value is private information: only the bidder knows it.
  • Bid bi: the number the bidder reports to the mechanism. The bid need not equal the value; the bidder can strategically write less or more.
  • Utility ui: if the bidder wins and pays pi, ui = vi − pi; if the bidder loses, ui = 0.
  • Independent private values (IPV): each bidder’s value is drawn independently of the others’, and each knows only their own value.

This definition of utility is called quasi-linear utility. The word “linear” refers to the payment being subtracted one for one from utility: paying 1 TL more reduces utility by exactly 1. A losing bidder does not get the item and pays nothing; the utility is zero. Losing is therefore not a “penalty”; nothing simply happens. The bidder’s goal is to maximize utility.

Figure 5.2 shows utility as the splitting of a bar. The value is the whole bar; the payment “eats” part of the bar and the rest stays with the bidder. If the payment were larger than the value, the remainder would be negative; the bidder would lose out by getting the item. The danger of the “overbidding” strategy comes from this.

The IPV model has two parts. Private means that everyone knows their own value and that this value is not affected by what others know. Someone buying a painting for their own living room does not change their own value even on learning how much others like the painting. Independent means that values are generated as independent random numbers: knowing Ayşe’s value says nothing about Bora’s. IPV is the basic model of auction theory. Most of the theorems in this chapter hold in this model; when the model changes, the results may change too (5.1.5).

There are two more assumptions often used in the examples. The first is that values are uniformly distributed on the interval [0, 1]; this is written vi ~ U[0, 1]. The second is that bidders are risk-neutral: a risk-neutral bidder compares uncertain outcomes by their expected utilities. For example, such a bidder sees an option that gives utility 10 or 0 with equal probability as equivalent to one that gives a sure utility of 5. These two assumptions are chosen to keep the calculations simple. The case of risk-averse bidders is examined separately in 5.4.3.

Common mistake

Taking value and bid to be the same thing. The value is the true number in the bidder’s head and does not change; the bid is a decision made by looking at that value. A “truthful bid” means bi = vi; but under the first-price rule a rational bidder writes bi < vi. Utility is always computed from the value: ui = vi − pi; the bid does not enter the utility formula directly, it only affects whether you win and, under some rules, the payment.

5.1.4Probability tools used in the examples

The chapter’s calculations rest on a few simple probability tools. They are collected briefly below.

The uniform distribution. v ~ U[0, 1] means that v can take every value between 0 and 1 “with equal chance”. More precisely, the probability of falling in any interval inside [0, 1] equals the length of that interval. For example, P(v < 0.3) = 0.3 and P(0.2 < v < 0.7) = 0.5. The function that gives this probability is called the distribution function (CDF) and is written F(t) = P(v ≤ t); for U[0, 1], F(t) = t. The derivative of the distribution function is the density f(t); for U[0, 1], f(t) = 1. The density shows how thickly probability is spread over the values; in the uniform distribution it is the same everywhere.

Products of independent events. If two events are independent, the probability that both occur is the product of the probabilities. If two bidders’ values are independent U[0, 1], the probability of the event “both values are below 0.5” is 0.5 · 0.5 = 0.25. All the winning-probability formulas in the chapter follow from this product rule: a bidder must beat every rival to win, and if the rivals are independent these probabilities multiply.

Expected value. The expected value of a random quantity is the average of the values it takes, weighted by their probabilities. The average obtained by repeating an experiment many times approaches the expected value. For v ~ U[0, 1], E[v] = 1/2. Two kinds of expected value come up often in the chapter: a bidder’s expected utility (what a bid earns on average) and the seller’s expected revenue (what a rule brings the seller on average).

The tail formula. For a random X with values between 0 and 1, the expected value can also be computed with this formula:

E[X] = ∫01 P(X > t) dt

The meaning of the formula is as follows. Let X be the length of a bar starting at 0. At each point t, the probability that the bar extends past that point is P(X > t). Adding up these probabilities over all t (taking the integral) gives the average length of the bar. For example, taking X ~ U[0, 1] gives P(X > t) = 1 − t and ∫01 (1 − t) dt = 1 − 1/2 = 1/2; this is the familiar E[X] = 1/2. The tail formula is very useful for finding the expectation of the maximum or minimum of several numbers, because the probability of events such as “the largest is below t” is easily written with the product rule (5.4.1).

Integrals of polynomials. The chapter needs only integrals of polynomials: ∫0a tk dt = ak+1/(k + 1). For example, ∫01 t2 dt = 1/3 and ∫0r 2t2 dt = 2r3/3.

Derivatives and maxima. A bidder’s expected utility is a function of the bid. If this function first rises and then falls, it takes its largest value where the derivative is zero. Two rules are used: the derivative of tk is k tk−1, and the derivative of a product of two functions is (g h)' = g' h + g h' (the product rule).

Example 5.1

The values of three bidders are independent U[0, 1]. What is the probability that a bidder whose value is 0.6 has a value higher than both of the other two bidders?

  1. For a single rival, P(vj < 0.6) = F(0.6) = 0.6 (in the uniform distribution the probability is the length of the interval).
  2. The two rivals’ values are independent; the probability that both are below 0.6 is the product: 0.6 · 0.6 = 0.36.
  3. Result: the bidder’s value is the highest with probability 36%. In general, with n bidders this probability is vn−1.

5.1.5Valuation models

How bidders value the item determines how the auction is analyzed. Three basic models are distinguished in the literature.

Definition 5.2
  • Private value: each bidder determines the item’s value only from their own information and taste. An example is a painting to hang in one’s own home. The values differ: v1 ≠ v2 ≠ …
  • Common value: the item’s true value is the same for everyone, but no one knows it exactly; the participants’ estimates differ. An example is the market value of the oil in an oil field.
  • Interdependent values: a bidder’s value depends both on their own private information and on the others’ information: vi = f(own signal, other signals).

Figure 5.3 visualizes the difference among the three models. In the private value model, learning what others think changes no one’s value. In the common value model the situation is reversed: a firm that learns another firm rated the field low suspects that its own estimate may be too optimistic. Information is valuable because everyone’s estimate is a noisy measurement of the same truth.

Most real items lie somewhere between the two extremes. A work of art is valued both by personal taste (the private component) and by the price at which it can be sold later (the common component). In a corporate acquisition, the buyer’s own synergies are the private component and the target’s cash flows the common one. The interdependent values model represents this mixed case.

The difference among the models is not only theoretical. The common value model leads to a phenomenon called the winner’s curse: the winner of the auction is most likely the participant with the most optimistic estimate of the value, and if this is not taken into account the winner loses on average. This phenomenon is examined numerically in 5.4.8. For the rest of the chapter the IPV model is assumed unless stated otherwise.

5.2The four basic formats

Auction formats are classified by two criteria. The first is how bids are made: in front of everyone while the price changes (an open, “outcry” auction) or once and secretly (sealed-bid). The second is what the winner pays: their own bid or the second-highest bid. Combining the two criteria gives the four basic formats. Running all four on the same four-person example shows the differences clearly.

The English (ascending) auction is the classic format seen at auction houses. The auctioneer starts at a low price and raises it step by step. When the price passes the amount a bidder is willing to pay, that bidder drops out. When one person remains, the auction ends; the last one remaining buys the item at the price at which the last rival dropped out. In theoretical analysis the “clock” version is usually used: the price rises continuously and bidders drop out by releasing a button. A bidder’s only decision is then reduced to the question “at what price will I drop out?”

The Dutch (descending) auction takes its name from the flower auctions in the Netherlands. The price starts at a high value and falls. The first to say “accept” buys the item at the current price, and the auction ends immediately. Because it is fast, it is used in flower and fish markets where many small lots are sold.

The first-price sealed-bid auction is the familiar form of public tenders. Each bidder submits a bid once and secretly. The highest bid wins and the winner pays their own bid.

The second-price (Vickrey) auction is also secret and one-shot. The winner is again the highest bidder, but the amount paid is the second-highest bid.

To compare the four formats the same four people are used. The highest amount each person is willing to pay for the item, that is, their limit, is: Ayşe 70, Bora 55, Cem 85, Deniz 60. These limits are private values. For now we assume that everyone bids their limit, that is, stays in an English auction up to their limit and accepts in a Dutch auction when the price comes down to their limit. When this assumption is sensible is examined in 5.3.

5.2.2The English auction, step by step

Let the price start at 40 and rise slowly. Each bidder stays in as long as the price is below their own limit.

Example 5.2
  1. Price 50: the smallest limit is 55; everyone is in (Figure 5.4).
  2. When the price passes 55, Bora drops out, because buying at this price would cost him (his limit is 55). Remaining: Ayşe, Cem, Deniz.
  3. When the price passes 60, Deniz drops out (her limit is 60). Remaining: Ayşe, Cem.
  4. When the price passes 70, the last rival, Ayşe, drops out. Cem is left alone and wins.
  5. Payment: Cem pays the price at which Ayşe dropped out, that is, about 70. If the price rises in steps of 1 TL, Cem pays 70 or 71; as the increment shrinks the payment approaches exactly 70. Cem’s utility is about 85 − 70 = 15.

The outcome seen in Figure 5.5 is a general property of the English auction: the winner is the person with the highest value, and the price paid is the second-highest value. The reason is simple. The auction continues until the person with the second-highest value drops out; when that person drops out no rival remains. Cem’s own limit of 85 has no effect on the price he pays. Had it been 75 or 100 instead of 85, Cem would still have won at 70. This is the same as the Vickrey observation in 5.1.1.

With private values, a bidder’s best strategy in an English auction is to stay in up to their limit. Dropping out before the limit may forgo a profit that could have been made: Ayşe, dropping out at 65, could have bought the item at a price just over 60 had Cem not been there. Staying in beyond the limit, on the other hand, risks winning at a loss. This reasoning is the English-auction counterpart of the truthfulness proof for the Vickrey auction (5.3.2).

5.2.3The Dutch auction, step by step

In a Dutch auction the price starts at 100 and falls by 5 at each step. Each bidder says “accept” when the price comes down to their limit.

Example 5.3
  1. Price 100: this price is above everyone’s limit; no one accepts, and the price falls by 5.
  2. Price 95: the highest limit is 85; still no one accepts.
  3. Price 90: again no one accepts.
  4. Price 85: the price reaches Cem’s limit. Cem says “accept” and pays 85. The auction ends at this moment; the others never get to speak.
  5. Result: Cem wins and pays 85; his utility is 85 − 85 = 0.

Comparing Figure 5.6 with the English auction figures shows an important difference. In the English auction, information emerges during the process: everyone sees who drops out at which price. In the Dutch auction, because the first to accept wins, the auction ends and no one learns anything about anyone else’s limit. A bidder answers only a single question in advance: “at what price will I accept?” The answer is a single number, and in effect this number is a hidden bid. The consequences of this observation are discussed in 5.2.5.

In the example Cem’s utility came out zero because he accepted at his limit. This is not realistic behavior. Had Cem waited a little instead of accepting at 85, for example accepting at 75, he would still have won as long as the rivals’ limits were below 75, and would have gained 10. The risk of waiting is that when the price falls to a rival’s limit, that rival accepts first. This trade-off is the same as bid shading in the first-price auction.

5.2.4First-price and second-price sealed-bid

In a sealed-bid auction everyone submits a bid once and secretly. No one sees the others’ bids; the envelopes are opened at the same time. If the four people write their limits, the envelopes contain 70, 55, 85, and 60.

Example 5.4
  1. The envelopes are opened: Ayşe 70, Bora 55, Cem 85, Deniz 60. Under both rules the winner is Cem, who made the highest bid.
  2. First price: Cem pays his own bid, 85. Since his value is 85, his utility is 85 − 85 = 0.
  3. Second price (Vickrey): Cem pays the second-highest bid, Ayşe’s 70. His utility is 85 − 70 = 15.

Under the first-price rule the winner’s payment equals their own bid; under the second-price rule it is independent of their own bid. In Figure 5.7, however long Cem’s bar is, the payment line stays at 70. This single difference completely changes the behavior of a clever bidder under the two rules: under second price, writing your value is best; under first price, you must go below your value.

Common mistake

Looking at Table 5.3 and concluding that “first price earns the seller more, because 85 > 70”. In the table everyone bids their limit; this assumption is not rational under the first-price rule. A rational bidder shades the bid under first price, that is, bids below the value. Once shading is taken into account, the expected revenues the two rules bring the seller become equal. This result is called revenue equivalence and is proved in 5.4.2.

Example 5.5

Let the limits be 40, 65, 80, and 90, and let everyone bid their limit. Who wins under the four rules and what do they pay?

  1. Under all four rules the winner is the bidder with the highest limit, 90.
  2. English: when the price passes 40 and 65 two bidders drop out; when it passes 80 the last rival drops out. Payment ≈ 80, utility ≈ 10.
  3. Second price: the envelopes are 40, 65, 80, 90; the payment is the second-highest bid, 80; utility 10.
  4. Dutch: when the price falls to 90 the winner accepts; payment 90, utility 0.

5.3Sealed-bid strategies

In the earlier examples everyone was assumed to bid their limit. Once this assumption is dropped, the question is: what should a clever bidder write in the envelope? The answer differs greatly by rule. Under the second-price rule the answer is “your value”, and it does not depend on what the rivals do. Under the first-price rule the answer depends on the rivals’ behavior and requires an equilibrium computation.

5.3.1The strategic property of the Vickrey auction

The rule of the Vickrey auction is this: the highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid. The most important consequence of this rule is that the bidder need not guess the rivals. To state this we first recall the concept of a dominant strategy.

Definition 5.3

A strategy is a weakly dominant strategy if, against every possible choice of the others, it does as well as any other strategy and against at least one choice it does strictly better. In an auction this means that the bidder’s utility is, for every bid profile of the rivals, at least as large as with any other bid and for some profiles strictly larger.

Theorem 5.1 (Vickrey, 1961)

In the single-item model with independent private values, in a second-price sealed-bid auction, bidding the true value (bi = vi) is a weakly dominant strategy for every bidder.

The idea behind the theorem can be put in one sentence: the bid determines only whether you win, not what you pay. If the bidder wins, they pay the rivals’ highest bid; this amount is independent of the number in their own envelope. The only job of the number written in the envelope is therefore to set a threshold: if the rivals’ highest bid is below this threshold the bidder wins, if above, the bidder loses. The best threshold is the point that separates where winning is profitable from where it is costly; and that point is the bidder’s value itself.

Figure 5.8 is a visual summary of the proof. The horizontal axis is the rivals’ highest bid B. When B is below 70, the truthful bidder wins and gets utility 70 − B; this utility falls linearly as B grows. When B passes 70, the truthful bidder loses and the utility is 0. A bidder who bids 50 loses when B is between 50 and 70, although winning would have been profitable in this range (gray region). A bidder who bids 90 wins when B is between 70 and 90, but winning is costly in this range (red region, negative utility). Each deviation does the same as the truthful bid at some values of B and worse at others; it is nowhere better.

The conditions of validity of the theorem matter. The result is proved in the single-item model with independent private values. It may not hold in the same form with common values, with budget constraints, or when values are interdependent. For example, a bidder whose budget is smaller than their value cannot bid their value; with common values the “value” is not even known. The Vickrey auction is the single-item special case of the VCG (Vickrey–Clarke–Groves) mechanism of mechanism design. In VCG too, each participant pays the harm they impose on the others; for a single item this harm is the second-highest value.

5.3.2Proof of truthfulness, step by step

The proof is a case analysis: the possible cases are examined one by one for the rivals’ highest bid B = maxj≠i bj. The bidder does not know B; but since the proof is carried out separately for every B, this ignorance is no problem. We begin with a numerical example: the value is v = 70 and three strategies are compared: truthful b = 70, over b = 90, under b = 50.

Example 5.6
  1. Truthful, B = 60: since 70 > 60, the bidder wins and pays 60; utility 70 − 60 = 10.
  2. Truthful, B = 80: since 70 < 80, the bidder loses; utility 0. The truthful bid always makes the right decision: it wins when there is a profit and loses when there would be a loss.
  3. Over b = 90, B = 60: B < v. It wins again and pays 60 again; utility 10. No difference from truthful.
  4. Over b = 90, B = 80: B is between v and b. It wins but pays 80; utility 70 − 80 = −10. Truthful would have got 0: a loss.

The same reasoning is written in general as follows.

Let the bidder’s value be v and the rivals’ highest bid B. With any bid, two outcomes are possible: winning (utility v − B, because the winner pays B) or losing (utility 0). So, with B fixed, whatever bid is made the utility is either v − B or 0. The larger of these two numbers is max(v − B, 0), and no bid can give more than this.

The truthful bid gives exactly this maximum. If B < v, the truthful bidder wins and gets v − B > 0. If B > v, the bidder loses and gets 0; in this case v − B < 0, so 0 is better. If B = v, both outcomes give utility 0 and how the tie is broken is immaterial. So for every B the truthful bid gives the largest attainable utility; no other bid can be better than it.

For strictness it suffices to show that every deviation is strictly worse somewhere. If b > v, then for a B with v < B < b the deviating bidder wins and gets v − B < 0; the truthful bid would have given 0. If b < v, then for a B with b < B < v the deviating bidder loses and gets 0; the truthful bid would have given v − B > 0. Thus b = v is a weakly dominant strategy. □

The proof never used how the rivals bid. The rivals may be truthful, may lie, may act at random; the result does not change. This is why dominant strategy is a stronger concept than Nash equilibrium: in a Nash equilibrium a player’s strategy is best against the rivals’ equilibrium strategies, whereas a dominant strategy is best against every strategy of the rivals. Mechanisms with this property are called strategy-proof or dominant-strategy incentive compatible (DSIC).

Common mistake

Thinking that “a truthful bid guarantees winning” or that “a truthful bid always gives positive utility”. A truthful bid loses when B > v and gets utility 0. Weak dominance says that the truthful bid is at least as good as any other bid in every case; it does not say that it pays off in every case. Another common mistake is to think that dominance holds “if the rivals are truthful too”; the proof is independent of the rivals’ behavior.

5.3.3Back to the game: theory and lab

The theoretical answer to the opening question in 5.1.1 is now clear: write your value in the envelope. In the opening envelopes the player wrote 70 and the rivals 60 and 45. With the truthful bid the player wins and pays 60; the gain is 10. Had the player written 65 or 100, the player would still win and pay the same 60. Had the player written 55, the item would go to the rival who wrote 60 and the player would miss the gain of 10. When the bid is moved up or down from the value, the gain does not increase for any B; it only decreases for some values of B.

The answer to the third opening question is also clear. Under the first-price rule, a player who writes 70 gets a gain of 70 − 70 = 0 even if winning. There the bid must be shaded below the value; how to shade it is the subject of 5.3.4.

Real behavior may differ from theory. In laboratory experiments it is common to bid above the value in second-price sealed-bid auctions, and this does not disappear easily even when the experiment is repeated. The same subjects approach equilibrium much faster in the ascending-price English auction (Kagel, Harstad, and Levin, 1987). One possible reason is this: the cost of overbidding appears only when B falls in a certain range, and this rarely happens; because subjects seldom see the loss, they do not change their habits. In an English auction, on the other hand, the moment the price passes the value is in plain view and dropping out is a natural decision.

A very close everyday example of the second-price rule is eBay’s automatic (proxy) bidding system. The user tells the system the highest amount they are willing to pay; on the user’s behalf the system bids the smallest amount that beats the rivals. The winner ends up paying a price one increment above the second-highest declared amount. This is an incremental version of the second-price rule.

5.3.4Bid shading under first price

Under the first-price rule a truthful bid is pointless: even if the bidder wins, the utility is v − v = 0. Moving the bid below the value is called bid shading. Shading creates a trade-off. Lowering the bid increases the profit left on winning (v − b) but reduces the probability of winning. A bid’s expected utility is the product of these two effects:

E[u(b)] = (v − b) · P(winning with b)

The first factor in the formula is the profit on winning, the second the probability of that profit. Since the utility is 0 on losing, no other term enters the formula. At b = 0 the probability of winning is 0; at b = v the profit is 0. At both ends the expected utility is zero; the best bid lies somewhere in between.

The probability of winning depends on how the rivals bid. So under first price the best bid, unlike under second price, is not independent of the rivals. When each bidder’s strategy depends on the others’, the right solution concept is an equilibrium. Here, however, each bidder does not know the rivals’ values, only their distribution. The equilibrium concept used in games of incomplete information of this kind is Bayes–Nash equilibrium.

Definition 5.4

Each bidder’s strategy is a function from value to bid: bi = βi(vi). A strategy profile is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium if, when the others play their strategies and their values are random according to their distributions, βi(vi) maximizes expected utility for every bidder i and every value vi. If everyone uses the same function, the equilibrium is symmetric.

The definition carries the idea of Nash equilibrium, “no one wants to deviate alone”, over to incomplete information. The difference is that the deviation is evaluated through expected utility: because the bidder does not know the rivals’ values, the bidder considers the average outcome of each bid.

Figure 5.10 makes the trade-off concrete. The rival’s value is U[0, 1] and the rival bids half of it; that is, the rival’s bid is uniformly distributed between 0 and 0.5. To win with bid b, the rival’s bid must be below b: P(vr/2 < b) = P(vr < 2b) = 2b (for b ≤ 0.5). The expected utility is then (0.8 − b) · 2b. This is a piece of a parabola; its derivative 2(0.8 − b) − 2b = 1.6 − 4b is zero at b = 0.4. At the peak the expected utility is (0.8 − 0.4) · 0.8 = 0.32.

The Dutch auction is strategically the same as this analysis. The answer to the question “at what price will I accept?” is a shaded bid. A bidder with value 0.8 accepts when the price falls to 0.4, when the rival behaves in the same way.

5.3.5Deriving the equilibrium bid

In the general case there are n bidders and the values are independent U[0, 1]. We look for a symmetric equilibrium. To do so we assume a guessed form, compute the best response, and show that the result is consistent with the assumption. The assumption is this: the rivals bid a fixed fraction of their values.

Example 5.7
  1. Assumption. Let the other n − 1 bidders bid bj = β · vj, where β is a constant between 0 and 1 and vj ~ U[0, 1].
  2. Winning probability. To win with bid b, every rival’s bid must be below b. For one rival, P(β vj < b) = P(vj < b/β) = b/β (for b ≤ β; in the uniform distribution the probability is the length of the interval). Since the rivals are independent, the probabilities multiply: P(win) = (b/β)n−1.
  3. Expected utility. Profit on winning times the probability of winning: E[u(b)] = (v − b) · (b/β)n−1.
  4. Derivative. The derivative of (v − b) is −1, and that of bn−1 is (n − 1) bn−2. Combining with the product rule and factoring out the common factor bn−2/βn−1 gives: dE[u(b)]/db = [ −b + (n − 1)(v − b) ] · bn−2/βn−1. At the maximum the derivative is zero.
  5. Solution. The outside factor is positive when b > 0; so the bracket must be zero: −b + (n − 1)(v − b) = 0. Expanding gives −b + (n − 1)v − (n − 1)b = 0, that is, n b = (n − 1)v and b = (n − 1)v/n. After simplification the solution comes out independent of β.
  6. Consistency. The best response found is “bid (n − 1)/n times your value”. This has the same form as the assumed one, and if β = (n − 1)/n is chosen, everyone is giving the best response to the rivals: a symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium. For n = 2, b = v/2: half the value with two bidders.

That the solution found in the fifth step is a maximum can also be checked from the sign of the derivative. The bracket equals (n − 1)v − n b. It is positive when b is below the equilibrium bid and negative when above. So the expected utility first rises, peaks at the equilibrium bid, and then falls. The point found is indeed the maximum. Also, bids b > β always win, but since at b = β the bidder already wins with probability 1, these bids simply mean paying more; the best bid does not fall in this region.

The expected utility of a bidder with value v in equilibrium can also be computed. While the rivals bid β vj, the bidder bids β v; this bid wins only if every rival’s value is below v. The winning probability is vn−1 and the profit is v − (n − 1)v/n = v/n. The product is vn/n. For n = 2, v = 0.8 this gives 0.64/2 = 0.32, the same as the peak value in Figure 5.10.

The formula can also be checked numerically. For n = 2, 3, 5, 10 and v = 0.3; 0.6; 0.8, when E[u(b)] is maximized on a grid of 10,001 points, the best bid coincides with (n − 1)v/n in every case.

Common mistake

Taking the equilibrium bid to be a rule independent of the rivals’ behavior. b = (n − 1)v/n is best when the values are U[0, 1] and the rivals bid in proportion to their values (in the form βv); β itself does not change the result (as long as the candidate bid does not exceed β). For example, if the single rival (n = 2) bid truthfully, the winning probability would be P(vr < b) = b and the best response maximizing (v − b) · b would again be b = v/2; but when the distribution changes, so does the formula. The truthful bid in Vickrey, on the other hand, is best in every case; the two results are not equally strong.

5.3.6Code trace: best_bid()

The result of the derivation can be compared with the best bid found by a computer program by brute force. The function below computes the expected utility of a bidder with value v, while the rivals bid β v, for every bid on the grid 0, step, 2·step, … and returns the largest.

def best_bid(v, n, step=0.1):
  beta = (n - 1) / n        # rivals bid beta*v
  best, b_best = -1, 0
  b = 0.0
  while b <= v:
    p = min(1, b / beta) ** (n - 1)  # P(win)
    eu = (v - b) * p
    if eu > best:
      best, b_best = eu, b
    b = round(b + step, 10)
  return b_best, best

The code works line by line as follows:

  • beta = (n - 1) / n: the fraction of their values that the rivals bid. For n = 2 it is 0.5.
  • best, b_best = -1, 0: the largest expected utility so far and the bid that gives it. Initially best is −1; since expected utility is never negative, the first bid certainly becomes the “new best”.
  • while b <= v: bids are tried from 0 up to the value. Bidding above the value is pointless because it gives negative utility on winning.
  • p = min(1, b / beta) ** (n - 1): the winning probability (b/β)n−1. The guard min(1, …) prevents the ratio from exceeding 1 when b > β; a probability cannot be larger than 1.
  • eu = (v - b) * p: the expected utility. If it is larger, the best is updated.
  • b = round(b + step, 10): the next bid. round removes the small errors that arise because 0.1 cannot be represented exactly in binary (e.g. 0.30000000000000004); otherwise at the last step b could slightly exceed 0.8 and the loop could end early.

5.4Revenue and information

So far the question has been asked from the bidder’s point of view: what should I bid? The seller’s question is different: which rule earns me more? The answer is surprising: under standard assumptions, all the basic rules bring the same revenue on average. Which assumptions break this equality, how the seller can raise revenue (the reserve price), and the winner’s curse that arises when information is common are all handled in the same framework.

5.4.1Expected revenue with two bidders

We start with the simplest case: there are two bidders, and their values v1, v2 are independent U[0, 1]. Should the seller choose the second-price rule or the first-price rule? For each rule we first write down the seller’s revenue in a single auction and then compute the expectation of that revenue.

Example 5.9
  1. Second price: revenue. Everyone bids truthfully (Theorem 5.1). The winner is the bidder with the larger value and pays the smaller value. The seller’s revenue is min(v1, v2).
  2. Second price: expectation. If the smaller of the two values exceeds t, both exceed t. By independence, P(min > t) = (1 − t) · (1 − t) = (1 − t)2. By the tail formula, E[min] = ∫01 (1 − t)2 dt. With the substitution s = 1 − t the integral becomes ∫01 s2 ds = 1/3.
  3. First price: revenue. In equilibrium everyone bids b = v/2 (5.3.5). The winner is again the bidder with the larger value and pays their own bid, that is, half the larger value. The revenue is max(v1, v2)/2.
  4. First price: expectation. If the larger of the two values is below t, both are: P(max < t) = t2, so P(max > t) = 1 − t2. By the tail formula, E[max] = ∫01 (1 − t2) dt = 1 − 1/3 = 2/3. The revenue is half of this: 2/3 · 1/2 = 1/3.
  5. English and Dutch. With private values the English auction gives the same result as second price: 1/3. The Dutch auction is strategically identical to first price: 1/3.
  6. Result. The four rules give the same expected revenue: 1/3.

5.4.2The revenue equivalence theorem

The equality with two bidders is no coincidence; it is a special case of a more general theorem.

Theorem 5.2 (Revenue equivalence)

With independent private values, risk-neutral and symmetric bidders, and the same allocation rule (the highest value wins), standard auctions in which the lowest-value type pays zero yield the same expected revenue (Vickrey, 1961; Myerson, 1981; Riley and Samuelson, 1981).

Why the theorem is true is explained by Myerson’s payment formula. In an auction, let the winning probability of a bidder with value v be x(v) and the expected payment p(v). If the bidders behave according to equilibrium, there is the following relation between the two:

p(v) = v · x(v) − ∫0v x(z) dz

On the right-hand side of the formula there is only the allocation rule x(·). The idea behind the formula is Myerson’s lemma from mechanism design: since a bidder with value v could also choose to act as though their value were lower, in equilibrium the expected utility must rise with v exactly at the rate of the winning probability. This condition, once the utility of the lowest type (here 0) is fixed, determines the expected payment uniquely. The conclusion is: the same x(·) → the same p(·) → the same expected revenue. In all standard auctions the bidder with the highest value wins, so x(·) is the same; their revenues are therefore the same.

Example 5.10

Two bidders, U[0, 1]. The expected payment of the bidder with value v is computed in three ways.

  1. By the formula. To win, the rival’s value must be below v: x(v) = v. Then p(v) = v · v − ∫0v z dz = v2 − v2/2 = v2/2.
  2. Under first price. The bid is v/2 and the winning probability v; on winning the bid is paid. The expected payment is v/2 · v = v2/2.
  3. Under second price. The winning probability is v; on winning the rival’s value is paid. Given that the rival’s value is below v, it is uniformly distributed on [0, v] and its average is v/2. The expected payment is v · v/2 = v2/2.
  4. Passing to revenue. The three routes give the same result. The seller’s expected revenue is the sum of the two bidders’ expected payments: 2 · E[v2/2] = E[v2] = ∫01 t2 dt = 1/3.

The same computation can be done for n bidders. Then x(v) = vn−1, p(v) = vn − vn/n = (n − 1)vn/n, and the expected revenue is n · (n − 1)/n · E[vn] = (n − 1)/(n + 1). The same result is reached from the second-price side: the expectation of the second-largest of n independent U[0, 1] values is (n − 1)/(n + 1). From the first-price side, the product of the expectation of the largest value, n/(n + 1), and the bid ratio (n − 1)/n is again (n − 1)/(n + 1).

The assumptions of the theorem matter. Risk aversion, budget constraints, common values, or asymmetric information can change the result. Common mistake

Reading revenue equivalence as “in every auction the two rules give the same price”. The theorem is about expected revenue; in a single auction the revenues are often different (the example of 0.9 and 0.2 in 5.4.1). Another mistake is to generalize the theorem to all rules: a rule that gives the item to a random bidder, or does not give it to the highest-value bidder, makes a different allocation and brings a different revenue.

5.4.3Changing the assumption: risk aversion

One assumption of revenue equivalence is that bidders are risk-neutral. What happens when this assumption is dropped? We build a model with two bidders and U[0, 1] values. Let the winner’s profit be x = v − price and the utility be

u(x) = xρ, 0 < ρ ≤ 1

Here ρ = 1 is the risk-neutral bidder: utility is the profit itself. ρ < 1 is a risk-averse bidder; for example for ρ = ½, u(x) = √x. The square-root curve is steep at small profits and flat at large ones. This means that a risk-averse person prefers a “small but certain” profit to a “large but uncertain” one: the first few lira of profit seem very valuable, the rest less so.

Second price does not change. The price is set by the rival’s bid, not by one’s own. The proof in 5.3.2 used nothing but the utility being an increasing function of profit; the truthful bid is therefore still weakly dominant. The revenue is again E[min] = 1/3.

Under first price the bid rises. If the rival bids β vr, the probability of winning with bid b is b/β and the expected utility is (v − b)ρ · b/β. Taking the derivative gives [−ρ (v − b)ρ−1 b + (v − b)ρ]/β. Setting it to zero and dropping the common factor (v − b)ρ−1 gives −ρ b + (v − b) = 0, that is,

b(v) = v / (1 + ρ)

is obtained. For ρ = 1 it gives v/2, the familiar risk-neutral equilibrium; for ρ = ½ it gives 2v/3. The risk-averse bidder bids higher to reduce the risk of losing; in a sense, the bidder pays an “insurance premium” to raise the probability of winning.

  1. Equilibrium bid: b = 0.6/(1 + 0.5) = 0.4. A risk-neutral bidder would bid 0.3.
  2. Winning probability: the rival bids 2vr/3; for 2vr/3 < 0.4 we need vr < 0.6: the probability is 0.6.
  3. On winning, the profit is 0.6 − 0.4 = 0.2 and the utility √0.2 ≈ 0.447. The expected utility is 0.6 · 0.447 ≈ 0.268.

5.4.4Revenue maximization: Myerson and the reserve price

Revenue equivalence says that standard auctions bring the same revenue. But what should the seller do if the seller sets efficiency aside and wants only to maximize expected revenue? Roger Myerson answered this question (Myerson, 1981). The key to the answer is the concept of virtual value.

Definition 5.5

For a bidder whose value distribution is F with density f, the virtual value is

ψ(v) = v − (1 − F(v)) / f(v)

Here F(v) is the probability that the value is below v, and f(v) is the density. For U[0, 1], since F(v) = v and f(v) = 1, ψ(v) = v − (1 − v) = 2v − 1.

The virtual value measures how much revenue the seller can really extract from a bidder. The value v cannot be collected in full, because the bidder can hide the value and the seller has to leave the bidder an “information rent” as an incentive to be truthful. The subtracted term (1 − F)/f is this rent. Myerson’s basic result is this: expected revenue equals the expectation of the winner’s virtual value. The mechanism that maximizes revenue therefore gives the item to the bidder with the largest virtual value; if even the largest virtual value is negative, it does not sell the item, because selling to someone with a negative virtual value reduces expected revenue.

If the virtual value is an increasing function and the bidders are symmetric, “the largest virtual value” and “the largest value” belong to the same person. The only difference is that no sale is made to those whose virtual value is negative. This is the second-price auction with a reserve price: the seller announces in advance a reserve price r; bids below r are invalid. The reserve is the point at which the virtual value is zero:

ψ(r) = 0 ⇒ 2r − 1 = 0 ⇒ r = 1/2

This threshold depends only on the value distribution; it is independent of the number of bidders n. For U[0, 1] the best reserve is 1/2 with two bidders and with ten.

The rules of the second-price auction with a reserve are as follows. If the highest bid is below r, the item is not sold. If only one bid is above r, the winner pays r. If two bids are above r, the winner pays the second-highest bid. In short, the winner pays max(r, second-highest bid). The reserve is as if the seller had also placed a bid; truthful bidding is weakly dominant under this rule too.

With two bidders, the expected revenue of the second-price auction with a reserve can be computed with the virtual-value result. The revenue is the expectation of the winner’s virtual value, and the item is sold only when the largest value reaches r (is at least r). The density of the larger of the two values is 2t (the derivative of P(max < t) = t2). Then

E[revenue] = ∫r1 (2t − 1) · 2t dt = [4t3/3 − t2]r1 = 1/3 + r2 − 4r3/3

At r = 0 the revenue of the second price without a reserve comes out as 1/3. At r = 1/2 it is 1/3 + 1/4 − 1/6 = 5/12 ≈ 0.417. The reserve raises revenue by about 25%. This has a cost: sometimes there is no sale at all, and the item stays with the seller although there is a buyer who values it. This efficiency loss is computed in 5.4.6.

Example 5.12

Two bidders, U[0, 1], reserve r = 1/2. The outcome is found for three pairs of values.

  1. (0.8, 0.3): only 0.8 passes the reserve. The winner pays max(0.5, 0.3) = 0.5. Without a reserve the winner would have paid 0.3.
  2. (0.9, 0.7): both pass the reserve. The winner pays max(0.5, 0.7) = 0.7; the reserve does not affect this auction.
  3. (0.4, 0.2): both are below the reserve; the item is not sold. Without a reserve the bidder with 0.4 would have bought it for 0.2.

The reserve pushes the price up when the rival is weak (pair 1), but loses the sale when both bidders are weak (pair 3). On average the first effect dominates.

5.4.5Code trace: best_reserve()

To find the best reserve numerically, two functions can be written. revenue(r) approximates on a grid the expected revenue of a two-bidder second-price auction with reserve r; best_reserve() tries the values r = 0; 0.1; …; 1.

def revenue(r, m=100):      # 2 bidders, U[0,1]
  v = [(i + .5) / m for i in range(m)]
  total = sum(max(min(a, c), r) for a in v
            for c in v if max(a, c) >= r)
  return total / m**2       # mean payment
def best_reserve(step=0.1):
  best, r_best = -1, None
  for k in range(round(1/step) + 1):
    R = revenue(k * step)
    if R > best: best, r_best = R, k * step
  return r_best, best

The code works line by line as follows:

  • v = [(i + .5) / m for i in range(m)]: the interval [0, 1] is divided into m = 100 equal parts and the midpoint of each part is taken: 0.005; 0.015; …; 0.995. These points represent the uniform distribution.
  • Two nested loops run over all value pairs (a, c); there are 100 × 100 = 10,000 pairs and each is equally likely.
  • if max(a, c) >= r: if the largest value does not reach the reserve there is no sale and the revenue is 0; these pairs do not take part in the sum.
  • max(min(a, c), r): if there is a sale, the winner pays the larger of the smaller value and the reserve.
  • total / m**2: the total payment is divided by the number of pairs; pairs without a sale are counted with a contribution of 0. The result approximates the expected revenue.
  • best_reserve: tries r = k · step for k = 0, 1, …, 10. range(round(1/step) + 1) ensures that r = 1 is tried as well. When a larger revenue is found, the best is updated.

5.4.6The cost of a reserve

A well-chosen reserve can raise the seller’s expected revenue (a very high reserve, for example r = 1, brings revenue down to zero), but it can reduce the total benefit society gets from the item, that is, social welfare. With two bidders and reserve r, three quantities are computed.

  • Expected revenue: 1/3 + r2 − 4r3/3 (5.4.4).
  • Probability of no sale: both values must be below r: r · r = r2.
  • Expected loss of social welfare: in the efficient outcome the bidder with the highest value gets the item and welfare equals this value. If this value is below r, the item is not sold and the value is lost. Since the density of the largest value is 2t, the loss is ∫0r t · 2t dt = 2r3/3.

Common mistake

Thinking that “the higher the reserve, the higher the revenue”. As the reserve rises, the winner’s payment goes up, but the probability of a sale goes down. The two effects balance at r = 1/2; beyond it the second effect dominates. Another mistake is to think that the best reserve changes with the number of bidders: for U[0, 1] the condition ψ(r) = 0 does not involve n.

5.4.7All-pay and multi-unit auctions

In an all-pay auction the highest bid wins but everyone pays their bid. At first sight it looks odd, but it models many real contests: lobbying, R&D races, election campaigns. In all of these the prize is won by one person, but the effort and money spent do not return to the losers.

With two bidders and U[0, 1] values, in the symmetric equilibrium the bid is b(v) = v2/2. This result follows easily from revenue equivalence. In the all-pay rule too the highest value wins, so the allocation rule is the same. The expected payment of a bidder with value v is therefore the same as under first and second price, v2/2 (5.4.2). Since in all-pay the bid is paid in every case, the expected payment is the bid itself: b(v) = v2/2. A bidder with value 0.8 bids 0.32; under first price the bid would be 0.4. Types with a low chance of winning hardly bid at all: a bidder with value 0.2 bids only 0.02.

The seller’s expected revenue is again 1/3. This is a strong demonstration of revenue equivalence: however different the rules look, as long as the highest value wins, the average revenue does not change.

When values are equal and known to all, the all-pay model has an interesting result. For example, if two firms spend on lobbying for a prize of value V (e.g. a contract), in equilibrium the expected total spending equals the value of the prize. The entire value of the prize “dissipates” in the contest; this phenomenon is called rent dissipation and explains the cost of competitive lobbying in public policy.

Multi-unit auctions are auctions in which several units are sold at the same time. If the units are identical (for example government bonds), there are two basic rules. In a uniform-price auction every unit won is paid for at the same unit price; whoever takes two units pays twice what the one who takes one unit pays. In a discriminatory (pay-as-bid) auction, for each accepted unit the bid placed on that unit is paid. If the units are worth more together (for example frequency licenses for neighboring regions), bidders are allowed to place package bids for bundles of units: a bidder can bid a single price for a whole bundle. A package bid is a form of bidding; the payment rule is chosen separately. VCG is one of the payment rules that can be used in this setting; for example, core-selecting package auctions use the “core” prices that are closest to the VCG payments (Day and Milgrom, 2008). Choosing the winning bundles is also computationally hard.

There is another way to raise revenue. Bulow and Klemperer (1996) showed that, when values are independent and come from the same (well-behaved) distribution, attracting one extra bidder to an auction without a reserve earns as much as an auction run with the best reserve price, and often more. In the U[0, 1] example this is clearly visible: with two bidders and the best reserve the revenue is 5/12 ≈ 0.417; with three bidders and no reserve it is (3 − 1)/(3 + 1) = 1/2. For the seller, increasing competition can be worth more than fine-tuning the rules.

5.4.8The winner’s curse

In a common-value auction the item’s true value is the same for everyone, but no one knows it. Each participant estimates the value and the estimates contain errors: some are above the true value, some below. The highest bid in the auction most probably comes from the participant who estimates the value highest. By winning, this participant also learns that their own estimate is most probably too optimistic.

Definition 5.6

The winner’s curse: in a common-value auction, because the winner bids without taking into account the information that winning carries (that their estimate is probably too high), the winner on average pays more than the item’s true value and loses money.

The essence of the curse is a statistical fact: even when estimates are correct on average, the largest estimate is systematically high. The average of five estimates can equal the true value; but the largest of the five is almost always above the true value. The winner is exactly the owner of this largest estimate. To reduce this risk, participants keep their bids below their observations and take the quality of their information into account.

The phenomenon was first documented in oil lease auctions in the Gulf of Mexico. Engineers at the Atlantic Richfield company noticed that the fields they won systematically returned less than expected, and gave the phenomenon the name “winner’s curse” (Capen, Clapp, and Campbell, 1971).

The size of the curse can be computed with a model. Let each firm’s estimate be V + ei and let the estimation errors ei be independent and U[−20, 20]. An error can be written as −20 + 40 u, where u ~ U[0, 1]. Since the expectation of the largest of n numbers U[0, 1] is n/(n + 1), the expectation of the largest error is

−20 + 40 · n/(n + 1) = 20(n − 1)/(n + 1)

The result is 6.7 with two firms, 13.3 with five, and 16.4 with ten. The curse grows as rivals increase, because the most optimistic member of a large group is more optimistic.

Figure 5.16 yields a result that runs against intuition. With private values, the bid approaches the value as rivals increase (5.3.7). With common values, as rivals increase the bid should move further from the estimate, because winning means being the most optimistic of the crowd. The more rivals there are, the more one must shade.

Common mistake

Applying the winner’s curse to private-value auctions. With private values the bidder knows their own value; what others think does not change it, and winning carries no bad news. The curse arises only when the value is common (or partly common) and estimates are noisy. Another mistake is to think that “the curse shrinks as rivals increase”; on the contrary, as rivals increase the largest estimate gets higher.

5.5Two-sided markets

So far there has been a single seller and many buyers. In stock exchanges, however, both sides are crowded: many buyers and many sellers place orders at the same time. Such markets are organized by the double auction. The matching and price-setting rule, the incentive problems of this rule, and the McAfee rule as a solution to those problems are the basic pieces of this structure. Auctions are part of a wider field, market design.

5.5.1The double auction

Definition 5.7

A double auction is a mechanism in which several buyers and several sellers place orders at the same time. Buyers submit bids: the highest amount they are willing to pay for one unit. Sellers submit asks: the lowest amount they will accept for one unit. The list of all orders is called the order book.

Stock exchanges, electricity markets, and carbon allowance markets have this structure. The mechanism does two jobs: it decides who trades with whom (matching) and it decides the price at which the trade takes place.

  • Matching: bids are sorted from largest to smallest and asks from smallest to largest. The most eager buyer is matched with the cheapest seller, the second most eager buyer with the second cheapest seller, and so on. Pairs are matched as long as the bid is greater than or equal to the ask.
  • Price: a single market price is chosen from the interval between the last matched pair and the first unmatched pair; for example, the midpoint of the interval. Everyone who is matched trades at this price.
  • Total surplus: the value created by a trade is the difference between the buyer’s value and the seller’s cost. For the matched pairs, the sum Σ (bid − ask) is called the total surplus. This surplus is the sum of the buyer surplus (buyer’s value − price) and the seller surplus (price − seller’s cost). The matching above gives the largest total surplus possible.

Why sorted matching gives the largest surplus can be seen as follows. If a bid is larger than an ask, that trade creates positive value. Matching the largest bids with the smallest asks means making the positive-value trades first. In the sorted lists the bids decrease and the asks increase, so the differences shrink pair by pair; stopping at the first negative difference means making all the positive-value trades and none of the negative-value ones.

Figure 5.18 and Figure 5.19 show the matching with the supply and demand curves familiar from economics. The demand curve (bids) is a decreasing step line and the supply curve (asks) an increasing one. Where the two curves cross determines the number of trades and the price interval. At every step before the crossing, demand is above supply: the trade creates value. After the crossing, supply rises above demand: the trade destroys value.

The logic of the two ends of the price interval is important. The lower bound is 60, because if the price falls below 60 the last matched seller (cost 60) loses money and does not want to sell. Also, if the price fell below 55, the buyer left out (55) would want to buy as well and the market would not be in equilibrium. The upper bound is 70, because if the price rises above 70 the last matched buyer does not want to buy; if it rises above 75 the seller left out would want to sell as well. Any price in the interval keeps these four people in place.

5.5.3Code trace: double_auction()

The matching and the midpoint price can be written as a short function.

def double_auction(bids, asks):
  b = sorted(bids, reverse=True)  # buyers
  s = sorted(asks)                # sellers
  k, n = 0, min(len(b), len(s))
  while k < n and b[k] >= s[k]:
    k += 1                   # pair matched
  if k == 0:
    return 0, None
  lo = max([s[k-1]] + b[k:k+1])
  hi = min([b[k-1]] + s[k:k+1])
  return k, (lo + hi) / 2

The code works line by line as follows:

  • b = sorted(bids, reverse=True): the bids are sorted from largest to smallest; the most eager buyer comes first.
  • s = sorted(asks): the asks are sorted from smallest to largest; the cheapest seller comes first.
  • k is the number of matched pairs; n is the length of the shorter list. The numbers of buyers and sellers can differ; the excess of the longer list cannot be matched anyway.
  • while k < n and b[k] >= s[k]: if the next buyer meets the next seller’s ask, the pair is matched and k increases by one.
  • if k == 0: if there are no trades there is no price either; (0, None) is returned.
  • lo = max([s[k-1]] + b[k:k+1]): the larger of the last matched seller’s ask and (if there is one) the first unmatched buyer’s bid. b[k:k+1] is a slice: it gives a one-element list if k is inside the list and an empty list if outside. The code therefore does not fail when all buyers are matched.
  • hi = min([b[k-1]] + s[k:k+1]): the smaller of the last matched buyer’s bid and (if there is one) the first unmatched seller’s ask.
  • The function returns the number of trades and the midpoint of the interval.

5.5.4Incentives in a double auction

In a one-sided auction the Vickrey rule was both efficient and strategy-proof. Is it possible to obtain the same in a two-sided market? The four properties we want are:

  • Efficiency: all value-creating trades are made.
  • Incentive compatibility (IC): for everyone, placing an honest order is best.
  • Individual rationality (IR): no one loses by taking part.
  • Budget balance: the market maker puts in no money from outside; what the buyers pay goes to the sellers.
Theorem 5.3 (Myerson–Satterthwaite, 1983)

In bilateral trade where the buyer’s value and the seller’s cost are independent and come from overlapping ranges, efficiency, (Bayesian) incentive compatibility, individual rationality, and budget balance cannot all hold together.

This is an impossibility result: at least one of the four properties must be given up. Practical rules differ in which one they give up.

The midpoint rule is efficient and budget balanced but not incentive compatible. The last matched buyer or seller can shift the price in their own favor by changing their order. In the example the last matched seller’s cost is 60, and with an honest order the seller sells at 65 and gets a surplus of 5. What if the seller asks 64 instead of 60? Since 70 ≥ 64 the trade still takes place, but the interval is [max(64, 55), min(70, 75)] = [64, 70] and the price becomes 67. The seller’s surplus is 67 − 60 = 7 > 5: lying pays off. The last matched buyer can do the same by lowering the bid. Exaggeration has a limit: if the seller asks above 70, the trade is lost. This is the same as the shading trade-off under first price.

The McAfee double auction sometimes gives up one trade to secure incentive compatibility (McAfee, 1992). The rule is as follows. The matching is done as above and k efficient trades are found. The average p0 of the orders of the first unmatched buyer and the first unmatched seller is computed.

  • If p0 falls between the last matched seller’s ask and the last matched buyer’s bid, all k trades take place at p0.
  • If it does not, the last pair (the one bringing the least surplus) is removed. In the remaining k − 1 pairs the buyers pay the removed buyer’s bid and the sellers receive the removed seller’s ask.

The intuition for why the rule is incentive compatible is that no one who trades can set their own price. (The full proof also examines how the changing of one’s order changes whether one trades at all; McAfee, 1992.) In the first case the price is set by the pair left out. In the second case the prices are set by the removed pair, and that pair does not trade. This is the two-sided version of the Vickrey idea that “the rival sets your payment”. Honesty is dominant; the price is that in the second case one trade is lost. In the second case the buyers pay more than the sellers receive; the difference stays with the market maker. The rule thus satisfies budget balance in the weak sense: the market maker puts in no money from outside, but sometimes has money left over.

In our example the first pair left out is 55 (buyer) and 75 (seller). p0 = (55 + 75)/2 = 65. This value lies in the interval [60, 70]; all three trades take place at 65 and no trade is lost. In this example the McAfee rule gives the same result as the midpoint rule.

5.5.5Code trace: mcafee()

The McAfee rule can be written as follows:

def mcafee(bids, asks):
  b = sorted(bids, reverse=True) + [float('-inf')]
  s = sorted(asks) + [float('inf')]
  k = 0
  while b[k] >= s[k]:
    k += 1                 # efficient trade
  if k == 0: return 0, None, None
  p0 = (b[k] + s[k]) / 2   # excluded pair
  if s[k-1] <= p0 <= b[k-1]:
    return k, p0, p0       # one price
  return k - 1, b[k-1], s[k-1]  # two prices

The code has two details:

  • Sentinels. Minus infinity (float('-inf')) is appended to the end of the bids list and infinity (float('inf')) to the end of the asks list. A buyer bidding minus infinity cannot meet any ask, not even that of a seller who asks 0; no buyer can meet a seller who asks infinity. The loop thus stops by itself at the end of the shorter list, and b[k] and s[k] are always defined. (Had the buyer sentinel been 0, it would match a seller asking 0 and the loop would run off the end of the list.) If no real pair is left out, p0 comes out infinite or undefined (nan), the price check fails, and the weakest pair is removed. This takes the place of the slice trick in double_auction().
  • Two return forms. The function returns (number of trades, buyer price, seller price). In the single-price case the two prices are the same; in the second case they differ.

In markets with many participants, the loss of one trade is a small fraction of the total surplus, so the rule is attractive in practice.

5.5.6Auction and market design

Market design is the work of building the rules that determine with whom and under what conditions participants are matched. The basic goals are efficiency, incentive compatibility, fairness, stability, and feasibility. The rules should be strategy-proof: it should not pay participants to lie in order to turn the rules to their advantage.

Auctions are market design tools that allocate scarce resources according to prices and bids. Electricity markets, emission permits, public tenders, and spectrum licenses are applications with prices. In markets without money (for example, placing students in schools or kidney exchange for organ transplants), priority and matching rules take the place of price. In both cases the designer faces the same trade-offs: revenue, efficiency, simplicity, and strategy-proofness cannot always be obtained together. The examples in the chapter show this clearly: the reserve gives up efficiency for revenue, and the McAfee rule gives up one trade for honesty.

5.5.7From theory to practice: a short history

An old institution. Auctions are very old. Herodotus describes auctions held in Babylon in the 5th century BC. In AD 193 in Rome, the Praetorian Guard put the imperial throne up for auction and sold it to Didius Julianus; Julianus was killed two months later. This is regarded as one of the most striking examples of the winner’s curse in history.

Theory. Auction theory began in 1961 with Vickrey’s paper. Vickrey compared the four formats and found the first form of revenue equivalence (Vickrey, 1961). Myerson defined the revenue-maximizing auction (Myerson, 1981). Both received the Nobel Prize in Economics: Vickrey in 1996, Myerson in 2007.

Spectrum auctions. In 1994 the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) held the first spectrum auction using the simultaneous multiple-round auction proposed by Paul Milgrom, Robert Wilson, and Preston McAfee. In this design many licenses are sold at the same time and in rounds; in each round bidders update their bids by looking at the prices of the other licenses. Milgrom and Wilson received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2020.

Matching markets. Alvin Roth and Lloyd Shapley received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2012 for their work on stable matching and swap rules in markets where price is not used.

A designer’s tools are not limited to the allocation and payment rules. Information disclosure is also a tool: which bids are shown, and when? Participation conditions (who may bid, whether a deposit is required) are likewise tools. Real designs are tested with laboratory experiments and field data before being put into practice.

5.6Review

In practice the chapter’s results are weighed by two questions: why is the Vickrey auction, the most elegant result of the theory, rarely used, and how fair is each auction? A summary of the four formats and practice problems complete this discussion.

5.6.1Why are Vickrey auctions rarely used?

The theoretical properties of the Vickrey auction are very attractive: truthful bidding is dominant, the item goes to the highest-value bidder, and revenue is the same as under the standard rules. Even so, the pure second-price sealed-bid auction is rarely seen in practice. Rothkopf, Teisberg, and Kahn (1990) and Ausubel and Milgrom (2006) discuss the reasons.

  • Trust. Participants must trust that the second-highest bid is reported truthfully. Only the auctioneer sees the sealed envelopes; the seller can add a fake “second bid” and pull the price down to just under the winner’s bid.
  • Intuitiveness. The rule “pay the second price” does not feel natural to most people. In the lab, overbidding is common (5.3.3).
  • Model assumptions. With common or interdependent values, the result that the truthful bid is dominant can lose its validity.
  • Many items. Budget constraints and complementary goods complicate the payment rule. In VCG, the multi-item generalization, revenue can be very low and bidders can obtain illegitimate gains with false identities.
  • The seller’s concern. The difference between the winner’s bid and the price paid can be large. A winner who bids 1 million and pays 100 thousand may be perceived in public as a “sale at a low price”. There is also the risk of collusive bidding (secret agreement) among bidders.

For these reasons practical designs often strike a balance between the incentive for honesty and ease of implementation. The English auction gives the same result with private values in a transparent way: the price forms in plain view and no one has to trust an undisclosed bid. The English auction therefore works in practice as the “visible” version of the Vickrey auction.

5.6.2Discussion: which auction, and how fair?

From a participant’s point of view the formats offer different experiences. The English auction is transparent; seeing the price step by step is an advantage. In the Vickrey auction the truthful bid is best and no strategizing is needed. The Dutch and first-price auctions are fast, but the amount of shading must be guessed. Which format is preferred depends on personal strategy and appetite for risk: a risk-averse person shades less under first price, as seen in 5.4.3, and raises the probability of winning.

The question of fairness is more complex. On the one hand, setting the rules in advance provides predictability and can reduce manipulation; everyone competes under the same rules. On the other hand, players with large budgets have an advantage: an auction measures ability to pay, not need. Access to participation and inequality of information also matter: a bidder with better information avoids the curse in a common-value auction while less informed rivals lose. The seller’s revenue and equality of opportunity are different goals. As the reserve price example showed, what is good for one goal may be bad for another. No rule guarantees “full fairness for everyone”.

5.7Summary

  • An auction is a combination of an allocation rule (who gets it?) and a payment rule (what do they pay?). A bidder’s value is private information and the bid is a strategic decision; utility is v − p on winning and 0 on losing. The basic model is independent private values (IPV); the common-value model leads to the winner’s curse.
  • The four basic formats: English (the price rises; the last one remaining pays the price at which the last rival dropped out), Dutch (the price falls; the first to accept pays that price), first-price sealed-bid (pays their own bid), second-price/Vickrey (pays the second-highest bid). With private values English ≈ second price; in every case Dutch ≡ first price.
  • Vickrey’s theorem: in a second-price auction, the truthful bid is a weakly dominant strategy. The bid determines only whether you win, and the rivals determine the payment; for every B the truthful bid gives max(v − B, 0).
  • Bid shading: under first price, the trade-off E[u(b)] = (v − b) · P(win) gives the bid b = (n − 1)v/n in the symmetric Bayes–Nash equilibrium; v/2 with two bidders. The shading is v/n and shrinks as competition grows.
  • Revenue equivalence: under IPV, risk-neutral symmetric bidders, and the same allocation rule, standard auctions have the same expected revenue: (n − 1)/(n + 1), 1/3 with two bidders. The reason is Myerson’s payment formula: p(v) = v x(v) − ∫0v x(z) dz. The all-pay auction (b = v2/2) brings the same revenue as well.
  • Risk aversion breaks the equality: under first price the bid rises to v/(1 + ρ) and the revenue rises to 4/9 at ρ = ½; second price stays at 1/3.
  • Myerson’s reserve price: expected revenue is the expectation of the winner’s virtual value; for U[0, 1], ψ(v) = 2v − 1 and the best reserve is r = 1/2 (independent of n). With two bidders the revenue is 1/3 + r2 − 4r3/3, 5/12 at the best reserve. The cost: with probability 25% there is no sale, and social welfare falls from 2/3 to 7/12.
  • The winner’s curse: with common values the winner is the one with the most optimistic estimate; if errors are U[−a, a], the expected overestimate is a(n − 1)/(n + 1). The more rivals, the more one must shade.
  • The double auction: bids in decreasing order and asks in increasing order are matched; the price is chosen from the interval [lo, hi] and the total surplus is the largest. By Myerson–Satterthwaite, with values and costs from independent, overlapping ranges, efficiency, IC, IR, and budget balance cannot all hold together; the midpoint rule is not IC, and the McAfee rule makes honesty dominant at the cost of one trade.

5.9Test Yourself

  1. Five bidders have limits 35, 62, 78, 81, and 50; everyone bids their limit (in the Dutch auction the price falls continuously). Under the four rules, who wins, what do they pay, and what is the utility?

    Answer

    The winner under every rule is the bidder with limit 81. English: the last rival drops out at 78, payment ≈ 78, utility ≈ 3. Second price: the payment is the second-highest bid, 78; utility 3. Dutch: accepts when the price falls to 81, payment 81, utility 0. First price: payment 81, utility 0. English and second price give the same result, as do Dutch and first price.

  2. In a Vickrey auction a bidder’s value is v = 40. The rivals’ highest bid B may be 20, 35, 50, or 60. What is the utility in each case for the truthful bid, for b = 30, and for b = 55?

    Answer

    Truthful (40): B = 20 → wins, pays 20, utility 20; 35 → utility 5; 50 and 60 → loses, 0. b = 30: 20 → 20 (same); 35 → loses, 0 (5 worse than truthful); 50, 60 → 0 (same). b = 55: 20 → 20; 35 → 5 (same); 50 → wins, pays 50, utility −10 (worse than truthful); 60 → 0 (same). No deviation is better than the truthful bid at any B; the cases where it is worse are those where B falls between b and v.

  3. In a first-price auction there are three bidders; two rivals bid 2/3 of their values and values are U[0, 1]. The third bidder’s value is 0.9. What is the expected utility of the bids b = 0.3; 0.45; 0.6; 0.75? Which is best?

    Answer

    The probability of beating one rival is min(1, b/(2/3)) = min(1, 1.5b); for two rivals it is squared. b = 0.3: P = 0.452 = 0.2025, E[u] = 0.6 · 0.2025 ≈ 0.122. b = 0.45: P = 0.6752 ≈ 0.456, E[u] ≈ 0.45 · 0.456 ≈ 0.205. b = 0.6: P = 0.92 = 0.81, E[u] = 0.3 · 0.81 = 0.243. b = 0.75: P = 1, E[u] = 0.15. The best is b = 0.6 = (3 − 1)/3 · 0.9, the same as the equilibrium bid.

  4. Three bidders, values U[0, 1]. By Myerson’s payment formula, what is the expected payment of the bidder with value v? In the all-pay auction, what is the equilibrium bid of the bidder with value 0.9?

    Answer

    To win, both rivals’ values must be below v: x(v) = v2. p(v) = v · v2 − ∫0v z2 dz = v3 − v3/3 = 2v3/3. Check: under first price the bid is 2v/3, the winning probability v2, and the product 2v3/3. In all-pay the bid is paid in every case, so the bid equals the expected payment: b(0.9) = 2 · 0.729/3 = 0.486.

  5. Two bidders, values U[0, 1], utility u(x) = xρ with ρ = 1/3. What are the equilibrium bid under first price and the seller’s expected revenue? How large is the difference from second price?

    Answer

    b(v) = v/(1 + ρ) = v/(4/3) = 3v/4. The winner is the bidder with the larger value; the revenue is 3/4 · E[max] = 3/4 · 2/3 = 1/2. Under second price the truthful bid remains dominant and the revenue is 1/3. First price earns 1/2 − 1/3 = 1/6 more; the difference grows as risk aversion increases (as ρ gets smaller).

  6. Two bidders, U[0, 1], a second-price auction with reserve r = 0.3. What are the expected revenue, the probability of no sale, and the expected loss of social welfare? Would the best reserve change with five bidders?

    Answer

    The revenue is 1/3 + 0.09 − 4 · 0.027/3 = 1/3 + 0.09 − 0.036 ≈ 0.387. The probability of no sale is 0.32 = 0.09. The welfare loss is 2 · 0.027/3 = 0.018. The best reserve comes from ψ(r) = 2r − 1 = 0 and does not involve the number of bidders; with five bidders too it is r = 1/2.

  7. In a common-value auction the estimation errors are U[−15, 15]. If three firms bid their estimates, what is the winner’s expected profit under first price and under second price? What is the answer for first price with six firms?

    Answer

    The expectation of the largest error is 15(n − 1)/(n + 1); for n = 3 it is 15 · 2/4 = 7.5. Under first price the winner pays their own estimate: expected profit ≈ −7.5. Under second price the second-largest estimate is paid; the expectation of the second-largest error is 15(n − 3)/(n + 1) = 0: expected profit 0. With six firms the first-price expected profit is −15 · 5/7 ≈ −10.7; the curse grows as rivals increase.

  8. Buyers 75, 62, 58, 30; sellers 20, 41, 55, 70. How many trades and which prices do the midpoint rule and the McAfee rule give? How much surplus is lost under the McAfee rule?

    Answer

    The sorted lists are already decreasing/increasing: 75 ≥ 20, 62 ≥ 41, 58 ≥ 55 are matched; we stop at 30 < 70, k = 3. Midpoint: the interval is [max(55, 30), min(58, 70)] = [55, 58], price 56.5; three trades. McAfee: p0 = (30 + 70)/2 = 50; 50 is not in [55, 58]. The last pair (58, 55) is removed: 2 trades, buyers pay 58, sellers receive 55; 2 · 3 = 6 stays with the market maker. The lost surplus is that of the third trade, 58 − 55 = 3.
