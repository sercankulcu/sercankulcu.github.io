Chapter 12: Behavioral and Collective Decisions

The players of classical game theory calculate flawlessly and care only about their own payoff. The people observed in the laboratory and in everyday life depart from this picture in systematic ways: they feel losses more heavily than gains, make small mistakes, and care about fairness and intentions. When they decide in groups they also deplete common resources, contribute too little to public goods, imitate one another and lose information, or, conversely, pool independent guesses and end up more accurate than any single member. This chapter works through, with numerical examples, the models that measure these deviations (prospect theory, quantal response, inequity aversion) and the models that explain group decisions (the commons, public goods and punishment, threshold projects, information cascades, the jury theorem).

12.1Behavioral decision making

In the classical model of game theory every player maximizes expected utility, makes no calculation errors, and cares only about their own payoff. These assumptions keep models simple and often yield surprisingly good predictions. Experiments with people, however, show that in some situations the predictions plainly fail. Behavioral game theory does not dismiss these deviations with “people are irrational”; it measures the direction and size of the deviations and builds them into the model. This section first treats individual decisions: choice under risk, the reference point, framing, and mistaken but sensible choice.

12.1.1Starting with a game: the Public Goods Game

The game that ties together all the topics of this chapter is the public goods game. The rules are short. Four players receive 10 tokens each round. Each contributes as many as they like to a common pool. The tokens in the pool are multiplied by 1.6 and divided equally among the four players. Tokens not contributed stay in the player’s pocket.

Let ki be the number of tokens that player i contributes to the pool. The payoff in one round is written as:

ui = 10 − ki + 1.6 · (k1 + k2 + k3 + k4) / 4 = 10 − ki + 0.4 · Σk

The 0.4 in the formula is the multiplier (1.6) divided by the number of players (4). Figure 12.1 follows a single token. When a player puts 1 token into the pool, it becomes 1.6 and is divided by four; each player receives 0.4. The contributor gave up 1 token and got 0.4 back: a net of −0.6. Each of the other three players, without giving anything, gains 0.4; together they gain +1.2.

This small calculation carries the central tension of the chapter. From the group’s point of view every token contributed is valuable: 1 token given returns 1.6 to the group. From the individual’s point of view every token contributed is a loss: 1 token given returns only 0.4 to the contributor. If everyone contributes 10, the pool is 40, grows to 64, and everyone receives 16. If nobody contributes, everyone keeps 10. The group optimum is full contribution, but the individual optimum (whatever the others do) is to contribute nothing. The same tension arises whenever an action serves the group’s common interest but is costly to the individual: working on a joint project, keeping a park clean, contributing to open-source software.

Before starting the game one can ask two questions. First: in a group of four, how many tokens are contributed on average in the first round, and how many after ten rounds? Second: how do contributions change if anyone contributing fewer than 3 tokens is fined 2 tokens (the game’s “Contribution with punishment” scenario)? The answer given by theory and the behavior observed in experiments diverge markedly. Both answers are worked out in 12.4. The sections in between build the models that explain the gap.

12.1.2Bounded rationality

In classical decision theory the decision maker knows all the options, computes the consequences and probabilities of each, and picks the one with the highest expected utility. In 1955 Herbert Simon argued that this picture does not fit real people. Our information is incomplete, our time is limited, and the number of options we can hold in mind at once is small.

Definition 12.1

Bounded rationality (Simon, 1955): because the decision maker’s information, time, and computational capacity are limited, they accept a “good enough” option that meets a given aspiration level instead of searching for the best of all options. This way of searching is called satisficing.

Consider a student looking for a summer internship. A fully rational decision maker would list all the companies, score each on salary, learning opportunities, and commute time, and pick the highest total. A real student applies to a few companies and accepts the first offer that meets the conditions “in Istanbul, at least minimum wage, a software job.” This behavior lowers the cost of computation; the price is possibly missing a better offer.

The most important finding of behavioral research is this: people’s deviations from the rational model are systematic, not random. The same errors are observed again and again, in different people and in different countries. Random errors cancel out on average and do not disturb models. Systematic errors, by contrast, always shift in the same direction; for exactly this reason they can be measured and added to a model with a formula. All the models in this chapter rest on this idea.

The causes of the deviations can be grouped under four headings: lack of information, time pressure, emotions, and shortcuts. The shortcuts are called heuristics: fast but sometimes misleading rules such as “if everyone is buying this product, it must be good” or “if it worked last time, it will work again.” Four common systematic deviations are summarized in Table 12.1.

Table 12.1: Four systematic deviations and everyday examples.
DeviationDefinitionExample
OverconfidenceTrusting one’s own estimates more than warrantedInvestors trading too much
Framing effectThe decision changes when the same option is presented differently“90% success” and “10% failure” are perceived differently
Loss aversionA loss feels stronger than a gain of equal sizePanic selling
Base rate neglectIgnoring the prior probability when interpreting new evidenceForgetting how common the disease is after a positive test

Overconfidence is people’s exaggeration of the accuracy of their own knowledge. Individual investors who trade often on the stock market earn less on average because of transaction costs, yet each believes their own forecast beats the market.

Base rate neglect is best understood with numbers. Suppose a disease affects 1% of the population. A test comes out positive for 90% of patients and, mistakenly, for 9% of healthy people. In a group of 1000 people there are 10 patients, and 9 of them test positive. About 89 of the 990 healthy people also test positive. Of the 98 people who test positive, only 9 are actually ill; that is, the probability of being ill after a positive result is about 9%. Most people think “the test is 90% accurate” and estimate this probability close to 90%: they ignore how common the disease is, that is, the prior probability. As this shows, the link Bayes’ rule draws between prior and posterior probability can run quite against intuition. The same Bayesian calculation is used again for information cascades (12.6.2).

Framing and loss aversion, in turn, are explained by a separate model: prospect theory.

12.1.3Prospect theory and the value function

In classical expected utility theory a person evaluates outcomes in terms of their total wealth. For someone whose wealth is 100,000 TL, winning 1,000 TL means wealth rising from 100,000 to 101,000. In 1979 Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that this does not match how people actually evaluate. People perceive outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point. The reference point is often the status quo, but it can also be an expectation or a goal.

Definition 12.2

Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) evaluates an option under risk as follows: each outcome x is written as a gain (x ≥ 0) or a loss (x < 0) relative to the reference point, valued by a value function v(x), weighted by probabilities, and summed. The value function used in these notes is:

v(x) = x0.88 (x ≥ 0),    v(x) = −2.25 · (−x)0.88 (x < 0)

The number λ = 2.25 here is called the loss aversion coefficient. The exponent 0.88 and λ = 2.25 are the values Tversky and Kahneman (1992) estimated from experiments.

The value function has three properties, and each explains a behavior. Figure 12.2 shows them on a single curve.

1. Concave for gains. The function x0.88 grows more slowly than x. Because the exponent is less than 1, the rate of increase falls as the number grows. In numbers: v(100) = 1000.88 ≈ 57.5 and v(200) = 2000.88 ≈ 105.9. When the amount doubles, the value does not double; it rises by only a factor of 20.88 ≈ 1.84. The curve flattens as it goes right: the joy of the first 100 TL is greater than that of the second 100 TL. The consequence is risk aversion in the gain region: a sure 100 TL is valued more than a lottery paying 200 TL or 0 TL with equal probability, because 57.5 > ½ · 105.9 ≈ 53.0.

2. Convex for losses. On the loss side the same diminishing sensitivity applies: the first 100 TL of loss hurts more than the second 100 TL of loss. But this time the result goes the other way. A sure loss of 100 TL has value v(−100) ≈ −129.5; a gamble with a 200 TL loss or a 0 TL loss with equal probability has value ½ · v(−200) ≈ ½ · (−238.3) ≈ −119.1. The risky option feels less bad: in the loss region people are risk seeking. This is the source of the behavior of “gambling rather than making the loss certain”; refusing to sell a losing stock because “it may come back” is one example.

3. Steeper for losses. A loss of the same size weighs λ = 2.25 times more than the gain: v(−100) = −2.25 · 57.5 ≈ −129.5. The curve has a kink at the reference point; approaching from the right the slope is gentle, from the left it is steep. This kink explains why even fair bets with zero expected value are rejected (12.1.4).

The effect of the reference point can be seen in a single example. An employee expecting a year-end bonus of 10,000 TL who receives 9,000 TL experiences it as a loss of 1,000 TL. For someone who expected no bonus at all, the same 9,000 TL is a large gain. The wealth of both has risen by the same amount; by classical theory both should be equally pleased. By prospect theory, satisfaction depends on where the outcome stands relative to the reference point.

Example 12.1

Let us compute the value function at a few points. On a calculator, x0.88 is x raised to the power 0.88 (in Python, x ** 0.88).

  1. v(10) = 100.88 ≈ 7.6. A gain of ten liras gives about 7.6 units of value.
  2. v(100) = 1000.88 ≈ 57.5. The amount grew tenfold; the value grew about 7.6-fold.
  3. v(1000) = 10000.88 ≈ 436.5. Again a tenfold amount, again about a 7.6-fold value: for a power function the ratio is constant (100.88 ≈ 7.6).
  4. v(−100) = −2.25 · 57.5 ≈ −129.5. A loss of the same size is 2.25 times heavier.

In this function, whenever the amount is multiplied by 10 the value grows by the same factor (about 7.6) at every scale. The ratio between loss and gain is likewise 2.25 at every scale.

12.1.4Loss aversion: the coin-toss gamble

The cleanest test of loss aversion is a fair bet. A coin is tossed; heads wins 100 TL, tails loses 100 TL. The expected value is ½ · 100 + ½ · (−100) = 0. For a risk-neutral decision maker, taking the bet and declining it are the same. In experiments, however, the vast majority of people reject this bet. Prospect theory explains it as follows.

Example 12.2
  1. Outcomes relative to the reference. Not betting is the reference point (0). The bet gives +100 with probability ½ and −100 with probability ½.
  2. Values. v(100) ≈ 57.5, v(−100) ≈ −129.5.
  3. Prospect value. ½ · 57.5 + ½ · (−129.5) = 28.75 − 64.75 = −36.0.
  4. Comparison. The value of not betting is v(0) = 0. Since −36.0 < 0, the bet is rejected.

A bet with zero expected value is rejected because a loss weighs more than a gain.

How large must the gain be for the bet to be accepted? Let the gain be G and the loss again 100 TL. For the bet to be accepted, its prospect value must exceed zero. The boundary is the point where the two terms are equal.

Example 12.3
  1. Equation. ½ · v(G) + ½ · v(−100) = 0, that is, ½ · G0.88 = ½ · 2.25 · 1000.88.
  2. Simplification. The ½’s cancel; divide both sides by 1000.88: G0.88 / 1000.88 = (G/100)0.88 = 2.25.
  3. Removing the exponent. Raise both sides to the power 1/0.88: G/100 = 2.251/0.88 ≈ 2.51.
  4. Result. G ≈ 100 · 2.51 ≈ 251 TL.

To accept the risk of losing 100 TL, one demands a chance of winning about 251 TL in return. This ratio is independent of the amount: to accept a loss of 200 TL, about 503 TL is needed. Experiments likewise find that people typically demand more than twice the loss.

The operation in the third step is inverting the power equation. If ya = b then y = b1/a, because (b1/a)a = b. Here a = 0.88, 1/a ≈ 1.136, and 2.251.136 ≈ 2.51. If the value function were linear (exponent 1), the threshold would be exactly λ · 100 = 225 TL; because concavity makes large gains slightly less valuable, the threshold rises to 251.

12.1.5Probability weighting

The second component of prospect theory is how probabilities are perceived. In classical theory a probability of 1% receives exactly weight 0.01. Experiments show that people feel small probabilities as larger than they are and large probabilities as smaller than they are. This perceived weight is called probability weighting and is written w(p).

Table 12.2: Probability weighting (Tversky and Kahneman, 1992 estimate).
Probability p0.010.500.99
Perceived weight w(p)0.0550.4210.912

According to Table 12.2, a probability of 1% feels like about 5.5%: it is overstated more than fivefold. 50% is perceived as about 42% and 99% as about 91%: medium and large probabilities are underestimated. Certainty (p = 1), however, receives full weight; this is why moving from 99% to 100% is valued far more than moving from 50% to 51%.

These weights explain why two seemingly opposite behaviors are seen in the same person. Someone who buys a lottery ticket overstates a very small chance of winning: a 0.01% chance feels bigger to them. The same person, when insuring a home, also overstates a small probability of disaster and is willing to pay a premium above the expected damage. A model based on expected value cannot explain both behaviors together: according to it, a person either seeks or avoids risk. With probability weighting both come from the same source: the overstatement of small probabilities.

Common mistake

“w(p) is a probability.” No, it is a decision weight. Probabilities sum to 1; weights do not: w(0.01) + w(0.99) = 0.055 + 0.912 = 0.967 ≠ 1. Also w(0.5) = 0.421, not 0.5. Weights are not manipulated like probabilities; they only measure how the decision feels.

12.1.6Code trace: prospect()

Let us trace with a short Python program how the value of a gamble changes as the gain amount changes. The program first defines the value function v(x), then the prospect value of a gamble that gives +G or −K with equal probability, and tries four different gain amounts.

def v(x):
    if x >= 0: return x ** 0.88
    return -2.25 * (-x) ** 0.88
def prospect(G, K):      # ½: +G, ½: −K
    return 0.5 * v(G) + 0.5 * v(-K)
for G in [100, 200, 251, 300]:
    print(G, round(prospect(G, 100), 1))

The code runs line by line as follows:

  • v(x) is the value function of Definition 12.2. If x >= 0 the gain branch x ** 0.88 runs; otherwise the loss branch -2.25 * (-x) ** 0.88 runs. In the loss branch -x is a positive number; the power is taken and then multiplied by −2.25.
  • prospect(G, K) is the value of the gamble that gives +G with probability ½ and −K with probability ½. Probability weighting is not used here; ½ is taken directly as the weight.
  • The loop tries G = 100, 200, 251, and 300; the loss is always 100. For each, the value is rounded to one decimal and printed.

The program prints four lines: 100 -36.0, 200 -11.8, 251 -0.1, 300 10.9. Table 12.3 shows how each line comes about. At G = 100 the bet is fair, yet its prospect value is −36.0. At G = 200 the expected value is +50, so a risk-neutral person would take the bet at once; the prospect value is still −11.8 and the bet is rejected. At G = 251 the value is almost zero (−0.1): this is exactly the threshold of Example 12.3. At G = 300 the value is +10.9 and the bet is now accepted.

Figure 12.3 shows the two measures side by side. The blue bars (expected value) grow steadily from left to right. The orange bars (prospect value) are below zero for the first three bets. The +200 / −100 bet is rejected even though its expected value is +50. Loss aversion is largely responsible for this gap: with λ = 2.25 a loss weighs so heavily that the bet is not accepted until the gain exceeds 251 TL.

12.1.7The endowment effect and status quo bias

Loss aversion shows up not only in gambles but also in the value people place on things they own. By classical theory the value of a mug does not depend on who holds it. Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler (1990) tested this prediction with a simple experiment. Half the students in a class were handed a mug at random. The mug owners were asked the least they would accept to sell it; those without a mug were asked the most they would pay to buy one.

The result was clear: the lowest selling price the owners demanded was about twice the highest price the buyers were willing to pay. Because the mugs were handed out at random, the tastes of the two groups are the same on average. By classical theory about half the mugs should have changed hands; in reality far fewer trades occurred than expected.

Definition 12.3

Endowment effect: owning an object raises the value placed on it. The lowest selling price an owner will accept is systematically higher than the highest price a buyer is willing to pay for the same object.

Prospect theory explains this effect through the reference point. The reference point of a mug owner is the state “I have a mug”; giving the mug up is a loss. The reference point of someone without a mug is “I have no mug”; getting the mug is a gain. Because a loss weighs λ times more, the seller asks for more than the buyer will pay.

Example 12.4

Let the mug’s use value be 20 TL. To keep the calculation simple, take the value function to be linear (exponent 1) but keep the loss aversion coefficient λ = 2.25.

  1. Owner. Giving up the mug is a loss of 20 TL from the reference point and feels like −2.25 · 20 = −45. To make up for this loss the owner asks for at least 45 TL.
  2. Buyer. Getting the mug is a gain of 20 TL: +20. The buyer pays at most 20 TL.
  3. Ratio. 45 / 20 = 2.25 = λ. The ratio of selling price to buying price is exactly the loss aversion coefficient.
  4. Result. At no price between 20 and 45 TL can the two sides agree: the buyer will not pay more than 20 and the seller will not accept less than 45. No trade takes place.

The ratio measured in the experiment (about 2) is close to what this model predicts. An important consequence of the endowment effect is that resources do not flow automatically to those who value them most. The Coase theorem in economics says that if there are no transaction costs, who initially receives a resource does not affect efficiency. The endowment effect weakens this conclusion: the initial allocation determines how much trade takes place.

Its everyday counterpart is status quo bias. Departing from the default option is also perceived as a loss. When a retirement plan switches from “those who want to join must apply” to “everyone is enrolled automatically, and those who want out may leave,” participation rises markedly. The options in the two rules are the same; only the reference point changes. This is also why default settings matter so much in software design: most users never change the default.

Common mistake

“The owner simply likes the mug more.” No. The object is the same and the mugs were handed out at random; on average the owners’ tastes do not differ from the buyers’. Only the reference point changes: the same mug is evaluated as a loss by the owner and as a gain by the buyer.

12.1.8The framing effect

The reference point can come from a person’s own situation, but also from how the options are presented. Tversky and Kahneman (1981) showed this with the famous “Asian disease” problem. Participants were asked to choose between two programs against an epidemic expected to kill 600 people. One group was given the options in terms of lives saved, the other in terms of lives lost.

Table 12.4: The two frames of the epidemic problem (Tversky and Kahneman, 1981).
FrameSure programRisky programMajority choice
GainA: 200 people are savedB: with probability 1/3 all 600 are saved, with probability 2/3 no one is saved72% A (sure)
LossC: 400 people dieD: with probability 1/3 no one dies, with probability 2/3 all 600 die78% D (risky)

The four programs in Table 12.4 are really two programs. In A, 200 people are saved, which means 400 die: A and C are the same outcome. In B, with probability 1/3 everyone is saved and with probability 2/3 everyone dies; D is the same: B and D are the same lottery. In all four programs the expected number saved is 200: ⅓ · 600 = 200.

Definition 12.4

Framing effect: a change of preference when options with identical outcomes are presented differently (for example, as gains or as losses).

By expected utility theory the preference should not change, because the options in the two frames are identical. What is observed is that the majority switches from the sure option (72%) to the risky option (78%). Prospect theory explains this switch through the shape of the value function. In the gain frame the reference point is “everyone dies” and those saved are gains. In the gain region the value function is concave: 200 sure survivors feel worth more than a 1/3 chance of 600 survivors. In the loss frame the reference point is “no one dies” and the deaths are losses. In the loss region the function is convex: 400 sure deaths feel worse than a 2/3 chance of 600 deaths.

Example 12.5

This time the value function is applied to the number of people (without probability weighting).

  1. Gain frame, A. v(200) = 2000.88 ≈ 105.9.
  2. Gain frame, B. ⅓ · v(600) ≈ ⅓ · 278.5 ≈ 92.8. 105.9 > 92.8: the sure program A is chosen.
  3. Loss frame, C. v(−400) = −2.25 · 4000.88 ≈ −438.5.
  4. Loss frame, D. ⅔ · v(−600) ≈ ⅔ · (−626.5) ≈ −417.7. −417.7 > −438.5: the risky program D is chosen.

The same function produces two different choices in the two frames; the behavior of the majority is consistent with the model.

The framing effect has many practical counterparts. The sentence “90% of patients survive this operation” receives more approval than “10% die.” The same price difference draws less reaction when presented as a “discount for paying cash” than as a “surcharge for paying by card.” The lesson for an engineer designing a platform is clear: the way options are presented is not neutral; it changes user behavior.

12.1.9Common mistakes in prospect theory

Because the concepts of prospect theory are close to one another, they are often confused. The four misconceptions below are the most common in exams and discussions.

Common mistake
  • “Loss aversion is risk aversion.” No. Risk aversion comes from the curvature of utility, loss aversion from the kink at the reference point (λ). The ±100 TL bet is rejected because of λ even if v is nearly linear for gains. Even with exponent 1 (no curvature) the prospect value is ½ · 100 − ½ · 225 = −62.5.
  • “w(p) is a probability.” No, it is a decision weight: w(0.01) + w(0.99) = 0.967 ≠ 1 and w(0.5) = 0.421.
  • “The reference point is always zero.” The reference can be an expectation, the status quo, or a goal. For someone expecting a 10,000 TL bonus, 9,000 TL is a loss.
  • “In the loss frame everyone chooses the risk.” The 78% is a majority tendency. The theory explains average behavior and does not predict individuals; 22% of participants chose the sure program in the loss frame too.

12.1.10Quantal response

The Nash equilibrium assumes that every player always plays a best response. A best response is the strategy that maximizes the payoff while the others’ choices are held fixed. Real people choose the better option more often, but not always. When the difference between options is small, they often choose the wrong one; as the difference grows, errors become rarer. A student choosing between two close answers on an exam may be wrong half the time; if one of the answers is obviously absurd, the student almost never picks it.

The most common way to model this behavior is logit choice. The idea is this: every option is given an “attraction” that increases with its payoff, and options are chosen with probability proportional to their attractions. The exponential function serves as the attraction. e ≈ 2.718 is a fixed number, and ex grows rapidly as x grows; it is always positive.

Definition 12.5

Logit choice: the player chooses each option a with a probability that depends on the option’s expected payoff u(a), namely

P(a) = eλ·u(a) / Σb eλ·u(b)

The denominator is the sum of the attractions of all options, so the probabilities add up to 1. The parameter λ ≥ 0 measures how sharp the choice is: for λ = 0 the choice is completely random, and as λ → ∞ it turns into the best response.

The λ here is unrelated to the λ = 2.25 of loss aversion; using the same letter in both models is merely a convention. In logit choice it helps to think of λ as “attention” or “sensitivity.” For λ = 0 all exponents are e0 = 1 and every option gets equal probability: the player pays no attention to payoffs at all. As λ grows, the effect of payoff differences grows; the attraction of the highest-payoff option overwhelms the others, and in the limit all the probability goes to it.

With two options the formula simplifies. Call the options “good” and “bad,” and let the payoff difference be d = u(good) − u(bad). Dividing numerator and denominator by eλ·u(good) gives:

P(good) = 1 / (1 + e−λ·d)

This is an S-shaped curve that depends only on the payoff difference. At difference 0 the probability is ½; as the difference grows it approaches 1.

In games this idea becomes an equilibrium concept. Each player’s expected payoff depends on the others’ probabilities; if the others also respond with logit choice, the probabilities are tied to one another.

Definition 12.6

Quantal response equilibrium (QRE; McKelvey and Palfrey, 1995): a probability profile in which every player responds with logit choice to the expected payoffs computed from the other players’ equilibrium probabilities. In other words, plugging the others’ probabilities into the logit formula returns each player’s own probabilities. As λ → ∞ the QRE tends to a Nash equilibrium.

QRE generalizes the Nash equilibrium: Nash is the extreme case of “zero error.” In this way QRE can capture, with a single parameter (λ), the systematic deviations observed in experiments that Nash cannot explain. The most striking example is the asymmetric Matching Pennies game (12.1.11).

This is not the only way to model erroneous behavior. A separate model, level-k reasoning, limits not the error but the depth of reasoning (Nagel, 1995). A level-0 player plays randomly. A level-1 player assumes the others are level-0 and best-responds to them. In general, a level-k player takes the others to be level k−1. In the classic “guess 2/3 of the average” game, level-0 guesses an average of 50; level-1 says ⅔ · 50 ≈ 33; level-2 says ⅔ · 33 ≈ 22. The Nash equilibrium (everyone says 0) is reached only at infinite depth; in experiments the guesses mostly cluster around 33 and 22. QRE assumes “a little error at every step,” level-k assumes “a limited number of steps”; the two models explain different experiments with different success.

Example 12.7

  3. Interpretation. The row player’s probability of Top is 1/2 and does not depend at all on the row player’s own payoff of 320: if 120 or 1000 were written instead of 320, p = 1/2 would remain. Only the column player’s probability q changes.

This result is odd at first sight. The row player has a very large prize in cell (Top, Left); intuition says Top should be played more often. By the Nash equilibrium, however, a player’s own payoffs have no effect on their own probabilities. Experiments side with intuition: in Goeree and Holt’s (2001) experiments the row players clearly shifted toward the large payoff and played Top far more often than half the time.

QRE captures this “own-payoff effect.” The row player’s expected-payoff difference between Top and Bottom is (40 + 280q) − (80 − 40q) = 320q − 40, and the column player’s difference between Left and Right is (80 − 40p) − (40 + 40p) = 40 − 80p. Common mistake

“The row player’s large payoff raises the row player’s probability of Top in the mixed Nash equilibrium.” No. In a mixed Nash equilibrium a player’s probability is determined by the opponent’s payoffs, because the aim is to make the opponent indifferent. When 320 changes, only q changes (1/8). People, however, also respond to their own payoff; this effect is absent in Nash and present in QRE.

12.2Social preferences

The deviations in the previous section concerned how a person values their own payoff. The deviations in this section concern a person caring about other people’s payoffs. In the classical model a player cares only about their own money; what the counterpart receives, how they were treated, or whether the outcome is fair does not enter the utility function. Laboratory experiments show that this assumption, too, fails systematically. Social preference models write motives such as fairness, envy, guilt, and reciprocity into the utility function; the players are then still rational, but they maximize something different.

12.2.1Dictator and ultimatum games

The simplest measures of social preferences are two small games.

Definition 12.7

Dictator game: one player (the dictator) divides 10 tokens between themselves and the counterpart; the counterpart can do nothing, and the division is carried out as stated.

Ultimatum game: the proposer offers s of the 10 tokens to the responder. If the responder accepts, the division (10 − s, s) is carried out; if the responder rejects, both get 0.

For someone who cares only about money, the solution of both games is short. In the dictator game giving yields nothing, so the dictator gives 0. In the ultimatum game the responder should accept every positive offer, since even 1 token is better than 0. Knowing this, the proposer makes the smallest positive offer (1 token). This result is the subgame perfect equilibrium found by backward induction.

Observed behavior departs markedly from this prediction in both games. In a meta-analysis combining more than a hundred dictator game studies, dictators give on average about 28% of the pie, and a sizable fraction give nothing at all (Engel, 2011). In the ultimatum game offers are typically 40–50%, and offers below 20% are frequently rejected. Figure 12.4 compares theory and observation.

Read together, the two games let us separate the motives. In the dictator game the other side has no power; the share given can come only from a sense of fairness or from altruism (caring about another’s well-being). In the ultimatum game the proposer fears rejection; the share added on top of the dictator game’s 28% is largely strategic, that is, meant to avoid rejection. The responder’s rejection of low offers means giving up their own money: a responder who rejects 2 tokens loses 2 tokens but deprives the proposer of 8. This rejection behavior cannot be explained by the model that cares only about money. Social preference models explain both giving and rejection by writing these motives into the utility function.

12.2.2Inequity aversion: the Fehr–Schmidt model

The model of Ernst Fehr and Klaus Schmidt (1999) assumes that people care not only about their own payoffs but also about the differences between payoffs. The difference is unpleasant in two directions: getting less than the counterpart arouses envy, getting more arouses guilt.

Definition 12.8

Inequity aversion (Fehr and Schmidt, 1999). In a two-person situation, if player i’s money is xi and the counterpart’s is xj, the utility is

ui = xi − α · max(xj − xi, 0) − β · max(xi − xj, 0),   0 ≤ β ≤ α, β < 1

where α measures the pain of falling behind (envy) and β the discomfort of being ahead (guilt).

The max(·, 0) expressions in the formula ensure that at most one of the two penalty terms is active. If the player is behind (xj > xi) only the α term is nonzero; if ahead, only the β term; at equality both are zero. Each of the constraints on the parameters has a meaning:

  • β ≤ α: people are more bothered by being behind than by being ahead.
  • β < 1: someone who is ahead does not burn their own money merely to reduce inequality. Burning 1 token reduces the gap by 1 and gains at most β < 1 in utility; the loss of the burned token is larger.
  • α = β = 0: this gives the classical player who cares only about money. The model contains the classical model as a special case.

The model’s prediction differs across the three games; let us solve each separately.

Example 12.8

Ultimatum game, responder. Let the offer be s < 5; the responder gets s, the proposer gets 10 − s, and the responder is behind.

  1. Utility of accepting. The difference is (10 − s) − s = 10 − 2s. u = s − α(10 − 2s).
  2. Utility of rejecting. Both get 0, there is no difference: u = 0.
  3. Acceptance condition. s − α(10 − 2s) ≥ 0 ⇔ s + 2αs ≥ 10α ⇔ s ≥ 10α / (1 + 2α).
  4. Numerical values. For α = 1, s ≥ 10/3 ≈ 3.33; for α = 2, s ≥ 20/5 = 4; for α = 0, s ≥ 0 (every offer is accepted).

As α grows the threshold rises, but it never exceeds 5: 10α/(1 + 2α) < 5 holds for every α. An equal split is acceptable to every responder.

Example 12.9

Dictator. If the dictator gives s ≤ 5, the dictator stays ahead (or equal); only the β term is active.

  1. Utility. The dictator’s money is 10 − s, the difference is 10 − 2s: u = (10 − s) − β(10 − 2s).
  2. Rearranging. u = 10 − 10β − s + 2βs = 10 − 10β + s(2β − 1).
  3. Slope. u is a linear function of s with slope 2β − 1. If β > 1/2 the slope is positive; the dictator gives as much as possible, that is, 5 (splits equally). If β < 1/2 the slope is negative; the dictator gives nothing. If β = 1/2 the slope is 0 and every share from 0 to 5 gives the same utility.
  4. Giving more than 5. If s > 5 is given, the dictator falls behind and the α term comes into play. Money falls and the pain of envy begins; for this reason the dictator does not give more than 5.

For example, a dictator with β = 0.6 gives 5 and gets u = 5; one with β = 0.3 gives 0 and gets u = 10 − 3 = 7.

This result explains the two clusters in experiments: some dictators give nothing, some split equally. In the model, intermediate values (for example giving 2 or 3) arise only in the boundary case β = 1/2; the intermediate values seen in real data suggest that other motives (for example the wish to look good) are also at work.

Public goods. A player who is sufficiently inequity averse contributes if the others contribute, because by not contributing they would stay ahead of the others and the β term would bother them. If the others do not contribute, neither does this player, because contributing alone would leave them behind and the α term would bother them. This behavior is called conditional cooperation, and such a player is a conditional cooperator. The dynamics of laboratory public goods experiments (12.4) come largely from the behavior of players of this type.

Common mistake

“If the threshold is 3.33, then 3 tokens are accepted.” No. The acceptance condition is s ≥ 3.33; 3 does not satisfy it (u = −1). When the threshold is not a whole number, the smallest accepted offer is the threshold rounded up. Moreover the threshold formula holds only for s < 5; for offers s > 5 the responder is ahead and the β term is active.

12.2.4Reciprocity and the trust game

Inequity aversion looks only at the distribution of outcomes: who got how much? People, however, often react not to the outcome but to the intention that led to it. The classic experiment measuring this is the trust game.

Definition 12.9

Trust game (Berg, Dickhaut, and McCabe, 1995): the sender sends the receiver any amount x of 10 dollars. The amount sent is tripled; the receiver gets 3x and sends back any amount y of it to the sender. The sender earns 10 − x + y and the receiver 3x − y.

The design of the game is chosen with care. Because every dollar sent is tripled, with full trust and full reciprocity the two sides together earn the most: 10 dollars become 30. But the receiver has no obligation at all to send anything back. The experiment was played one-shot and under full anonymity: the participants did not know one another, would never meet again, and even the experimenters did not know who did what individually. Strategic reasons such as reputation or future reward were thus eliminated.

For players who care only about money the solution is found by backward induction. The last move is the receiver’s; sending back only reduces their own payoff, so y = 0. The sender, foreseeing this, knows that anything sent will be lost; so x = 0. The theory’s prediction: nothing is sent, and both sides stay where they started.

In the experiment, however, senders sent 5.16 dollars on average and receivers sent back 4.66 dollars on average (Figure 12.6). The great majority of senders sent something; some receivers sent back more than was sent, some sent nothing. The average sender started with 10 dollars and ended with 10 − 5.16 + 4.66 = 9.50 dollars; the average receiver kept 15.48 − 4.66 = 10.82 dollars.

Definition 12.10

Reciprocity (Rabin, 1993): a person’s responding to the counterpart’s intention, answering kind behavior with kindness and unkind behavior with punishment.

The difference between reciprocity and inequity aversion can be seen in a thought experiment. Suppose a responder in the ultimatum game receives an offer of 2 tokens. In the first case the other person chose the offer themselves. In the second case a lottery that neither side could influence determined the offer. Inequity aversion cannot tell the two cases apart, because in both the outcome is (8, 2) and the utility is the same. Reciprocity can: in the first case the other person has a selfish intention and rejection punishes that intention; in the second there is no intention to punish and acceptance is more likely. The receiver’s sending something back in the trust game is also explained by reciprocity: the sender has shown trust, and the receiver responds to this good intention.

Common mistake

“Trusting paid off.” Not on average. What came back (4.66 dollars) was less than what was sent (5.16 dollars); the average sender lost 0.50 dollars. Even so, most people sent. The surprising result of the experiment is not that trust paid but that it was so widespread although it did not pay. In terms of the total, trust did create value: because each dollar sent was tripled, the two sides’ combined earnings rose from 10 dollars to an average of 10 + 2 · 5.16 ≈ 20.3 dollars.

12.3Collective decisions and common resources

So far the decisions of one or two people have been examined. From here on the focus is the outcome a group produces together. The basic problem with group outcomes is that each individual, when making their own decision, does not account for its effect on the other members. This section models the problem in its most classic form, the overuse of a common resource; it then treats remedies and the welfare criteria used to evaluate outcomes.

12.3.1Individual and collective interest

A decision made by a group is called a collective decision. In group decisions social influence, pressure to conform, and common interests play a role. Communities make decisions by majority vote, by consensus (a common decision that everyone agrees to), or by more complex voting systems. But a group’s outcome is not always determined by an explicit vote. Often the outcome is the sum of small decisions each individual makes alone: nobody votes for “let there be a traffic jam,” but everyone choosing their own route produces one.

Individual interest maximizes a person’s own utility, collective interest the group’s total utility. The two often conflict. Three examples show different forms of this conflict:

  • Traffic. Every driver chooses the shortest route for themselves; the total latency is greater than central planning would give. This is the basic example in the topic of selfish routing and the price of anarchy: in the two-link Pigou network the selfish equilibrium raises the total latency by a factor of 4/3 over the optimum.
  • Work groups. Everyone focuses on their own task; a joint project that nobody owns alone stalls. This is a public goods problem (12.4).
  • Common resources. Fisheries, pastures, groundwater, the atmosphere: resources that everyone can use but that shrink as they are used are overused.

The classic name for the last example is the tragedy of the commons. In an article published in 1968, Garrett Hardin gave the example of herders letting animals graze on a pasture open to all. For each herder, adding one more animal brings the whole gain to themselves, while the cost of the pasture’s degradation is divided among all the herders. So every herder keeps adding animals and the pasture is exhausted. Hardin’s verbal argument can be turned into a numerical model with game theory.

12.3.2The tragedy of the commons: a numerical model

Four herders graze animals on a common pasture. Let the number of animals herder i grazes be gi and the total number of animals G = g1 + g2 + g3 + g4. As the pasture becomes crowded each animal gets less feed; let the value per animal be 100 − G. At 100 animals the pasture is completely exhausted and the animals become worthless. A herder’s payoff is the number of animals times the value per animal:

ui = gi · (100 − G)

This model closely resembles the Cournot model: there firms choose a quantity and the price falls with total output; here herders choose a number of animals and the value per animal falls with the total. So the solution method is the same: best responses are found with a derivative. First the group optimum is computed, then the individual optimum.

Example 12.10

Social optimum. The group’s total payoff is Σui = G · (100 − G); which herder has how many animals does not change the total.

  1. Expansion. G(100 − G) = 100G − G2.
  2. Derivative. The derivative of 100G is 100 and that of G2 is 2G: the derivative is 100 − 2G.
  3. Set to zero. 100 − 2G = 0 ⇒ G = 50. The second derivative is −2, so this is a peak.
  4. Value. The total payoff is 50 · 50 = 2,500. With an equal split each herder grazes 12.5 animals and earns 12.5 · 50 = 625.

The optimum can be thought of as an agreement: “everyone grazes 12.5 animals.” The question is whether this agreement is self-enforcing. For that we ask whether one herder would want to deviate while the others keep the agreement.

Example 12.11

A single herder deviates. The other three herders graze 12.5 each, 37.5 animals in total. If herder 1 grazes g animals, the total is 37.5 + g.

  1. Payoff. u1 = g · (100 − 37.5 − g) = g · (62.5 − g) = 62.5g − g2.
  2. Derivative. 62.5 − 2g = 0 ⇒ g = 31.25.
  3. The deviator’s payoff. The total number of animals is 37.5 + 31.25 = 68.75, the value per animal is 31.25; the payoff is 31.25 · 31.25 ≈ 976.6. This is far more than the 625 under the agreement.
  4. The others’ payoff. Each gets 12.5 · 31.25 ≈ 390.6; it falls from 625 to 390.6.

The agreement is not self-enforcing: every herder wants to deviate. The deviator’s gain comes from the others’ loss.

If everyone acts on the same logic, the outcome is the Nash equilibrium. To find it, write herder i’s general best response. Let the others’ total number of animals be G−i:

ui = gi(100 − gi − G−i)  ⇒  derivative 100 − 2gi − G−i = 0  ⇒  gi = (100 − G−i) / 2

Example 12.12

Symmetric Nash equilibrium.

  1. Symmetry. The four herders are in the same position; in equilibrium all choose the same g. Then G−i = 3g.
  2. Equation. g = (100 − 3g)/2 ⇒ 2g = 100 − 3g ⇒ 5g = 100 ⇒ g* = 20.
  3. Total and payoff. G = 80, the value per animal is 20; the payoff per herder is 20 · 20 = 400, 1,600 in total.
  4. Check. When the other three graze 60 animals, the best response is (100 − 60)/2 = 20. Nobody wants to deviate ✓.

Figure 12.7 shows the three situations on the same curve. The top of the curve is the optimum. A single herder’s deviation pushes the total to the right of the peak; when everyone deviates the total number of animals rises to 80 and the group payoff falls to 1,600. The tragedy is this: in equilibrium the total payoff is only 64% of the optimum, and each herder gets 400 instead of the 625 they would have received had the agreement been kept. Everyone has calculated correctly from their own standpoint; the outcome is bad for everyone. This structure is a many-player, continuous-strategy form of the Prisoner’s Dilemma.

Why the equilibrium is far from the optimum is explained by the concept of an externality. An externality is an effect of one person’s decision on others that does not enter their own calculation. When a herder adds one more animal, the value per animal falls by 1, and this fall is passed on to every animal on the pasture. The herder feels only the part of the loss that falls on their own animals; in the symmetric case, being one of four herders, this share is roughly 1/4. The remaining 3/4 falls on the other herders and does not enter the herder’s calculation. Because every herder ignores part of the cost, everyone grazes too many animals.

How is the equilibrium reached? If the herders best-respond in turn, the process converges to this equilibrium. Let the pasture start empty. Herder 1, with nobody else present, grazes (100 − 0)/2 = 50; herder 2, against 50, grazes 25; herder 3, against 75, grazes 12.5; herder 4, against 87.5, grazes 6.25. In later rounds herder 1, against the others’ 43.75, reduces the herd while the other herders increase theirs. As the rounds go on all four numbers approach 20. In this sense the equilibrium is stable: it is not merely an assumption but the point a reasonable learning process arrives at.

This is why common resources are exhausted more easily in large, anonymous groups.

Three kinds of remedy have been proposed for escaping the tragedy.

1. Pigouvian tax. In this remedy, named after the economist Arthur Pigou, a tax is levied on each unit that creates an externality. The cost the individual ignores is thereby added back to their calculation. If a herder pays a tax t per animal, the payoff becomes ui = gi(100 − G − t).

Example 12.13
  1. Best response. Derivative 100 − t − 2gi − G−i = 0 ⇒ gi = (100 − t − G−i)/2. The formula is the same as without the tax; only 100 − t replaces 100.
  2. Symmetric equilibrium (n = 4). g = (100 − t − 3g)/2 ⇒ g = (100 − t)/5.
  3. Targeting the optimum. We want G = 4g = 50, that is, g = 12.5: (100 − t)/5 = 12.5 ⇒ 100 − t = 62.5 ⇒ t = 37.5.
  4. Check. With the tax each herder grazes 12.5 animals; a herder nets 12.5 · (100 − 50 − 37.5) = 156.25 and the total tax revenue is 4 · 12.5 · 37.5 = 1,875. The herders’ net payoffs plus the tax revenue sum to 4 · 156.25 + 1,875 = 2,500, that is, the optimum.

For general n the same calculation gives g = (100 − t)/(n + 1) and, for the optimum, t = 100 − 50(n + 1)/n; for example, for n = 9, t ≈ 44.4.

The numbers in the check show an important property of the Pigouvian tax: the tax does not destroy total value, it only moves it around. If the revenue is redistributed to the herders in equal shares, independent of the number of animals, each herder receives 625; everyone is better off than in the no-tax equilibrium. The size of the tax is not arbitrary either: at the optimum, adding one more animal lowers the value of each of the other herders’ 37.5 animals by 1. The tax equals this externality (37.5) at the optimum.

2. Quotas or permits. The total number of animals is limited directly to 50 and the rights are divided among the herders. If rights can be bought and sold, the herders who will use them most productively buy them. Fishing quotas and carbon emission permits are examples of this remedy. A quota fixes the quantity directly; a tax fixes the price and leaves the quantity to individuals’ decisions.

3. Local institutions. The work of Elinor Ostrom showed that local communities can sustain their resources for centuries through monitoring, graduated sanctions, and shared rules. For this work Ostrom received the Nobel Prize in economics in 2009. The principles of this third path are given in 12.3.4.

12.3.4Ostrom’s design principles

According to Hardin there were only two ways out of the tragedy of the commons: either the state controls the resource centrally, or the resource is privatized and divided. Ostrom (1990) questioned this dichotomy. She studied many local governance arrangements that had lasted for centuries, from the Alpine pastures of Switzerland to the village forests of Japan, from the irrigation associations of Spain to the irrigation systems of the Philippines. In these arrangements the resource belonged neither to the state nor was it divided; the users governed themselves through shared rules. Ostrom identified the principles that the successful cases share.

Clear boundaries fix the set of players; they keep outsiders from coming in and exhausting the resource, and make clear “who is playing against whom.” Participation in the rules ensures that rules are made with local knowledge and are seen as legitimate. Monitoring makes it possible to notice deviation in a repeated game; if deviation goes unseen, no threat of punishment works. Graduated sanctions do two things: a small mistake or misunderstanding does not lead to a penalty that would break up the community, while repeated violation becomes steadily more costly. Cheap conflict resolution settles disputes before they grow where the rules are unclear. All of these mechanisms turn the conditions that sustain cooperation in repeated games (observation, punishment, a long relationship) into concrete institutions.

One of the cases Ostrom studied is from Turkey: the inshore fishermen of Alanya. After rising competition and fights over fishing grounds in the 1970s, the fishermen set up their own arrangement. At the start of each season the fishermen with fishing rights are listed and named fishing spots are allocated by lot. During the season the boats rotate, moving each day to the next spot in line with the direction of the fish migration. In this way everyone gets an equal share of good and bad spots, the number of boats in disputes falls, and the rule is easy to monitor: the boat fishing in the wrong spot is noticed at once by that spot’s right holder for the day. The rule did not come from the state, nor was the resource divided and sold; yet the institutional arrangement works.

Common mistake

“The solution for a common resource is either the state or the market.” Ostrom’s local institutions are a third way. A resource can be sustained, without being nationalized or privatized, through rules that the users set and monitor themselves. This does not mean that every common resource will manage itself: the principles are conditions found in the successful cases and missing in the failed ones.

12.3.5Social welfare criteria and the veil of ignorance

In the common-pasture example the word “optimum” was used for the situation that maximizes the total payoff. But the total is not the only way to evaluate a group’s outcomes. A policy can raise the total while leaving a few people very badly off. For this reason several criteria are used for social welfare.

Definition 12.11

Let the individuals’ utilities be u1, …, un.

  • Utilitarian criterion: SW = Σ ui. The option with the largest sum is best.
  • Egalitarian criterion: min ui. The option that maximizes the utility of the worst-off individual is best.
  • Nash social welfare: Π ui. The option with the largest product of utilities is best.

The three criteria can rank the same options differently. The optimum on the common pasture (G = 50, total 2,500) is the best by the utilitarian criterion. Nash social welfare stands somewhere in between: the product cares about the total but heavily penalizes any utility approaching zero, because a single zero wipes out the whole product.

Which criterion to choose is a philosophical question, but decision theory provides a tool that sharpens it. In the famous thought experiment of the philosopher John Rawls, the rules of a society are chosen without knowing who will be in which position. The decision maker stands behind a veil of ignorance: they do not know whether they will be born rich or poor, healthy or ill. The social choice then becomes an individual decision problem under uncertainty. Each policy is an “act,” each position a “state of nature,” and the uncertainty criteria of decision theory apply.

  • A decision maker who gives every position equal probability and maximizes expected utility applies the Laplace criterion. Since the expected utility is (1/n)Σui, this rule chooses the same policy as the utilitarian criterion (Harsanyi, 1955).
  • A decision maker who tries to make the worst case as good as possible applies the maximin rule and arrives at the egalitarian criterion (Rawls, 1971).
Example 12.14

In a society with three positions there are two policies: P = (10, 6, 2) and R = (5, 5, 5). The numbers are the utility of the person in each position.

  1. Laplace (Harsanyi). The mean of P is 18/3 = 6, the mean of R is 5. 6 > 5 ⇒ P.
  2. Maximin (Rawls). The worst position under P is 2, under R it is 5. 2 < 5 ⇒ R.
  3. Nash social welfare. The product for P is 10 · 6 · 2 = 120, for R it is 53 = 125. 125 > 120 ⇒ R.

The same table recommends different policies depending on which uncertainty criterion is chosen. The utilitarian criterion looks at the size of the total, the egalitarian criterion at the worst case; in this example the Nash criterion chooses the policy close to equality.

The strength of the veil argument is that it reduces an argument about justice to the structure of a decision problem on which everyone can agree. The debate does not vanish; it only moves: the question “Which policy is just?” becomes “Which decision rule is reasonable under uncertainty?” Someone strongly risk averse is closer to maximin, a risk-neutral person to the Laplace criterion. These criteria are also basic tools in algorithmic fairness work that studies the fair division of resources and tasks.

12.4Public goods and punishment

The common pasture is a problem of “taking”: everyone takes too much from the common resource. A public good is a problem of “giving”: everyone gives too little to the common project. The structure of the two problems is the same: an individual’s decision creates an effect that does not enter their own calculation. This section solves the Public Goods Game from the start of the chapter theoretically, explains the results of laboratory experiments with a numerical model, and examines how punishment protects cooperation and where it falls short.

12.4.1Public goods in the laboratory

A public good is a good whose use by one person does not reduce its use by another and from whose use no one can be excluded: street lighting, clean air, national defense, open-source software. Non-excludability means that those who do not contribute can benefit too. A person who benefits from others’ contributions without contributing is a free rider, and the behavior is called free riding.

The Public Goods Game (12.1.1) is the laboratory model of this structure. Each token contributed returns 0.4 to the contributor and 1.6 to the group in total. The theoretical solution takes one line.

Theorem 12.1 (Contributing nothing is dominant)

In the four-player Public Goods Game with payoff ui = 10 − ki + 0.4 · Σk, ki = 0 is a strictly dominant strategy for every player who cares only about their own money. In the unique Nash equilibrium everyone contributes 0 and receives 10; in the profile in which everyone contributes 10, everyone receives 16.

Whatever the other contributions are, raising player i’s contribution ki by 1 changes the payoff by −1 + 0.4 = −0.6: 1 token leaves the pocket and 0.4 returns from the pool. Since the payoff is a decreasing linear function of ki, the maximum is attained at ki = 0, and this does not depend on the others’ contributions. If every player plays their dominant strategy the profile is (0, 0, 0, 0) and everyone receives 10. If everyone contributes 10 the pool is 40, becomes 64 when multiplied, and everyone receives 16. □

Theory gives a clear prediction: zero contribution. Laboratory experiments show a different picture (Ledyard, 1995):

  • First rounds: about 40–60% of the endowment is contributed. Subjects are neither fully cooperative nor fully free riders.
  • Decline with repetition: when the game is repeated for about ten rounds with the same group, contributions fall round by round and approach the free-riding level in the last rounds.
  • Who? Fischbacher, Gächter, and Fehr (2001) asked subjects how much they would contribute for each possible value of the other members’ average contribution, and thereby derived each subject’s contribution schedule. About half the subjects are conditional cooperators: they want to contribute as much as the others do. About a third are free riders: they contribute 0 whatever the others do.

Figure 12.8 shows the schedules of the two types. The mechanism of the decline can be read from this figure. Conditional cooperators contribute slightly below the average; the blue line lies below the dashed line. Free riders pull the average down. In the next round the conditional cooperators look at this lower average and again contribute slightly below it. The contribution erodes a little more each round.

12.4.2Code trace: conditional()

The model is as follows: of four players, P1 always gives 0 (a free rider); P2, P3, and P4 are conditional cooperators and this round give the average of the others’ contributions in the previous round. Initially (t = 0) the three cooperators have contributed 10 tokens each. The function returns the contribution list for every round.

def conditional(c, rounds):
  history = [c]
  for t in range(rounds):
    new = [0]                   # P1 always free-rides
    for i in range(1, len(c)):
      others = sum(c) - c[i]
      new.append(others / (len(c) - 1))
    c = new
    history.append(c)
  return history

The code runs line by line as follows:

  • history stores the contribution lists of the rounds; the first element is the initial contributions.
  • In each round the list new starts with [0]: P1 (index 0) is always a free rider.
  • The inner loop runs over P2–P4 (indices 1, 2, 3). others = sum(c) - c[i] is the total contribution of the three players other than player i in the previous round. Dividing this total by len(c) - 1 = 3 gives the average, which becomes the player’s new contribution.
  • At the end of the round c is replaced by the new contributions and appended to the history. The order matters: all players in a round look at the previous round’s contributions; in the same round they do not see one another’s new decisions.

The pattern can be written in general. If the cooperators contribute x in one round, then in the next round the sum of the others for each is 0 + x + x = 2x and the average is 2x/3. Each round the contribution falls to two thirds of the previous value (shrinks by a third):

xt = 10 · (2/3)t:   10 → 6.7 → 4.4 → 3.0 → 2.0 → 1.3

Figure 12.9 shows the decline. In five rounds the contribution falls from 10 to 1.3: a single free rider dissolves the majority’s cooperation. The payoff columns are instructive too. In every round the free rider P1 earns more than the cooperators: at t = 0, 22 against 12. Free riding pays every round; the cooperators’ “punishment” is to cut their contributions. But this punishment hits everyone, themselves included: in round 5 the cooperators get 10.3 instead of 12. This simple model captures the qualitative features of the laboratory decline: a high start, round-by-round erosion, and an approach to the free-riding level in the last rounds.

Common mistake

“Since contributions fell, the subjects learned to free ride.” In this model nobody learns anything. The cooperators never change their rule: each round they contribute the others’ average. The decline arises by itself from the combination of the conditional-cooperation rule and a single free rider. In experiments, too, an important part of the decline is thought to come from this mechanism.

12.4.3With and without punishment: Fehr and Gächter

If the only weapon in the conditional cooperators’ hands is cutting their contributions, they punish themselves as they punish the free rider. Fehr and Gächter (2000) gave subjects a second weapon: direct punishment.

The design of the experiment is as follows. Groups of 4 play the public goods game; each token contributed returns 0.4 tokens to the contributor. At the end of each round the contributions are announced (anonymously). Then each subject may assign punishment points to the other members, paying from their own money; the payoff of whoever receives the points falls. Each group plays 10 rounds with and without punishment. The experiment was run in two forms: fixed groups, in which the same people play together every round, and groups reshuffled every round.

  • Without punishment: contributions are high in the first rounds and then fall toward zero. The dynamics of 12.4.2 are seen here as well.
  • With punishment: contributions rise; in fixed groups they approach full cooperation. Those who contribute furthest below the group average receive the most punishment.

The most surprising finding concerns the groups reshuffled every round. In these groups the probability of meeting the same person again is almost nil. Punishing gives the punisher nothing: the punished person’s future behavior does not affect the punisher’s future groups. Even so, subjects punished. The behavior of punishing, at one’s own cost, someone who violates a norm, when there is no material gain to oneself, is called altruistic punishment (Fehr and Gächter, 2002).

The theory’s prediction is clear. For someone who cares only about money, punishing is a pure cost; so nobody punishes. Knowing this, everyone behaves as if there were no possibility of punishment and again contributes 0. In the experiment punishment was both used and contributions stayed high: both of the theory’s predictions failed. Inequity aversion and reciprocity explain this behavior: the free rider is ahead and acts with bad intentions; punishing the free rider reduces the inequality and answers the intention.

12.4.4Code trace: punish()

Can punishment make free riding unprofitable? The function below computes the players’ payoffs given contributions and punishment points. The rule is this: the punisher pays 1 token for each point, and the punished loses 3 tokens for each point. A punishment point harms the punished three times as much as it costs the punisher; punishment is effective but also expensive for the punisher.

def punish(c, p):        # p[i][j]: i's points to j
    n = len(c)
    share = 1.6 * sum(c) / n
    u = [10 - c[i] + share for i in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            u[i] -= p[i][j]       # costs 1 per point
            u[j] -= 3 * p[i][j]   # target loses 3
    return u

The code runs line by line as follows:

  • p[i][j] is the number of punishment points player i gives to player j; p is a points matrix.
  • share is the amount from the pool that falls to each player: the total contribution is multiplied by 1.6 and divided by n.
  • u holds the payoffs before punishment: the tokens kept, 10 - c[i], plus the pool share.
  • The nested loop runs over every (i, j) pair. The number of points is deducted from the punisher i’s payoff, and 3 times the points from the punished j’s payoff.

Let the call be punish([0, 10, 10, 10], p): P1 is a free rider and P2–P4 contribute fully. Each cooperator gives P1 2 punishment points; in the points matrix p[1][0] = p[2][0] = p[3][0] = 2 and everything else is 0.

Punishment deters free riding, but punishing is also costly. Let the same punish() function be run under three different punishment arrangements (Table 12.13).

The last row is important. P4 contributed but did not punish. The punishment by P2 and P3 has already erased the free rider’s advantage; P4 enjoys this benefit without bearing the cost of punishing and receives the highest payoff in the group (12). Punishment is itself a public good: its cost falls on the punisher, its benefit (deterring the free rider, higher contributions in the future) on everyone. A cooperator who does not punish is a second-order free rider, and the behavior is second-order free riding. Punishing those who do not punish looks like a solution, but that punishment is costly too and the problem moves up one level: third-order free riding.

For players who care only about money the result is found by backward induction. In the last round punishing is only a cost, because there is no future round for the punishment to deter; nobody punishes. Players who know this do not contribute in the last round. In the round before, the behavior of the future rounds is already settled; punishing is again only a cost. This logic runs all the way back: the threat is not credible and contributions fall to 0. In experiments, however, people do punish (12.4.3): altruistic punishment partly overcomes the problem of second-order free riding.

It has also been found that punishment does not work the same way everywhere. Herrmann, Thöni, and Gächter (2008) ran the same public goods experiment with punishment in 16 cities, Istanbul among them. In some cities subjects punished not only low contributors but also high contributors. Punishing those who comply with norms is called antisocial punishment. Where antisocial punishment was common, the cooperation-raising effect of punishment was largely erased. The authors found that antisocial punishment was more frequent in societies where civic norms and the rule of law are weak. One possible cause of antisocial punishment is revenge: a free rider punished in the previous round retaliates against the high contributors they believe punished them.

The lesson of this finding is that punishment is an institution, and how it works depends on the norms around it. When designing a platform or an institution, the rule “users may punish one another” does not by itself produce cooperation; who uses the rule, against whom, and on what grounds also matters. Ostrom’s principles of graduated sanctions and accountable monitoring (12.3.4) can also be read as institutional safeguards that limit the abuse of punishment.

Common mistake

“Punishment always increases cooperation.” Punishment is costly and gives rise to second-order free riding; antisocial punishment can erase its effect. Whether the option to punish raises cooperation depends largely on whether those who use it target the norm violators rather than the norm followers.

12.4.6Back to the Public Goods Game

The two questions at the start of the chapter can now be answered. The first was the contribution predicted by theory and by experiments. Theory’s answer was given in Theorem 12.1: 0 tokens. Each token contributed returns only 1.6/4 = 0.4 to the contributor; whatever the others do, not contributing is better by 0.6 per token. In reality, in repeated experiments contributions in the first rounds are about 40–60% of the endowment and decline as rounds go on (Ledyard, 1995). When participants can punish one another at their own expense, contributions stay high (Fehr and Gächter, 2000).

The second question was the game’s “Contribution with punishment” scenario: anyone contributing fewer than 3 tokens is fined 2 tokens. Here the punishment comes not from the players but from the rule itself; the punisher bears no cost. This rule changes the players’ incentives.

12.5Threshold projects

In the public goods game every token contributed enlarges the pool a little; the relationship between contribution and benefit is linear. In many real projects, however, the benefit appears only when a certain threshold is reached. A bridge built halfway is useless; a crowdfunding campaign that misses its target produces no product; a petition that does not collect enough signatures is not taken up. This structure turns the contribution problem into an entirely different game.

12.5.1Contribution becomes a coordination game

Definition 12.12

Threshold public good (or threshold project): a public good from which everyone receives a benefit if the total contribution reaches a certain threshold, and the contributions are wasted if it does not. In the threshold-project scenario of the Public Goods Game, 4 players start with 10 tokens each; if the total contribution reaches 22 everyone receives 12 extra tokens, and if it does not the contributions are lost:

ui = 10 − ki + 12 · [Σk ≥ 22]

The equilibria of this game are very different from those of the linear public goods game. Contributing nothing is no longer dominant: when the threshold is missed by a hair, the player who supplies the missing tokens gains 12. But contributing is not dominant either: if the threshold cannot be reached, every contribution is wasted. A player’s best response depends on what the others contribute. This turns the game into a coordination game: the right decision for the players depends on their sharing the same expectation as the others.

There are two kinds of equilibrium.

Equilibrium 1: nobody contributes. If everyone contributes 0, the most a single player can contribute is 10 tokens and the threshold of 22 cannot be reached. A contributor only loses. Nobody wants to deviate; everyone receives 10, 40 in total.

Equilibrium 2: the total is exactly 22. If the total contribution is exactly 22, every contributor is pivotal: withdrawing even 1 token drops the total to 21, the threshold is missed, and 12 tokens are lost. Contributing extra is pointless too: the threshold is already met and the excess is wasted. For example, in the profile (6, 6, 5, 5) the payoffs are (16, 16, 17, 17), 66 in total.

Which equilibrium is played? Theory does not say. The zero equilibrium is a safe equilibrium that exposes nobody to risk; the threshold equilibrium is better for everyone but requires trust. A contributor whose partners do not contribute burns the contribution: a player who contributes 6 falls to 4 instead of 10 if the others do not contribute. Without trust the zero equilibrium may be chosen. This is the same dilemma as in the Stag Hunt: the cooperative equilibrium is Pareto dominant, but a lone cooperator suffers a big loss.

A second coordination problem is the multiplicity of equilibria that reach the threshold. The contribution profile (10, 10, 2, 0) is also an equilibrium; the payoffs are (12, 12, 20, 22). Every profile whose total is exactly 22 is an equilibrium, and in all of them the group’s total payoff is the same: 40 − 22 + 4 · 12 = 66. The threshold equilibria are efficient but may be unequal. Who contributes how much is a bargaining problem: every player wants the others to contribute more.

In real-life threshold projects a mechanism that eases these problems is common: the refund. In crowdfunding campaigns that miss their target the money is returned to the contributors. Then if the threshold is not met the contribution is not burned; the risk of “contributing and burning it” disappears. The player’s contribution is spent only when the threshold is met, that is, the contribution is paid only when the 12 is received in return. This rule erases the safety advantage of the zero equilibrium and makes coordination easier.

12.5.2Which contribution profiles are equilibria?

To check whether a profile is an equilibrium, we ask whether any player can raise their payoff by deviating alone. In a threshold project there are two kinds of deviation: lowering the contribution (if the threshold is met) and raising it (if it is not). Let us check five profiles in turn.

Example 12.16
  1. (0, 0, 0, 0). If P1 alone contributed 10, the total would be 10 < 22 and the 10 tokens would be burned. No player can reach the threshold alone; nobody wants to deviate. It is a Nash equilibrium; everyone’s payoff is 10.
  2. (6, 6, 5, 4). The total is 21, the threshold is missed; the payoffs are (4, 4, 5, 6). If P4 contributes 5 the total becomes 22 and P4 gets 10 − 5 + 12 = 17 > 6. It is not an equilibrium.
  3. (6, 6, 5, 5). The total is 22, the payoffs are (16, 16, 17, 17). If P1 drops to 5 the total becomes 21 and P1 gets 10 − 5 = 5 < 16. The same holds for the other players; contributing more also pays in vain. It is a Nash equilibrium; the total is 66.
  4. (7, 6, 5, 5). The total is 23. If P1 drops to 6 the total becomes 22, the threshold is still met, and P1 gains 1 token (16 instead of 15). This profile is not an equilibrium.
  5. (10, 10, 2, 0). The total is 22. If P3 withdraws (contributes 0) the total becomes 20 and the threshold is missed: P3 gets 10 instead of 20. P1 and P2 are pivotal too. If P4 contributes, P4 only loses. It is a Nash equilibrium; the payoffs are (12, 12, 20, 22).

Figure 12.11 shows the (6, 6, 5, 5) equilibrium. A general conclusion follows from these five checks: in this game the Nash equilibria are of exactly two kinds. Either everyone contributes 0, or the total contribution is exactly 22. If the total is between 0 and 22 the threshold is not met; any player who contributes gains by cutting the contribution to 0, because it is wasted anyway. If the total exceeds 22, any contributing player reduces the contribution by 1, the threshold is still met, and the player gains 1 token. Only the profiles with total 0 and total exactly 22 remain; 12.5.1 showed that both kinds are equilibria. Scanning all possible profiles by computer confirms this: of the 114 = 14,641 profiles in which each player can contribute between 0 and 10, all 850 profiles with total exactly 22, and only those, together with the zero profile, are equilibria. The efficient equilibria are many; who contributes what is a bargaining and coordination problem.

Common mistake

“A threshold project has a single equilibrium.” Both zero contribution and every profile whose total is exactly 22 are equilibria. Moreover, “equal contributions (5 each)” is not a special equilibrium; an equal split is not even possible with whole tokens, and very unequal profiles such as (10, 10, 2, 0) are also equilibria.

12.6Collective information

In the earlier sections the interests of the group members conflicted: when one took more, the others lost. In this section everyone’s interest is the same: finding the right answer to a question. The difficulty is that each member knows the answer only in part. Whether the group can pool this scattered knowledge depends on how information is shared. Three models treat the question from different angles: the wisdom of crowds (averaging estimates), information cascades (decisions made in sequence, each following the last), and the Condorcet jury theorem (majority voting).

12.6.1The wisdom of crowds

In 1906 Francis Galton came across an interesting competition at a livestock fair in England. Visitors bought tickets to guess the weight of an ox that was to be slaughtered and dressed. After the contest Galton collected and examined 787 valid guesses. The median of the guesses (the one in the middle when sorted) was 1,207 pounds; the ox’s actual weight was 1,198 pounds: an error of less than 1% (about 0.75%). Among the participants were butchers and farmers, but most were non-experts; the combination of their guesses was strikingly accurate (Galton, 1907).

This phenomenon is known as the wisdom of crowds. The basic explanation is that independent errors cancel out. One person overestimates the weight and another underestimates it; if the errors point in different directions and are independent of one another, they largely cancel in the average. Scott Page turned this intuition into an exact identity.

Theorem 12.2 (Diversity prediction theorem)

Let the true value be θ, the estimates of n people x1, …, xn, and their average x̄. Then

(x̄ − θ)2 = (1/n) Σ (xi − θ)2 − (1/n) Σ (xi − x̄)2

In words: the crowd’s error = the average of the individual squared errors − the diversity of the predictions (Page, 2007). Diversity is the average of the squared deviations of the estimates from their own mean.

Split each individual error into two parts: xi − θ = (xi − x̄) + (x̄ − θ). Square it: (xi − θ)2 = (xi − x̄)2 + 2(xi − x̄)(x̄ − θ) + (x̄ − θ)2. Sum over all i and divide by n. In the middle term (x̄ − θ) is the same for every i and comes outside; what remains is Σ(xi − x̄). This sum is zero, because the deviations from the mean always add up to zero: Σxi − n · x̄ = 0. The last term, summed n times and divided by n, is (x̄ − θ)2. So we obtain: average individual error = diversity + the crowd’s error; rearranging gives the theorem. □

Example 12.17

The true value is 50; five people’s estimates are 40, 45, 55, 60, 70.

  1. Average. (40 + 45 + 55 + 60 + 70)/5 = 270/5 = 54.
  2. The crowd’s error. (54 − 50)2 = 16.
  3. Individual squared errors. (x − 50)2: 100, 25, 25, 100, 400. Total 650, average 130.
  4. Diversity. (x − 54)2: 196, 81, 1, 36, 256. Total 570, average 114.
  5. Check. 130 − 114 = 16 ✓.

The crowd’s error (16) is far smaller than the error of an average member (130); the difference is exactly the diversity (114).

The consequences of the theorem are strong. Since diversity is never negative, the crowd’s error is never larger than the error of an average member: the crowd always predicts at least as well as its average member. The greater the diversity, the greater the gain. A smaller example shows the same relation: let the true value be 10 and the estimates 8, 12, 16. The average is 12 and the crowd’s error is 4. The individual squared errors are 4, 4, 36 (average 44/3) and the diversity terms are 16, 0, 16 (average 32/3); the difference is 12/3 = 4 ✓.

The theorem is an identity and always true; but the crowd’s being truly wise depends on two conditions: independence and diversity. If the estimates are not independent of one another, the errors do not cancel but pile up in the same direction. If everyone follows everyone else, the estimates converge, diversity collapses, and the crowd can be as wrong as a single member. This collapse arises even among rational individuals in information cascades (12.6.2).

Common mistake

“The crowd is better than its best member.” The theorem does not say this. The crowd is at least as good as the average member; it can be worse than the best member. In the example the error of those who guessed 45 and 55 is 25; the crowd’s 16 beats them, but had there been someone who knew the true value exactly, that person’s error would have been 0.

12.6.2Information cascades

In a new city two restaurants stand side by side. One has a queue; the other is empty. Most people join the queue: “that many people can’t be wrong.” But each of the people in the queue may have thought the same thing. The first few may have entered that restaurant by chance and the later ones followed them. In that case the queue carries almost no information about the restaurant’s quality. Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch (1992) studied this phenomenon with an exact model.

The model. There are two urns. Urn A contains 2 a-balls and 1 b-ball, urn B contains 1 a-ball and 2 b-balls (Figure 12.12). One of the urns is chosen by a coin toss; nobody knows which. In turn, each person secretly draws a ball from the urn, sees it, and puts it back. Then they hear the earlier people’s guesses (not their balls) and say aloud whether they think the urn is A or B. A correct guess is rewarded.

Each ball is the urn’s own color with probability 2/3; this is why the ball is called a signal: it carries noisy but useful information about the urn. Everyone’s question is the same: given the information at hand, what is the probability that the urn is A?

Bayesian calculation. At the start the two urns are equally likely: P(A) = 1/2. This is the prior probability. After an a signal is seen, the posterior probability is computed with Bayes’ rule:

P(A | a) = P(a | A) · P(A) / [P(a | A) · P(A) + P(a | B) · P(B)] = (⅔ · ½) / (⅔ · ½ + ⅓ · ½) = 2/3

The way to simplify the calculation is to think in terms of odds. The odds in favor of A start at 1 to 1. An a signal has probability 2/3 under A and 1/3 under B; so each a doubles the odds in favor of A. Each b halves them. With independent signals the order does not matter; only how many more a’s than b’s there are matters. When there are f more a’s than b’s the odds are 2f to 1 and

P(A) = 2f / (2f + 1)

Example 12.18
  1. f = 0 (no signals, or equal numbers of a and b): P(A) = 1/(1 + 1) = 1/2.
  2. f = 1 (one more a): P(A) = 2/3.
  3. f = 2 (two more a’s): P(A) = 4/5 = 0.8.
  4. f = −2 (two more b’s): P(A) = 1/(4 + 1) = 0.2.
  5. Signals a, a, b (f = 1): the long way, P(A) = (⅔ · ⅔ · ⅓) / (⅔ · ⅔ · ⅓ + ⅓ · ⅓ · ⅔) = (4/27)/(4/27 + 2/27) = 2/3. The shortcut gives the same result.

What do people do? The first person has only their own signal; on seeing a they say A, on seeing b they say B. Their guess reveals their signal exactly. The second person infers the first person’s signal from the first guess and combines it with their own. If the two signals agree, they say so. If they differ, f = 0 and the two urns are equally likely; by the model’s convention, in a tie the person follows their own signal. So the second person’s guess also reveals their signal.

The cascade. If the first two people saw a and said A, the third person infers two a’s from the earlier guesses: f = 2. If their own signal is b, f = 2 − 1 = 1 and P(A) = 2/3 > 1/2. It is rational to ignore their own signal and say A. Had their own signal been a, they would also say A. So the third person’s guess is A whatever their signal, and carries no information about it. The fourth person can learn nothing from the third saying A; they are in exactly the same position as the third and say A for the same reason. Everyone after that says A.

Definition 12.13

Information cascade: a person finds the information inferred from the behavior of those before them stronger than their own private information, ignores their own information and follows the others, so that their behavior carries no new information for those after. In the two-urn model the cascade begins when the difference inferred from earlier guesses reaches |f| = 2.

Cascades have two important properties. First, they can be wrong. Even if the true urn is B, the probability that the first two people both draw a is ⅓ · ⅓ = 1/9; in that case the whole crowd falls into a wrong A cascade, and it is not corrected no matter how many people later see b. Second, they are fragile. A cascade rests on only two signals; the hundreds of people saying A really carry the information of two balls. A single piece of public information (for example, two b balls that everyone can see) can break it. The sudden reversals of fashion trends, product reviews, and financial panics are explained by this fragility: a long-running trend can be completely reversed by a small new piece of information.

The model has also been tested in the laboratory. Anderson and Holt (1997) ran this urn experiment with students. In most of the situations in which cascades could form (that is, in which the difference inferred from the guesses reached 2), cascades did form: subjects followed those before them even when it went against their own signals.

12.6.3Code trace: cascade()

The cascade model can be written as a function of a few lines. The function takes the sequence of signals and returns each person’s guess. The variable f is the a − b difference inferred from the earlier guesses.

def cascade(signals):
    f = 0                  # a − b inferred from guesses
    guesses = []
    for s in signals:
        if f >= 2: t = 'A'     # A cascade
        elif f <= -2: t = 'B'  # B cascade
        else:
            t = 'A' if s == 'a' else 'B'
            f += 1 if s == 'a' else -1
        guesses.append(t)
    return guesses

The code runs line by line as follows:

  • f starts at 0: there are no guesses yet.
  • If f >= 2 there is an A cascade: the person says A whatever their own signal. If f <= -2 there is a B cascade: the person says B. In these two cases f does not change, because a guess inside a cascade carries no information for those after.
  • Otherwise (|f| ≤ 1) the person follows their own signal. When f = 1, their own a makes f = 2 and their own b makes f = 0, and in a tie they follow their own signal; f = −1 is symmetric. In this case the guess reveals the signal; later people can infer it, so f is updated.

Common mistake

“Those who follow the cascade are irrational,” or “they conform out of social pressure.” In the model nobody feels social pressure and everyone calculates correctly by Bayes. Person 3’s saying A is the best guess given the information at hand. The problem lies not in the individuals but in how information is shared: people announce their guesses, not their signals.

12.6.4The Condorcet jury theorem

In cascades people decide in turn and follow one another. In a jury, by contrast, everyone votes at the same time and independently; the decision is made by majority. There is a single right answer (is the defendant guilty or not?). The question is how the probability that the majority vote finds this right answer changes as the jury grows. This question differs from the Condorcet paradox, in which preferences go in a cycle: the paradox asks “is the group’s preference consistent?”; the jury theorem asks “does the group find the truth?”

Theorem 12.3 (Condorcet jury theorem)

If n (an odd number of) voters vote independently and each picks the right answer with probability p > 1/2, the probability that the majority is right increases with n and tends to 1 as n → ∞ (Condorcet, 1785). If p < 1/2 the opposite holds: as the crowd grows, the majority is almost surely wrong.

To compute the probability that the majority is right we use the binomial probability. The probability that exactly k of the n voters vote correctly is the product of the number of ways to choose the k correct voters and the probability of a single such choice:

P(exactly k correct) = C(n, k) · pk · (1 − p)n − k

Here C(n, k) is the number of ways to choose k people out of n (for example C(5, 3) = 10). pk is the probability that all k chosen people are right; (1 − p)n − k is the probability that all the rest are wrong; thanks to independence the probabilities multiply. If the number of correct votes is greater than n/2, the majority is right. Therefore

Pn = Σk > n/2 C(n, k) · pk · (1 − p)n − k

Example 12.19

A jury of three, p = 0.6. A majority needs at least 2 correct votes.

  1. All three correct. C(3, 3) · 0.63 = 0.216.
  2. Exactly two correct. The wrong voter can be chosen in 3 different ways: C(3, 2) · 0.62 · 0.4 = 3 · 0.36 · 0.4 = 0.432.
  3. Total. P3 = 0.216 + 0.432 = 0.648.

A single voter is right with probability 60%; the majority of a three-person jury is right with probability 64.8%.

12.6.5Code trace: jury()

The binomial sum is computed with a short function. Python’s math.comb(n, k) gives C(n, k).

from math import comb
def jury(n, p):
    total = 0
    for k in range(n // 2 + 1, n + 1):
        term = comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k)
        total += term
    return total
print(round(jury(5, 0.6), 4))

The code runs line by line as follows:

  • range(n // 2 + 1, n + 1) produces the numbers of correct votes that give a majority. n // 2 is integer division; for n = 5 it is 2, so the loop takes k = 3, 4, 5.
  • For each k, term is the probability that exactly k people are right, and it is added to total.
  • The last line rounds jury(5, 0.6) to four decimals and prints it.

12.6.6When does the jury theorem break down?

The theorem has two assumptions: the votes are independent, and each voter’s probability of finding the truth is greater than a half. A third, implicit assumption is that voters vote honestly on what they know. When any of these fails, the theorem’s conclusion fails too.

Dependence. In a jury of 5, if 4 people follow the vote of an opinion leader, the majority is always that person’s vote and the probability of being right is 0.6. With independent votes it was 0.683. Dependence reduces the jury to a single voter; the crowd’s advantage is lost. Information cascades and common information sources (everyone reading the same news story) are the main mechanisms that break independence.

Competence. On a question where p < 1/2, for example when a clue misleads everyone in the same direction, error becomes certain as the crowd grows: with p = 0.4 a majority of 101 is right with probability only 2.1%. Enlarging the crowd strengthens not the information but the shared mistake.

Strategic voting. If voters take into account that their vote matters only when it can change the outcome, that is, when it is pivotal, voting against their own signal can be rational (Austen-Smith and Banks, 1996). For example, under a unanimity rule a juror’s vote is pivotal only when all the other members say “guilty.” It follows that all the other members saw a sign of guilt; this information can be strong enough to override the juror’s own “innocent” sign. By this logic jurors may drift to a “guilty” vote, and the unanimity rule can raise the probability of convicting an innocent person (Feddersen and Pesendorfer, 1998). A rule designed to protect the innocent can work in reverse because of strategic behavior.

The wisdom of crowds, cascades, and the jury theorem teach the same lesson: a group’s knowledge is valuable to the extent that its members’ independent information can come together. Averaging, sequential decisions, and majority voting are three different mechanisms for combining information; all three fail when independence is broken.

Common mistake

“The jury theorem and the Condorcet paradox are the same.” Both bear Condorcet’s name but answer different questions. The theorem assumes a right answer exists and concerns the probability that the majority finds it. The paradox looks for no right answer; it shows that, even when individual preferences are consistent, majority preferences can cycle (A > B, B > C, C > A).

12.7Common mistakes in collective decisions and example problems

Because the results of the models in the second half of the chapter resemble one another, they are easily, and sometimes wrongly, generalized. The list below gives the most frequent misconceptions together with the correct statements.

Common mistake
  • “The crowd is always wise.” Independence and p > 1/2 are required. In the cascade all six of six people were wrong; with p = 0.4 a large jury is almost surely wrong.
  • “Those who follow a cascade are irrational.” Each acted correctly by Bayes; the problem is that information does not accumulate.
  • “Punishment always increases cooperation.” Punishment is costly and gives rise to second-order free riding; antisocial punishment can erase its effect.
  • “A threshold project has a single equilibrium.” Both zero contribution and every profile whose total is exactly 22 are equilibria.
  • “The solution for a common resource is either the state or the market.” Ostrom’s local institutions are a third way.
  • “The jury theorem and the Condorcet paradox are the same.” The theorem concerns the probability of finding the truth, the paradox the cycling of preferences.

The five problems below use the tools of the chapter together. Each changes one parameter of an example worked in the text; the solution path is familiar, but the numbers must be recomputed.

Example 12.20

Question. With the prospect theory parameters (v(x) = x0.88, λ = 2.25), is a +300 / −150 coin-toss bet accepted?

Solution. v(300) = 3000.88 ≈ 151.3, v(−150) = −2.25 · 1500.88 ≈ −2.25 · 82.2 ≈ −185.0. The prospect value is ½ · 151.3 − ½ · 185.0 ≈ 75.7 − 92.5 ≈ −16.8 < 0: the bet is rejected. It is rejected although its expected value is +75 because the gain falls below the acceptance threshold (150 · 2.51 ≈ 377 TL).

Example 12.21

Question. In the commons model (ui = gi(100 − G)) with n = 9 herders, what are the equilibrium G* and the total payoff? What tax achieves the optimum?

Solution. g* = 100/(n + 1) = 100/10 = 10, G* = 90; the value per animal is 10 and the total is 90 · 10 = 900 (36% of the optimum). With the tax, g = (100 − t)/10; for the optimum G = 50, g = 50/9, that is, (100 − t)/10 = 50/9 ⇒ 100 − t = 55.6 ⇒ t ≈ 44.4.

Example 12.22

Question. In the punished scenario of the Public Goods Game, what would the equilibrium contribution be if the fine were 1 token instead of 2?

Solution. Contributing 3 tokens costs 3 · 0.6 = 1.8, while the fine is only 1 token. Whatever the others contribute, contributing nothing is 1.8 − 1 = 0.8 better; contributing 1 or 2 both costs tokens and does not avoid the fine. The equilibrium contribution is 0; a fine rule of this size is ineffective.

Example 12.23

Question. In the Fehr–Schmidt model, at least how many of the 10 tokens does a responder with α = 2 demand?

Solution. s ≥ 10α/(1 + 2α) = 20/5 = 4. Check: for s = 4, 4 − 2 · (10 − 8) = 0; accepting and rejecting give the same utility and the acceptance condition (≥ 0) is met. For s = 3, 3 − 2 · 4 = −5 < 0, reject. The responder demands at least 4 tokens.

Example 12.24

Question. In the Condorcet jury theorem with p = 0.7, with what probability is the majority of a 3-person jury right?

Solution. P3 = 0.73 + 3 · 0.72 · 0.3 = 0.343 + 0.441 = 0.784. A single voter’s accuracy of 0.7 rises to 0.784 in a majority of three.

12.8Summary

  • Bounded rationality: because information, time, and computation are limited, people look for “good enough.” The deviations are systematic, not random; that is why they can be modeled.
  • Prospect theory: outcomes are evaluated as gains or losses relative to a reference point. The value function is concave for gains, convex for losses, and λ ≈ 2.25 times steeper for losses. The ±100 TL bet is rejected (−36.0); the acceptance threshold is ≈ 251 TL. Probability weighting overstates small probabilities (w(0.01) ≈ 0.055).
  • The endowment effect and framing have the same root: when the reference point changes, the same object or the same program is valued differently. For the mug the selling/buying ratio is λ; in the epidemic problem 72% choose the sure option in one frame and 78% the risky option in the other.
  • Quantal response: in logit choice the better option is chosen more often; λ = 0 is random, λ → ∞ is the best response. In asymmetric Matching Pennies, QRE captures the shift toward the large payoff (0.83 at λ = 0.02) instead of Nash’s 1/2.
  • Social preferences: the average share in the dictator game is about 28%, and offers in the ultimatum game are 40–50%. In the Fehr–Schmidt model a responder with α = 1 rejects offers below 10/3; a dictator with β > 1/2 splits equally. In the trust game 5.16 dollars are sent on average and 4.66 come back: reciprocity responds to intention.
  • The commons: in the equilibrium with 4 herders g* = 20, total 1,600; the optimum is 2,500 (64%). As n grows the externality grows and the total goes to zero. The Pigouvian tax (t = 37.5), quotas, and local institutions based on Ostrom’s eight principles are remedies.
  • Welfare criteria: the utilitarian, egalitarian, and Nash social welfare criteria can choose different policies. Behind the veil of ignorance the Laplace rule leads to the utilitarian criterion (Harsanyi) and maximin to the egalitarian criterion (Rawls).
  • Public goods and punishment: in theory contributing nothing is dominant; in experiments contributions start at 40–60% and erode. Conditional cooperation and a single free rider produce this decline (10 → 1.3). Punishment protects cooperation but is costly; it can give rise to second-order free riding and antisocial punishment. A punishment imposed as a rule moves the equilibrium from 0 to 3.
  • Threshold projects: contribution becomes a coordination game; the zero equilibrium and every profile with total exactly 22 are equilibria. Threshold equilibria can be efficient (total 66) but unequal; a refund makes coordination easier.
  • Collective information: the diversity prediction theorem (the crowd’s error = average individual error − diversity); information does not accumulate in a cascade (six of six people are wrong); the jury theorem strengthens the majority for independent and competent voters (5 voters, p = 0.6 ⇒ 0.683). All three mechanisms rest on independence.

12.10Test Yourself

  1. With the prospect theory parameters (v(x) = x0.88, λ = 2.25), is a bet giving +150 TL or −50 TL with equal probability accepted? What is the least gain needed to take the risk of losing 50 TL?

    Answer

    v(150) = 1500.88 ≈ 82.2, v(−50) = −2.25 · 500.88 ≈ −2.25 · 31.3 ≈ −70.4. The prospect value is ½ · 82.2 − ½ · 70.4 ≈ 5.9 > 0: accepted. The threshold is G = 50 · 2.251/0.88 ≈ 50 · 2.51 ≈ 125.7 TL; 150 is above it. The ratio (≈ 2.51) does not depend on the size of the loss.

  2. A 10-token ultimatum game is played in the Fehr–Schmidt model and offers are whole numbers. What is the least number of tokens a responder with α = 0.5 will accept? What is the answer for a responder with α = 3?

    Answer

    The threshold is 10α/(1 + 2α). For α = 0.5: 5/2 = 2.5; for s = 2, 2 − 0.5 · 6 = −1 (reject), for s = 3, 3 − 0.5 · 4 = +1 (accept): at least 3. For α = 3: 30/7 ≈ 4.29; for s = 4, 4 − 3 · 2 = −2 (reject); s = 5 is an equal split and is accepted: at least 5. However large α is, the threshold does not exceed 5.

  3. Three herders graze animals on a common pasture; the value per animal is 60 − G and herder i’s payoff is gi(60 − G). What are the symmetric Nash equilibrium, the social optimum, the ratio of the equilibrium to the optimum, and the tax per animal that achieves the optimum?

    Answer

    The best response is gi = (60 − G−i)/2. Symmetric: g = (60 − 2g)/2 ⇒ 4g = 60 ⇒ g* = 15, G* = 45; the value per animal is 15, per herder 225, total 675. Check: when the others have 30, (60 − 30)/2 = 15. Optimum: G(60 − G) is maximal ⇒ G = 30, total 900. The ratio is 675/900 = 75%. With the tax g = (60 − t)/4, G = 3(60 − t)/4 = 30 ⇒ t = 20.

  4. In the Public Goods Game (4 players, 10 tokens each, multiplier 1.6), what would the equilibrium contribution be if the rule were “anyone contributing fewer than 5 tokens is fined 2 tokens”? What would the equilibrium contribution and payoff per player be if the fine were 4 tokens?

    Answer

    Contributing 5 costs 5 · 0.6 = 3 tokens relative to contributing 0. If the fine is 2, the fine avoided is less than 3: not contributing is better by 1 token, and the equilibrium contribution is 0. If the fine is 4, 4 > 3: contributing 5 is better by 1 token; more than 5 costs 0.6 per token, and 1–4 both costs tokens and does not avoid the fine. 5 is dominant. Everyone contributes 5: the pool is 20 · 1.6 = 32, the share per player 8, the payoff 10 − 5 + 8 = 13.

  5. In a threshold project (4 players, 10 tokens each, threshold 22, reward 12), is the profile (9, 9, 4, 0) a Nash equilibrium? What are the payoffs? What is the answer for the profile (5, 5, 5, 5)?

    Answer

    (9, 9, 4, 0): the total is 22, the payoffs are (13, 13, 18, 22). If a contributor reduces by 1 the total becomes 21 and the threshold is missed (for example if P3 contributes 3 it gets 7 < 18); contributing extra is a wasted payment; if P4 contributes it only loses. It is a Nash equilibrium; the total is 66. (5, 5, 5, 5): the total is 20, the threshold is missed and everyone gets 5. If P1 contributes 7 the total becomes 22 and P1 gets 10 − 7 + 12 = 15 > 5: it is not an equilibrium.

  6. In the two-urn cascade model the true urn is A. The signals the five people see, in order, are a, b, b, b, a. What is each person’s guess? What would P(A) be if the five signals were pooled?

    Answer

    Person 1: a ⇒ A, f = 1. Person 2 sees b; together with the earlier guess f = 0, P(A) = 1/2; in a tie they follow their own signal ⇒ B, f = 0. Person 3: b ⇒ P(A) = 1/3, B, f = −1. Person 4: b ⇒ with their own b f = −2, P(A) = 1/5, B; the f inferred from the earlier guesses becomes −2 and a B cascade begins. Person 5 sees a; from the earlier guesses −2, with their own a −1, P(A) = 1/3 < 1/2 ⇒ B. The guesses are A, B, B, B, B. Had the five signals been pooled, 2 a’s and 3 b’s ⇒ f = −1, P(A) = 1/3; in this example the signals themselves are misleading, and even pooling the information would not have given the right answer.

  7. Each voter independently votes correctly with probability p = 0.8. What is the probability that the majority is right in juries of 3 and 5? What happens in a jury of 5 if four members copy the fifth’s vote?

    Answer

    P3 = 0.83 + 3 · 0.82 · 0.2 = 0.512 + 0.384 = 0.896. P5 = 10 · 0.83 · 0.22 + 5 · 0.84 · 0.2 + 0.85 = 0.2048 + 0.4096 + 0.32768 ≈ 0.942. If four members copy, the five votes are always the same; the majority is one person’s vote and the probability falls to 0.8.

  8. In a society with three positions the policies’ utilities are P = (9, 4, 2), Q = (4, 4, 4), R = (6, 5, 3). Which policy do the utilitarian criterion (Laplace), the egalitarian criterion (maximin), and Nash social welfare choose?

    Answer

    The averages are 15/3 = 5, 12/3 = 4, 14/3 ≈ 4.67 ⇒ the utilitarian criterion chooses P. The worst positions are 2, 4, 3 ⇒ maximin chooses Q. The products are 9 · 4 · 2 = 72, 43 = 64, 6 · 5 · 3 = 90 ⇒ Nash social welfare chooses R. The three criteria choose three different policies.
