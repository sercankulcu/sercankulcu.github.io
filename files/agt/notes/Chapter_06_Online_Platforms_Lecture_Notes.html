6.1Platforms and markets

Every topic in this chapter rests on a single observation: the value of a platform depends on the number and identity of the people who use it. This observation overturns the usual market intuitions. Paying one side of the market can be profitable. Everyone’s expectations can decide the fate of the market. In an auction called “second price,” truthful bidding may not be the best strategy.

6.1.1A game to start with: the sponsored search auction

When someone types “cheap flights” into a search engine, a few ads appear above the results. The positions of these ads are not chosen at random. For every search, a small auction is held among the advertisers who have bid on that keyword. Advertisers bid per click: “if a user clicks my ad, I will pay at most this much.” Ads are placed into three slots according to a score that is the product of the bid and a quality measure. The top slot is more visible and gets more clicks.

Figure 6.1 shows the game’s screen. The game asks two simple questions. First: if a click earns the advertiser 8 TL, is bidding 8 TL the best choice? Second: does the highest bidder always get the top slot? The answer to both questions is “no.” Most of these notes explain why. The answer to the first question comes from the structure of the GSP auction and is shown with numbers in Section 6.4.11. The answer to the second comes from the quality score and is computed in Section 6.4.10.

To answer these questions, first recall the single-item second-price auction. In this auction (the Vickrey auction), the highest bidder wins the item but pays the second-highest bid rather than their own. Under this rule, truthful bidding, that is, bidding exactly the item’s value to the bidder, is a dominant strategy: whatever the others bid, truthful bidding never gives a worse outcome. The reason is that the bid does not determine the payment; it only determines whether you win. A bidder who bids above their value also wins in cases where the payment exceeds the value, and so incurs a loss. A bidder who bids below their value loses in cases where they could have made a profit. The GSP rule used in sponsored search tries to extend this idea to several slots, but the extension does not preserve truthfulness.

6.1.2What is an online platform?

Definition 6.1

An online platform is a digital intermediary that brings together users, sellers, and service providers. Value comes less from the platform itself than from the access the sides have to one another. Such a structure is called a two-sided market.

The second sentence of the definition is important. A bakery produces bread, and the value of the bread does not depend on how many customers the bakery has. A marketplace, by contrast, produces nothing itself. Its value to a buyer depends on how many sellers and how much variety of goods are there. Its value to a seller depends on how many buyers are there. The real service the platform sells is lowering the cost for the two groups of finding and matching with each other.

Nor is a platform merely a meeting place. It sets the rules: who may join, who sees whom, who pays what. A marketplace decides in what order sellers are listed. A search engine decides which ad goes into which slot. A ride-hailing app decides which driver goes to which passenger, and at what price. Together, all these rules form a mechanism: a rule that produces an outcome and payments from the information and choices reported by the participants.

6.1.3Two-sided markets and cross-side network effects

What separates a two-sided market from a one-sided one is that the sides add value for each other. This link is defined as follows.

Definition 6.2

Cross-side network effect: the number of participants on one side determines the value obtained by the other side. In a marketplace, many sellers mean many choices for a buyer, and many buyers mean many customers for a seller. This effect is also called an indirect network effect.

Figure 6.2 introduces the notation used in the rest of these notes. Side A is the buyers and side B is the sellers. pA and pB are the fees the two sides pay the platform. eB measures the value a buyer contributes to sellers; that is, how much side B cares about side A. eA is likewise the value a seller contributes to buyers. In this notation the subscript names the side that receives the value. This detail matters in the pricing rule.

The structure in the figure has three consequences.

  • Cross-side network effect. The number of participants on one side determines the value obtained by the other side. When the platform grows one side, it automatically draws in the other.
  • Price structure. The platform chooses not only the total price level but also how the fee is split between the sides: (pA, pB). Two marketplaces may charge the same total fee, but in one the buyer pays and in the other the seller pays. The outcomes can be very different.
  • The chicken-and-egg problem. If there are no sellers, buyers do not come; if there are no buyers, sellers do not come. A new platform starts out empty, and each side waits for the other to arrive. To break the vicious circle, one of the sides must be “drawn in” first with free use, discounts, or direct payments.

The chicken-and-egg problem gives rise to the two main questions of this chapter. First: under what conditions does a platform grow, and under what conditions does it collapse? This is the critical mass question (Section 6.2). Second: which side should be drawn in, that is, subsidized? This is the price structure question (Section 6.3).

6.1.4The platform is a mechanism, the users are players

Mechanism design is the art of choosing game rules that will produce a desired outcome. Platforms are one of the largest application areas of this art. Platform design (fee, ranking, and rating rules) steers users' strategic behavior. Users in turn choose strategies according to incentives. The behavior that emerges is the Nash equilibrium under the rules: the situation in which no user wants to change their own behavior given the behavior of the others.

This has a practical consequence: a rule should be evaluated not on the assumption that users will behave “in good faith,” but on the assumption that they will pursue their own interests. When a platform introduces an incentive, users work out how best to use it. Sometimes this calculation produces the behavior the platform wanted; sometimes it produces an unexpected exploit.

Table 6.2 puts two columns side by side. Each tool on the left is designed to reward a particular behavior. Each behavior on the right is an abuse of a tool. For example, a free trial is offered to attract new users, but one person can open many accounts and use the trial again and again. A rating and badge system is introduced to promote trustworthy sellers, but if ratings can be bought, the badge measures not trustworthiness but willingness to cheat.

The lesson of the table is this: the right incentive brings cooperation and efficiency, and the wrong incentive brings manipulation and loss of trust. The platform designer’s job is to find rules under which users, by following their own interests, reach the outcome that the platform and society want. The rest of these notes studies this job in four areas: participation (network effects), price structure, ad auctions, and reputation systems.

Common mistake

“A platform is only an intermediary; users' behavior is independent of the platform.” This is wrong. Users' behavior is an equilibrium under the rules the platform sets. The same users behave differently under a different ranking or fee rule. For example, if the average rating determines the ranking, the payoff from writing fake reviews rises; if the ranking pulls the scores of items with few reviews toward the average, that payoff falls (Section 6.5.3).

6.2Network effects and critical mass

If the value of a product rises with the number of people who use it, the question “how many people will use it?” is as important as the product itself. After the types of network effects, a simple one-parameter model explains three phenomena. There are several possible outcomes for the same platform. Expectations determine which of these outcomes is reached. A small difference in the starting point can turn into a large difference in the outcome.

6.2.1Types of network effects

Definition 6.3

If the value that using a product or platform gives a person depends on the number of other people using it, this link is called a network effect. If an additional user raises the value, the network effect is positive; if it lowers the value, it is negative.

Network effects fall into four categories.

Direct (same side)
Value comes from the number of users on the same side. The more of a person’s friends use WhatsApp, the more valuable the app is to that person. Telephones, e-mail, and messaging apps are of this type.
Indirect / cross-side
Value comes from the number of users on the other side. On Uber, many drivers mean a short wait; a short wait attracts many riders; and many riders mean more work for drivers. For a rider, what matters is not the other riders but the drivers.
Negative
Crowding sometimes lowers value: congestion, information pollution, long queues. In a two-sided market, sellers on the same side are rivals; for a seller, a new seller means that buyers will have to be shared.
Local
Value comes not from all users but from the immediate neighborhood. For a rider in Istanbul, drivers in Ankara are irrelevant. If a whole group of friends moves to another messaging app, the app’s total user count means nothing to that group. This is why competition is won locally: city by city, group by group.

A positive network effect sets up a feedback loop. A growing platform becomes more valuable, and because it is more valuable it grows even more. A shrinking platform loses value, and because it loses value it shrinks even more. In such a loop the market is expected to “tip” in one direction: either the platform reaches critical mass and grows, or it fails to reach it and collapses. Intermediate states are unstable. Making this idea precise requires a model.

6.2.2A simple adoption model

The model below is a simplified version of the network effect model of Easley and Kleinberg (2010, Chapter 17). Its aim is not realism but to show the essence of the dynamics with the fewest possible assumptions.

Definition 6.4

Adoption model. There are N = 1000 potential users. The type θi of user i is a number between 0 and 1 that indicates how much they care about the platform, and it is uniformly distributed on this interval: θi ~ U[0, 1]. If a fraction x of the population joins, the payoff user i gets from joining is

θi · x − p

Here p = 0.16 is the cost of joining (a fee or the effort of joining). A user who does not join gets a payoff of 0.

Every part of the definition has a meaning. The term θi · x is the network effect: the value of the platform rises in proportion to the fraction x of participants. If nobody is there (x = 0), the platform gives no one any value. θi is personal interest: the same crowd is worth a lot to someone who cares a great deal about the platform (θ near 1) and little to someone who cares little (θ near 0). p is a fixed cost that is the same for everyone.

The notation θ ~ U[0, 1] means “types are spread with equal probability between 0 and 1.” Its practical meaning is simple: for any threshold a, the fraction of users whose type exceeds a is 1 − a. For example, those with a type above 0.64 make up 36% of the population; among 1000 people, that is 360.

Who joins? A user joins if their payoff is nonnegative:

θi · x ≥ p ⟺ θi ≥ p / x

In the second step both sides are divided by x; since x is positive, the direction of the inequality does not change. This inequality gives the lowest type needed to join: θ* = p / x. Those whose type is above this threshold join. By the uniform distribution, their fraction is 1 − p/x.

There is a subtle but important point here. When deciding whether to join, the user does not yet know x, because x depends on everyone’s decision. The user decides according to an expectation: “I think a fraction x of the population will join.” The fraction that actually joins under this expectation is given by the function

F(x) = 1 − p/x (F(x) = 0 if x ≤ p)

The case in parentheses is explained as follows. If x ≤ p, the threshold p/x ≥ 1. Nobody has a type above 1, so nobody joins. If the expected crowd is too small, even the user who cares most about the platform sees no value that covers the cost.

Example 6.1
  1. Expectation x = 0.5. Suppose everyone thinks half the population (500 people) will join. The threshold is θ* = p/x = 0.16/0.5 = 0.32. Those with a type above 0.32 join: F(0.5) = 1 − 0.32 = 0.68, that is, 680 people.
  2. Expectation x = 0.25. Suppose everyone thinks 250 people will join. The threshold rises to 0.16/0.25 = 0.64. The fraction that joins is F(0.25) = 1 − 0.64 = 0.36, that is, 360 people.
  3. Comparison. When the expectation is halved, the threshold doubles. Because the platform’s value falls as the crowd shrinks, only those who care more about the platform join.
  4. Expectation versus realization. In both cases the realized participation differs from the expectation: 0.68 joined when 0.5 was expected, and 0.36 joined when 0.25 was expected. The gap between expectation and realization changes the expectation of the next period.

Figure 6.3 shows these two cases with bars. Each bar lies on the type axis between 0 and 1. The orange part is those who join and the blue part is those who do not. As the expectation falls, the boundary moves to the right and the orange part shrinks.

6.2.3Self-fulfilling expectations: equilibria

In both cases of Example 6.1 the expectation turned out wrong. The case in which the expectation turns out right, that is, exactly as many people join as everyone expected, is of special importance.

Definition 6.5

In the adoption model, an equilibrium is a self-fulfilling expectation: everyone expects a fraction x to join, and under this expectation a fraction x really does join. Formally, an equilibrium is a solution of the equation F(x) = x.

This definition is the counterpart of Nash equilibrium in this model. In equilibrium no user wants to change their decision: those who join have a nonnegative payoff, and those who do not join would have a negative payoff if they did. Since the expectation is correct, nobody has been wrong.

Example 6.2
  1. Set up the equation. For x > p, the condition F(x) = x becomes 1 − 0.16/x = x.
  2. Clear the fraction. Multiply both sides by x (x is positive): x − 0.16 = x2. Collecting the terms on one side gives x2 − x + 0.16 = 0.
  3. Solve the quadratic. The roots of ax2 + bx + c = 0 are x = (−b ± √(b2 − 4ac)) / 2a. Here a = 1, b = −1, c = 0.16. The expression under the root (the discriminant) is 1 − 4 · 0.16 = 1 − 0.64 = 0.36, whose square root is 0.6. The roots are (1 − 0.6)/2 = 0.2 and (1 + 0.6)/2 = 0.8.
  4. Check. F(0.2) = 1 − 0.16/0.2 = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2 ✓ and F(0.8) = 1 − 0.16/0.8 = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8 ✓.
  5. Do not forget zero. x = 0 is also an equilibrium: if it is expected that nobody will join, then x ≤ p, so F(0) = 0 and indeed nobody joins.

The model has three equilibria: x = 0 (nobody), x = 0.2 (200 users), and x = 0.8 (800 users).

Figure 6.4 shows the equilibria graphically. The horizontal axis is the expected fraction x, and the vertical axis is the fraction that actually joins, F(x). At every point on the dashed 45° line, expectation and realization are equal. The equilibria are the points where the blue curve crosses the dashed line. The curve is flat up to 0.16, then rises quickly and approaches 1 without reaching it.

There are two kinds of points in the graph. The filled dots (0 and 0.8) are stable equilibria, and the hollow dot (0.2) is an unstable equilibrium. An unstable equilibrium means this: if the expectation deviates slightly from this point, the dynamics push it away from the equilibrium.

That a platform can be both successful (800 users) and a failure (0 users) under the same conditions is the most important lesson of this model. The product, the price, and the users are the same; the only thing that decides the outcome is expectation. This is the platform version of the multiple equilibria problem in game theory: as in coordination games, everyone acts according to what the others will do, and both “everyone comes” and “nobody comes” are self-fulfilling.

6.2.4Tipping step by step

Defining an equilibrium does not say how one gets there. For that we assume a simple dynamic over time: each period, users take the participation fraction they observed in the previous period as this period’s expectation. Those who join this period in turn determine the next period’s expectation:

xt+1 = F(xt)

Here xt is the expected fraction in period t, and xt+1 is the fraction that results from that expectation and becomes the expectation of the next period. The dynamics start from an initial expectation x0.

Example 6.3

Starting above the threshold: x0 = 0.25. Suppose the platform has created the impression that 250 users are expected at the start.

  1. t = 0 → 1: threshold θ ≥ 0.16/0.25 = 0.64; fraction joining x1 = 1 − 0.64 = 0.36.
  2. t = 1 → 2: participation exceeded the expectation (0.36 > 0.25). With the new expectation 0.36 the threshold is 0.16/0.36 ≈ 0.444; x2 = 1 − 0.444 = 0.556.
  3. t = 2 → 3: the feedback continues: x3 = 1 − 0.16/0.556 = 0.712.
  4. t = 3 → 4: x4 = 1 − 0.16/0.712 = 0.775. The increments begin to shrink: 0.11, 0.20, 0.16, 0.06.
  5. t = 4 → 6: x5 = 0.794, x6 ≈ 0.798. The path approaches the stable equilibrium 0.8. The market has tipped in the platform’s favor: about 800 users.

Starting below the threshold: x0 = 0.19. Suppose the initial expectation is only slightly below the critical threshold.

  1. x1 = 1 − 0.16/0.19 = 1 − 0.842 = 0.158. Participation fell short of the expectation.
  2. Since x1 = 0.158 ≤ p = 0.16, we get x2 = 0: nobody joins and the platform collapses.

Figure 6.5 shows one way to follow the dynamics on the graph. This drawing is called a cobweb plot and is read as follows. Start at xt on the horizontal axis. Go vertically up to the curve; the height of the curve is xt+1 = F(xt). Then go horizontally to the 45° line, which “carries” the new value over to the horizontal axis. From there go vertically to the curve again, and so on. If the curve is above the line, the steps move right; if it is below, they move left.

The consequence of this rule is clear in the graph. Between 0.2 and 0.8 the curve lies above the line: in every period participation exceeds the expectation and the fraction grows. To the right of 0.8 the curve is below the line: the fraction shrinks and returns to 0.8. To the left of 0.2 the curve is also below the line: the fraction shrinks and falls to 0. So 0.8 and 0 attract paths from both sides, while 0.2 pushes them away in both directions.

Table 6.3 gives a striking comparison. The difference between the two starting points is only 0.06 (60 users). Yet one goes to 800 users and the other to zero. This is the mathematical reason for the “all or nothing” behavior of platforms.

Definition 6.6

The unstable equilibrium of the adoption model is called the critical mass. If the initial expectation is above the critical mass, the dynamics go to the large stable equilibrium; if it is below, they go to zero. The market’s spontaneous movement to one of these two extremes is called tipping.

In this example the critical mass is 0.2, that is, 200 users. If the expectation is above it, the market goes to 0.8; if below, to 0.

6.2.5Which equilibrium is stable?

Stability is easy to see in the graph, and the same conclusion can be reached by computation. Consider a small deviation near an equilibrium: xt = x* + d. One period later the deviation is approximately F′(x*) · d. Here F′(x*) is the slope of the curve at the equilibrium point. If the slope has absolute value less than 1, the deviation shrinks every period and the equilibrium is stable. If it is greater than 1, the deviation grows and the equilibrium is unstable.

For F(x) = 1 − p/x the slope is F′(x) = p/x2. This follows from writing the term p/x as p · x−1, whose derivative is −p · x−2, together with the minus sign in front.

  • x* = 0.8: F′(0.8) = 0.16/0.64 = 0.25 < 1. The deviation falls to a quarter each period: stable. For example, starting from x = 0.81 gives F(0.81) ≈ 0.802; the deviation of 0.01 has fallen to 0.002.
  • x* = 0.2: F′(0.2) = 0.16/0.04 = 4 > 1. The deviation roughly quadruples each period: unstable. For example, starting from x = 0.21 gives F(0.21) ≈ 0.238; the deviation of 0.01 has grown to about 0.038.
  • x* = 0: immediately around 0, F is zero (the curve is flat); the slope is 0. A small expectation returns to zero at once: stable.

This computation explains why the filled and hollow dots in Figure 6.4 are drawn as they are. If the curve crosses the 45° line steeply (slope greater than 1), the equilibrium is unstable; if it crosses shallowly (slope less than 1), it is stable.

p = 0.21. The equation is x2 − x + 0.21 = 0. The discriminant is 1 − 0.84 = 0.16, with square root 0.4. The roots are (1 − 0.4)/2 = 0.3 and (1 + 0.4)/2 = 0.7. Check: F(0.3) = 1 − 0.21/0.3 = 1 − 0.7 = 0.3 ✓, F(0.7) = 1 − 0.21/0.7 = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7 ✓.

On both sides of 0.5 the curve is below the line. So starting below 0.5 the path goes to 0. Starting above, participation decreases and approaches 0.5. This equilibrium is stable from one direction only: a small drop makes the market collapse.

p = 0.3. The discriminant is 1 − 1.2 = −0.2 < 0: there is no real root. The curve is always below the 45° line; whatever expectation one starts from, participation shrinks and goes to 0. The market never forms.

Table 6.4 and Example 6.4 show two general results. First: as the fee falls, the threshold falls and the number of users at the large equilibrium rises. When p drops from 0.21 to 0.09, the critical mass falls from 300 to 100 users. Second: for the equation x2 − x + p = 0 to have a real root, the discriminant must satisfy 1 − 4p ≥ 0, that is, p ≤ 0.25. If p > 0.25, the market never forms. The critical mass and the large equilibrium always add up to 1 (0.2 + 0.8, 0.3 + 0.7), because in a quadratic the sum of the roots is −b/a = 1. As the threshold rises, the large equilibrium falls by the same amount.

These results explain why platforms behave unusually at the start. There are four common strategies:

Initial subsidy
A free period or a bonus reduces p and lowers the critical mass. Once the crowd has formed, the platform can raise the fee, because the large equilibrium is stable and a small fee increase does not destroy it.
Seeding
The platform itself supplies the first content, sellers, or drivers. The actual participation x is then greater than zero before users' participation even begins.
Expectation management
The perception that “everyone is here” moves the initial expectation x0 above the threshold. The model shows that the expectation itself is an input; advertising and promotion change this input.
Standalone value
One starts with a tool that is useful even without the network and builds the network later. For example, an app that began as a photo-editing tool attracts users even without a network effect; when a sharing feature is added, the crowd is ready. In the model this means adding a term to the payoff that does not depend on x; then x = 0 is no longer an equilibrium.

History shows both directions. WhatsApp’s value grew exponentially as users increased. Friendster, on the other hand, collapsed in the opposite direction through the same feedback loop once it began losing users. The mechanism at work is the same in both cases; the difference is which side of the threshold the expectation was on.

6.2.7Common mistakes with network effects

Common mistake

Forgetting the expectation. Writing 1 − p when computing F(x), that is, forgetting that participation depends on x. For p = 0.16 and x = 0.4 the correct answer is F(0.4) = 1 − 0.16/0.4 = 1 − 0.4 = 0.6, not 0.84.

Confusing the threshold type with the fraction who join. In the same example, 0.4 is the type at the joining threshold (θ* = p/x), not the fraction who join. The fraction who join is the part above the threshold: 1 − θ*.

Skipping the zero equilibrium. The equation x2 − x + p = 0 gives only the positive equilibria. Because the equation was multiplied by x when it was set up, x = 0 must be checked separately; the situation in which nobody joins is always an equilibrium.

Mistaking the unstable equilibrium for a “middle way.” Although 0.2 is an equilibrium, the market does not stay there. Since real expectations are never exactly 0.2, 0.2 is not a target but a boundary.

6.3Platform pricing

In a one-sided market the pricing question is simple: at what price should the product be sold? On a two-sided platform the question splits in two: how much should be charged in total, and how should that total be divided between the sides? The answer to the second question is found with a numerical model and is surprising at first glance: a profit-maximizing platform pays one of the sides.

6.3.1Principles of two-sided pricing

According to Rochet and Tirole (2003), besides the overall price level a platform also decides the price structure: which side pays the fee, and how much? For a one-sided firm there is no such thing as a “price structure.” In a two-sided market, however, the structure matters as much as the overall level, because one side’s fee changes not only that side’s participation but also, through the cross-side network effect, the other side’s participation.

The basic principle can be summarized in two points:

  • The side that adds more value to the other side, or that is more price-sensitive, is charged less, or even subsidized. A subsidy means a negative price: the platform pays every participant on that side.
  • This loss is covered by the fee collected from the other side, which the subsidized side draws to the platform.

There are many everyday examples of this principle:

Search and social media
Users use the service for free and advertisers pay. Users' attention is valuable to advertisers; charging users would reduce that attention.
Credit cards
Cardholders receive points and miles, and merchants pay a commission. A cardholder’s use of the card is valuable to the merchant; points increase that use.
Marketplaces
Buyers get coupons and sellers pay a commission.
Revenue models
Commission, membership, advertising, per-transaction fees. These models determine which side bears the fee and in what form.

Armstrong (2006) reduces this principle to a single rule in a simple linear model:

pA = markup − the value an A participant adds to side B

According to the rule, if the second term is large, the price falls below zero. The markup is the difference between the price the platform would charge a participant, and the cost, if there were no network effect. The logic of the rule is this: when the platform attracts a side-A participant, it gains two things. The first is the fee collected from that participant. The second is the value that participant adds to side B, because side B is willing to pay more for that value. If the second gain is large, the platform is willing to pay an A participant to attract them. The rule is derived in general form in Section 6.3.5.

6.3.2A numerical example: the marketplace model

Consider a marketplace. Let n denote the number of participants (in thousands) and p the monthly fee (in TL). Serving one more participant costs the platform nothing (marginal cost 0). For a seller, buyers are very valuable (eB = 0.75); for a buyer, seller variety matters less (eA = 0.25). Participation is determined by these two equations:

nA = 20 + 0.25 · nB − pA
nB = 80 + 0.75 · nA − pB

The platform’s profit is the sum of the fees collected from the two sides:

Π = pA · nA + pB · nB

Reading the equations one at a time reveals the assumptions of the model. The 20 in the buyer equation is the number of buyers (in thousands) who would come even with a zero fee and no sellers at all. The term 0.25 · nB is the additional buyers drawn by sellers: 250 buyers per thousand sellers. The term −pA is the buyers lost to the fee: every 1 TL increase in the fee costs a thousand buyers. The seller equation has the same form, but its constant (80) and network effect coefficient (0.75) are larger. The importance sellers attach to buyers is three times the importance buyers attach to sellers.

The platform’s question is: what are the profit-maximizing (pA, pB), and which side is subsidized?

6.3.3The profit-maximizing prices step by step

Computing directly in terms of prices is hard, because each price changes both participation levels and the levels depend on each other. There is a way to make the computation easier: make the participation levels, rather than the prices, the decision variables. Each pair (nA, nB) corresponds to a single pair of prices. The platform can think “I want this many buyers and this many sellers” and read the prices that deliver those numbers from the equations.

The solution uses partial derivatives. The partial derivative of a function of two variables with respect to one variable is the derivative taken while the other variable is held constant; it is written with ∂. For example, the partial derivative of nA nB with respect to nA is nB, because nB behaves like a constant. At the peak of a function of two variables both partial derivatives are zero: at the top, the slope is zero whichever direction one steps in.

Example 6.5
  1. Make the participation levels the decision variables. Solve the participation equations for the prices: pA = 20 + 0.25 nB − nA and pB = 80 + 0.75 nA − nB.
  2. Substitute into the profit. pA nA = 20 nA + 0.25 nAnB − nA2 and pB nB = 80 nB + 0.75 nAnB − nB2. Adding them gives

    Π = 20 nA + 80 nB + (0.25 + 0.75) nAnB − nA2 − nB2

    The coefficient of nAnB is the sum of the two cross effects (here 1). This term shows that the platform can collect the value the two sides add for each other.
  3. Set the partial derivatives to zero. With nB held fixed, ∂Π/∂nA = 20 + nB − 2nA = 0. With nA held fixed, ∂Π/∂nB = 80 + nA − 2nB = 0. This gives two linear equations.
  4. Solve the equations. From the first, nA = (20 + nB)/2. Substituting into the second gives 80 + (20 + nB)/2 − 2nB = 0. Multiplying both sides by 2: 160 + 20 + nB − 4nB = 0, that is, 3nB = 180 and nB = 60. Substituting back, nA = 80/2 = 40.
  5. Read off the prices. pA = 20 + 0.25 · 60 − 40 = 20 + 15 − 40 = −5 and pB = 80 + 0.75 · 40 − 60 = 80 + 30 − 60 = 50.
  6. Compute the profit. Π = (−5) · 40 + 50 · 60 = −200 + 3000 = 2800.
  7. Is it a peak? In the profit, the terms −nA2 and −nB2 give the function a “dome” shape; the coefficient of the cross term (1) is not large enough to spoil this dome (the condition: the coefficient must be less than 2). The point found is therefore a peak.

Solution: buyers are paid 5 TL per month (pA = −5), and sellers pay 50 TL. The platform has 40 thousand buyers and 60 thousand sellers; the profit is 2800 thousand TL.

The interpretation of the result is the main idea of the chapter. The platform pays buyers but does not lose money. The 200 paid to buyers is small next to the 3000 collected from sellers. Sellers are willing to pay 50 TL because the platform has 40 thousand buyers, and every buyer adds value for sellers.

  • The subsidy is profitable. As buyers are charged, profit falls: 2800 → 2776.9 → 2592.3. If buyers are charged 10 TL, the number of buyers falls to 23.8 thousand, and sellers decline too. The 238 thousand TL collected from buyers does not make up for the loss on the seller side.
  • Why? An extra buyer adds 0.75 · 60 = 45 TL of value for sellers, and the platform recovers this through pB. The buyer’s markup is 40. Since 45 > 40, the buyer is subsidized: 40 − 45 = −5.
  • Counterfactual. If the cross effects were reversed, that is, if buyers added little value for sellers (eA = 0.75, eB = 0.25), the subsidy would disappear. The profit expression does not change (the cross term is still 0.75 + 0.25 = 1), so participation is still 40 and 60. But the prices change: pA = 20 + 0.75 · 60 − 40 = 25, pB = 80 + 0.25 · 40 − 60 = 30. This time buyers are willing to pay, because 60 thousand sellers add a great deal of value for them.

The last row shows a subtlety. Profit and participation depend only on the sum of the cross effects. The price structure, however, depends on the distribution of the effects. The total value the platform collects does not change; what changes is which side it is collected from. The script in the presentation scans the grid of (pA, pB) in steps of 0.5 TL and finds the highest profit at the point (−5, 50). This is a numerical check of the solution found with derivatives.

6.3.5The general rule: markup equals the value a participant brings

The prices in the numerical example obey the rule pA = nA − eB · nB = 40 − 0.75 · 60 = −5. This is no coincidence. The rule holds in every linear model of the same form.

Theorem 6.1 (Pricing rule in the linear model)

Let participation be given by nA = a + eA nB − pA and nB = b + eB nA − pB, with marginal cost 0 and eA + eB < 2. The profit-maximizing prices are:

pA = nA − eB · nB, pB = nB − eA · nA

Here nA is the markup on side A, and eB · nB is the total value an A participant adds to side B.

The steps of the example are repeated with general numbers. The prices are written in terms of participation as pA = a + eA nB − nA and pB = b + eB nA − nB. Substituting into the profit expression gives Π = a nA + b nB + (eA + eB) nAnB − nA2 − nB2.

The partial derivative with respect to nA is set to zero: a + (eA + eB) nB − 2nA = 0. This gives a = 2nA − (eA + eB) nB.

Substitute this into the formula for pA: pA = 2nA − (eA + eB) nB + eA nB − nA. The eA nB terms cancel, leaving pA = nA − eB nB. The computation for side B is symmetric.

Finally, we show that the point found is a peak. The second derivatives of the profit are −2 and −2, and the cross derivative is e = eA + eB. For a function of two variables, if (−2)(−2) − e2 > 0 and the diagonal terms are negative, the stationary point is a peak. This condition is equivalent to e < 2, which is the assumption of the theorem. □

The reason the rule contains a “markup” term is as follows. Without network effects (eA = eB = 0), side A would reduce to a one-sided monopoly problem: with demand nA = a − pA the profit is pA(a − pA), and the best price is a/2; at this price nA = a/2. That is, with linear demand the monopoly price equals the number of participants. The term nA in the rule is this monopoly price. The second term is the correction for the network effect.

Example 6.7

Theorem 6.1 is applied to the scenarios in the table.

  1. Optimum (eA = 0.25, eB = 0.75). pA = 40 − 0.75 · 60 = −5 ✓; pB = 60 − 0.25 · 40 = 50 ✓.
  2. Effects reversed. pA = 40 − 0.25 · 60 = 25 ✓; pB = 60 − 0.75 · 40 = 30 ✓.
  3. Equal effects (eA = eB = 0.5). Since the sum is still 1, participation is 40 and 60. pA = 40 − 0.5 · 60 = 10, pB = 60 − 0.5 · 40 = 40. The profit is 400 + 2400 = 2800. In this case there is no subsidy: the value a buyer adds for sellers (30) is smaller than the markup (40).

The rule answers the “Why?” question in Table 6.5 in one line. The criterion for a subsidy is not that a side is crowded, that it brings in revenue, or that it consists of sellers. The criterion is whether the value one participant on that side adds to the other side exceeds the markup.

6.3.6Code trace: participation()

In Example 6.5 the participation levels were found as the joint solution of two equations. In reality users do not solve a system of equations; each side reacts to the other’s current size. The short function below imitates this process: the prices are fixed, and the sides react to each other in turn.

def participation(pA, pB, rounds=8):
  nA, nB = 0, 0
  for t in range(rounds):
    nA = 20 + 0.25 * nB - pA   # buyers
    nB = 80 + 0.75 * nA - pB   # sellers
  return nA, nB

The logic of the code is as follows:

  • Initially the platform is empty: nA, nB = 0, 0.
  • In each round the buyers react first, to the current number of sellers: nA = 20 + 0.25 * nB - pA.
  • Then the sellers react to the new number of buyers: nB = 80 + 0.75 * nA - pB. The second line uses the nA that the first line updated in the same round.
  • After the loop has repeated rounds times, the final participation levels are returned. The default of 8 rounds is enough for this example.

The function is called with the optimal prices, as participation(-5, 50). Since pA = -5, the - pA term in the buyer equation becomes +5.

First: in the first round the buyer subsidy draws in sellers. 25 thousand buyers bring 48.75 thousand sellers to the platform even though the fee is 50 TL. Second: the cross effect grows the two sides together. In each round the increase in buyers increases sellers, and the increase in sellers increases buyers. Why does it converge? The deviation from the fixed point shrinks by a fixed factor every round. The distance of the number of sellers from 60 in the first round is 11.25 (60 − 48.75). This distance is multiplied by eA = 0.25 when passing through the buyer equation and by eB = 0.75 when passing through the seller equation. In one round the deviation falls by a factor of eA · eB = 0.25 · 0.75 = 0.1875: 11.25 → 2.11 → 0.40 → 0.07. After five rounds the difference is invisible at this rounding.

The convergence condition is the product of the cross effects: eA · eB < 1. If the product exceeded 1, the deviation would grow each round, and participation would explode or collapse. This is the case in which the network effect is too strong and the tipping behavior of Section 6.2.4 appears. The fixed point itself still exists but is now unstable, like the threshold 0.2 in the adoption model.

Common mistake

Using the old nA value in the second line of the trace. The code runs in order: when nB is computed in round 1, nA is already 25. The correct value is 80 + 0.75 · 25 − 50 = 48.75. The answer 30 (= 80 − 50) forgets the contribution of the buyers, and the answer 18.75 forgets the constant term and the fee. It should also be remembered that the prices do not change during the loop: convergence is guaranteed not by a price update but by the condition eA · eB < 1.

6.3.7The model in the game

The Two-Sided Platform Pricing game uses the same model with two additional parameters:

nA = baseA − sA · pA + eA · nB
nB = baseB − sB · pB + eB · nA

  • base: the number of users who join when the fee is 0 and the other side is empty.
  • s: the price sensitivity, that is, how many users a 1-unit increase in the fee loses. In the marketplace scenario sA = 11 and sB = 5; buyers are much more price-sensitive. In the numerical model s = 1 was used.
  • Participation is limited to 0–100 users; fees are integers between −5 and 10.
  • The profit is Π = pA · nA + pB · nB, and participation is again the fixed point of mutual reaction (the game’s code computes it with a loop like participation()).

6.3.8Common mistakes in pricing

Common mistake

“The crowded side is charged less.” Crowding is not the criterion for a subsidy. In the numerical example sellers (60 thousand) outnumber buyers (40 thousand), but it is the buyers who are subsidized. What matters is the value a participant adds to the other side.

“A subsidy is a loss.” A subsidy is an investment. Looking at one side alone, the platform loses 200 on buyers; but these buyers make it possible for sellers to pay 50 TL. Profit must be computed as a whole.

Confusing the markup with the network effect. In the rule, nA is A’s markup and eB nB is the value A adds to B. The term eA (the value B adds to A) does not appear in the formula for pA; it is in the formula for pB. The subscript indicates the side that receives the value.

6.4Sponsored search

Search ads are among the largest sources of revenue for platforms and are sold through an auction held billions of times a day. When several slots are sold at once, the choice of payment rule matters: GSP and VCG bring in different revenue for the same bids, and in GSP truthful bidding is not an equilibrium. In real systems, quality scores and budget constraints also come into play.

6.4.1The sponsored search model

For every search query, an auction is held among the advertisers who have bid on that keyword. The items sold are ad slots. The slots are not identical: the top slot is clicked more. The auction sells not a single item but several ranked items at once.

Definition 6.7

Sponsored search auction. There are m slots, and the click counts from top to bottom are α1 > α2 > … > αm. Advertiser i has a value per click of vi. The advertiser bids bi per click; if placed in slot j they pay pj per click. The utility of the advertiser in slot j is

αj · (vi − pj)

An advertiser who gets no slot has a utility of 0.

The utility formula has two factors. αj is the number of clicks the advertiser receives. vi − pj is the profit left over from each click: the value of the click minus the payment per click. A higher slot brings more clicks but is usually more expensive. The advertiser’s question is “more clicks, or cheaper clicks?” This tension is the source of strategic behavior in GSP.

All the numerical examples in this section use the same data: three slots, with 300, 200, and 100 clicks per thousand impressions; four advertisers, with values per click of 10 TL for A, 8 for B, 5 for C, and 2 for D. Initially everyone is assumed to bid their value: b = v.

6.4.2Two payment rules: GSP and VCG

Both rules rank the ads by bid; the highest bid goes to the top slot. The difference is only in the payment. In the notation, b(k) denotes the kth highest bid: b(1) ≥ b(2) ≥ ….

Definition 6.8

Generalized Second Price (GSP) auction. The ads are ranked by bid. The advertiser in slot j pays the bid of the one below them per click:

pj = b(j+1)

GSP looks like the natural generalization of the single-item second-price auction. With a single slot (m = 1) it is indeed identical to the Vickrey auction: the winner pays the second-highest bid. With several slots, however, truthfulness is lost. The reason is shown in Section 6.4.5.

Definition 6.9

VCG auction. The ranking is the same. The advertiser pays the loss their presence imposes on the others. The total payment of the advertiser in slot j is

αj · pj = Σk ≥ j (αk − αk+1) · b(k+1)

where αm+1 = 0. Here αj · pj is the total payment and pj is the payment per click. In VCG, truthful bidding is a dominant strategy.

The VCG rule is an application of a general principle. A participant’s payment is the cost that their presence imposes on the other participants:

payment = (others' welfare without them) − (others' welfare with them)

This cost is called the externality. A participant who pays the externality maximizes their own utility and total welfare at the same time. Misreporting their value cannot gain them anything. The truthfulness of VCG comes from this alignment.

The sum in the formula is the form this principle takes in sponsored search. What would happen without the advertiser in slot j? Those above would be unaffected and would stay where they are, because they already bid higher. Each of those below would move up one slot. The advertiser in slot k + 1 would move up to slot k and gain αk − αk+1 extra clicks. Each click is worth b(k+1) to them (equal to their value if they bid truthfully). The total is the sum of these losses from k = j to the end. That is, in VCG the advertiser in slot j pays the sum of the click values that those below them lose by being pushed down one slot each.

Figure 6.9 shows the two rules side by side. In the ladder on the left, each arrow goes from the bid below to the payment of the slot above. The bar on the right shows that A’s total VCG payment consists of three parts. Each part is the click value that an advertiser below A loses because of A.

6.4.3GSP and VCG payments for the same bids

With bids equal to values (bid = value), the payments under the two rules are computed step by step. The ranking is by bid: A goes to slot 1, B to slot 2, and C to slot 3; D is left out.

Example 6.8

GSP. Each slot pays the bid of the one below per click.

  1. Slot 1: A pays the bid of B below: 8 TL per click × 300 clicks = 2400.
  2. Slot 2: B pays C’s bid: 5 × 200 = 1000.
  3. Slot 3: C pays D’s bid: 2 × 100 = 200. GSP revenue is 2400 + 1000 + 200 = 3600.

VCG. Payment = others' welfare “without them” − others' welfare “with them.” Starting from the bottom makes the computation easier.

  1. C (slot 3). Without C, D would move up to slot 3: the others' welfare (only those below) is 100 · 2 = 200. With C present, D is out: 0. C pays 200 − 0 = 200, which is 200/100 = 2 per click.
  2. B (slot 2). Without B, C would move up to slot 2 (200 · 5 = 1000) and D to slot 3 (100 · 2 = 200): a total of 1200. With B present, C is in slot 3: 100 · 5 = 500, and D is out. B pays 1200 − 500 = 700, which is 3.5 per click.
  3. A (slot 1). Without A, B, C, and D would each move up a slot: 300 · 8 + 200 · 5 + 100 · 2 = 2400 + 1000 + 200 = 3600. With A present, it is 200 · 8 + 100 · 5 = 1600 + 500 = 2100. A pays 3600 − 2100 = 1500, which is 5 per click.
  4. Check with the formula. (300 − 200) · 8 + (200 − 100) · 5 + (100 − 0) · 2 = 800 + 500 + 200 = 1500 ✓. VCG revenue is 1500 + 700 + 200 = 2400.

The welfare of the advertisers above never changes in any of these computations. Only those below are counted, because an advertiser’s departure affects only those below them.

6.4.4Code trace: gsp_vcg()

Both payment rules are computed by a single short function. The function takes the list of bids (bids) and the slot click counts (ctr), and returns the total GSP and VCG payments for each slot.

def gsp_vcg(bids, ctr):
  m = len(ctr)
  b = sorted(bids, reverse=True) + [0]*m
  a = ctr + [0]     # dummy slot: 0 clicks
  gsp, vcg = [0]*m, [0]*(m + 1)
  for j in range(m):
    gsp[j] = a[j] * b[j+1]   # next bid
  for j in range(m-1, -1, -1):  # bottom up
    extra = (a[j] - a[j+1]) * b[j+1]
    vcg[j] = vcg[j+1] + extra
  return gsp, vcg[:m]

The logic of the code, line by line, is as follows:

  • m = len(ctr): the number of slots. 3 in the example.
  • b = sorted(bids, reverse=True) + [0]*m: the bids are sorted from largest to smallest, and m zeros are appended. This way a “next bid” exists for every slot; even if there are fewer advertisers than slots, b[j+1] does not run past the end of the list.
  • a = ctr + [0]: a dummy (m+1)th slot with 0 clicks is added. This is the assumption αm+1 = 0 in the formula.
  • gsp, vcg = [0]*m, [0]*(m + 1): the result lists are zeroed. The extra last element (0) of vcg is the starting value of the recursion.
  • The first loop: gsp[j] = a[j] * b[j+1]. The click count of slot j times the bid below.
  • The second loop goes from the bottom up: extra = (a[j] - a[j+1]) * b[j+1] is the click value lost by the advertiser one slot below, who stays one slot lower instead of taking this one. vcg[j] = vcg[j+1] + extra: the payment of slot j is the payment of the slot below plus this extra loss. The losses imposed on those below are thus carried upward.
  • return gsp, vcg[:m]: the results are returned with the dummy slot’s zero dropped.

The second loop does not recompute the sum in the VCG formula from scratch for each slot. The total for slot j is found by adding a single term to the total for slot j + 1. Both loops therefore finish in O(m) steps; together with sorting, the total work is O(n log n) (where n is the number of advertisers).

6.4.5Truthful bidding is not an equilibrium of GSP

For a bid profile to be a Nash equilibrium, no advertiser may be able to raise their utility by changing their bid while the others are held fixed. The truthful profile fails this test. To see this, it is enough to look at A’s options.

Example 6.9
  1. Utilities in the truthful profile. Everyone bids their value. A is in slot 1 and pays B’s bid (8): 300 · (10 − 8) = 600. B is in slot 2 and pays 5: 200 · (8 − 5) = 600. C is in slot 3 and pays 2: 100 · (5 − 2) = 300. D is out: 0. Platform revenue is 3600.
  2. A’s utility as a function of the bid. With the others (8, 5, 2) fixed, A’s bid only determines which slot A lands in. Below 2, A is left out: 0. Between 2 and 5, A lands in slot 3 and pays D’s 2: 100 · (10 − 2) = 800. Between 5 and 8, A lands in slot 2 and pays C’s 5: 200 · (10 − 5) = 1000. Above 8, A stays in slot 1 and pays 8: 600.
  3. Best response. The largest utility is 1000, obtained with a bid between 5 and 8. For example, if A bids 6, A drops to slot 2 and pays C’s 5: 200 · (10 − 5) = 1000 > 600.
  4. Conclusion. Truthful bidding is not a best response for A. The truthful profile is not a Nash equilibrium.

Figure 6.11 plots A’s utility function. The function is a step function because A’s payment depends not on A’s own bid but on the bid below. When the bid changes within a step, neither the slot nor the payment changes. The steps change only when the bid passes one of the other bids (2, 5, 8). The truthful bid falls on the rightmost step, but this step is not the highest.

Why does this happen? Slot 1 brings only 100 more clicks than slot 2 (300 versus 200). For these extra clicks, A’s payment per click rises from 5 to 8. Moreover, this higher price is paid not only for the extra 100 clicks but for all 300 clicks. The marginal cost of moving up exceeds the marginal benefit. A gets fewer clicks but pays less per click: 1000 > 600.

This observation also explains why the single-item Vickrey auction is truthful. With a single item, the loser’s utility is 0; there is no “fewer but cheaper” option. With several slots, every lower slot is such an option. In GSP an advertiser can lower their bid and so change the payment rule itself (which bid they will be below). In VCG, the payment equals the loss imposed on the others, whichever slot the advertiser is in. Lowering the bid only changes the slot, and this change never raises total welfare, and therefore never raises A’s utility.

6.4.6Code trace: profitable_dev()

Checking whether a profile is an equilibrium means searching for a profitable deviation for each advertiser. The function below performs this search for one advertiser.

def profitable_dev(i, b, v, ctr, cands):
  old = gsp_utility(i, b, v, ctr)
  for x in cands:
    b2 = b[:]; b2[i] = x   # only i changes
    u = gsp_utility(i, b2, v, ctr)
    if u > old:
      return x, u   # profitable
  return None     # no deviation

The function uses a helper gsp_utility(i, b, v, ctr). This helper sorts the bids; if i is in slot j it returns ctr[j] · (v[i] − the bid below), and if i is out of the slots it returns 0. The bids are assumed to be distinct; how to rank equal bids requires a separate tie-breaking rule.

profitable_dev works as follows:

  • old: i's utility with the current bids.
  • For each candidate bid x, the bid list is copied (b2 = b[:]) and only i's bid is changed. Copying matters: if the original list were modified, later candidates would be tested against the wrong profile.
  • u is i's utility in the new profile. If u > old, a profitable deviation has been found, and (x, u) is returned.
  • If no candidate is profitable, None is returned.

The function stops at the first profitable deviation; it finds a profitable deviation, not the best one. For an equilibrium check this is enough, because a single profitable deviation suffices to show that the profile is not an equilibrium. By contrast, a result of None is valid only for the candidates tried. To say that a profile is an equilibrium, the candidates must cover all the meaningful bids. With a step utility function, one candidate from each step suffices: the candidates in the example (9, 6, 4, 3, 1) place at least one bid on each step of the truthful profile and of the equilibrium.

Definition 6.10

If a GSP bid profile is a Nash equilibrium and no advertiser prefers taking the slot above at that slot’s current price per click to their own slot, the profile is called a locally envy-free equilibrium. For the advertiser in slot j the condition is:

αj · (v − pj) ≥ αj−1 · (v − pj−1)

The condition must hold for every slot (and, for the first advertiser without a slot, against the lowest slot). A profile in which no advertiser envies any slot is called envy-free.

The meaning of the condition is this. Nash equilibrium looks only at deviations that can be made alone. For B to move up to slot 1, B would have to exceed A’s bid (10) and pay 10; this is unprofitable. But in B’s eyes the “current price” of slot 1 is 5 (what A pays). Envy-freeness requires that nobody even think, “I would like to be in A’s place at the price A pays.” This is a stronger condition, and it describes equilibria that survive in repeated bidding environments: if someone envies the one above, they can raise their own bid and so increase the payment of the one above; this pressure breaks the equilibrium.

Example 6.11
  1. B (slot 2). In place, B gets 200 · (8 − 3.5) = 900. If B took slot 1 at its current price of 5, B would get 300 · (8 − 5) = 900. There is no gain; B does not envy.
  2. C (slot 3). In place, 100 · (5 − 2) = 300. If C took slot 2 at 3.5, 200 · (5 − 3.5) = 300. C does not envy.
  3. D (out). In place, 0. If D took slot 3 at 2, 100 · (2 − 2) = 0. D does not envy.

Every advertiser is exactly indifferent toward the slot above. The envy-freeness condition holds with equality. This is the distinguishing feature of the lowest-revenue envy-free equilibrium: if the prices were a penny lower, someone would envy the one above.

Theorem 6.2 (Edelman–Ostrovsky–Schwarz, Varian)

In the model of this chapter (complete information, slot click-through rates independent of the advertiser, constant values per click, and more advertisers than slots), the GSP auction always has a locally envy-free equilibrium. In every locally envy-free equilibrium, GSP revenue is at least VCG revenue. In the one of these equilibria with the lowest revenue, the slots are allocated as in VCG and every advertiser pays the VCG price per click.

The proof of the theorem is outside the scope of these notes (Edelman, Ostrovsky, and Schwarz, 2007; Varian, 2007). But how the lowest equilibrium is constructed can be read off the example: the bid of the advertiser in slot j + 1 is set equal to the VCG price per click of slot j. B’s bid is the VCG price of slot 1, which is 5; C’s is the VCG price of slot 2, which is 3.5; D’s is the VCG price of slot 3, which is 2. Since in GSP everyone pays the bid of the one below, the payments equal the VCG payments.

As a result, in these equilibria GSP revenue is at least 2400, and the lowest equals VCG. The 3600 found with truthful bids is not the revenue at an equilibrium. GSP’s advantage of “more revenue with the same bids” largely disappears once the bids change.

6.4.9Budgets and online allocation

Real advertisers have daily budgets. Moreover, searches arrive one at a time, and the platform must immediately give each one to an advertiser without knowing which searches will come in the future. This kind of decision is called an online decision: the input arrives piece by piece, and each piece is processed irrevocably the moment it arrives.

Example 6.12

A and B each have a budget of 100 TL. For “running shoes” searches both bid 1 TL; for “sports bag” searches only A bids 1 TL. First 100 shoe searches arrive, then 100 bag searches. The platform gives each search to an advertiser whose budget suffices and earns the amount of the bid.

  1. The greedy rule (highest bid, ties to A). In the shoe searches the two bids are equal; all 100 go to A. A’s budget runs out. In the bag searches only A had bid, but no money is left: 0. Revenue is 100.
  2. The Balance rule: the search goes to the advertiser with the larger remaining budget. The shoe searches are split alternately between A and B: A 50, B 50. In the bag searches A’s remaining 50 TL is used: 50. Revenue is 100 + 50 = 150.
  3. The optimum with knowledge of the future. If the bag searches had been known in advance, all 100 shoe searches would go to B and all 100 bag searches to A. Revenue is 200.

But when budgets exist, the greedy rule exhausts the flexible advertiser (A, who bids on several searches) early. Then, when the searches that only A values arrive, nobody can take them.

The success of online algorithms is measured by the competitive ratio: the ratio of the algorithm’s revenue to the optimum with knowledge of the future, on the worst search sequence. The greedy rule guarantees only 1/2 of the optimum in the worst case; this example is exactly that worst case (100/200). When bids are small relative to budgets, the MSVV rule, which weights each bid by the fraction of the budget remaining, obtains at least 1 − 1/e ≈ 63% of the optimum whatever the order of searches (Mehta, Saberi, Vazirani, and Vazirani, 2007). In the example the Balance rule obtained 75%; the guarantee is 63%, but on some sequences better is achieved. Here e ≈ 2.718 is the base of the natural logarithm.

Common mistake

“Choosing the highest bid on every search is the best.” This is true if there are no budgets. With budgets, giving one search to someone consumes money that this advertiser could have used for future searches. In online allocation every decision also affects the future; rules that take the remaining budget into account therefore give better guarantees.

6.4.10GSP with quality scores

Up to here, every ad was assumed to receive the same number of clicks in a slot. In reality some ads are more engaging than others. Because a platform’s revenue comes from clicks, putting an ad at the top that bids high but that nobody clicks is bad for the platform. The quality score solves this problem.

Definition 6.11

GSP with quality scores: the quality qi of ad i is its click propensity. In slot j, the number of clicks for i is αj · qi. The platform ranks ads by the score bi · qi. The advertiser in slot j pays per click the lowest bid needed to keep their position:

pj = scorej+1 / qi = b(j+1) q(j+1) / qi

The meaning of the score is simple: b · q measures the expected revenue an impression will bring the platform. The bid is the payment per click, and quality is proportional to the click probability; their product is the expected payment per impression. The payment formula comes from the same logic. To keep its position, advertiser i needs its score to be no less than the score of the one below: bi qi ≥ scorej+1. This is equivalent to bi ≥ scorej+1/qi. GSP’s principle of “pay the lowest bid needed to keep your position” turns the boundary of this inequality into the payment. If all qualities are 1, the formula reduces to ordinary GSP.

Example 6.13

The same four advertisers and the same bids (A 10, B 8, C 5, D 2). The qualities are A 0.4, B 0.8, C 0.6, D 1. The slot click counts are α = 300, 200, 100.

  1. Scores. A: 10 · 0.4 = 4; B: 8 · 0.8 = 6.4; C: 5 · 0.6 = 3; D: 2 · 1 = 2.
  2. Ranking. B (6.4) > A (4) > C (3) > D (2). A, the highest bidder, is no longer first.
  3. Payments. B in slot 1, the score below is 4: 4/0.8 = 5 TL per click. A in slot 2, the score below is 3: 3/0.4 = 7.5 TL per click. C in slot 3, the score below is 2: 2/0.6 ≈ 3.33 TL per click.
  4. Never above the bid. Every payment is below the bid: 5 ≤ 8, 7.5 ≤ 10, 3.33 ≤ 5. This is always so, because the advertiser’s score is greater than the score of the one below.
  5. Clicks. B: 300 · 0.8 = 240; A: 200 · 0.4 = 80; C: 100 · 0.6 = 60. The total is 380.
  6. Revenue. 240 · 5 + 80 · 7.5 + 60 · 3.33 = 1200 + 600 + 200 = 2000.
  7. Comparison: ranking by bid alone. The order becomes A, B, C. The clicks are 300 · 0.4 = 120, 200 · 0.8 = 160, 100 · 0.6 = 60; the total is 340. The payments under ordinary GSP are 8, 5, 2: revenue is 120 · 8 + 160 · 5 + 60 · 2 = 960 + 800 + 120 = 1880.

Common mistake

Taking the payment in GSP with quality scores to be b(j+1) (the bid below). The correct payment is the score of the one below divided by the advertiser’s own quality. In the example, B pays not the bid of A below (10) but 4/0.8 = 5. If the bid below were paid, B would pay more than its own bid (8).

6.4.12Common mistakes in ad auctions

Common mistake

Mistaking GSP for Vickrey. “GSP is a second-price auction, so truthful bidding is dominant.” This is true only with a single slot. With several slots, truthful bidding may sometimes be a best response, but not always.

Counting those above in a VCG payment. An advertiser’s departure affects only those below. In A’s VCG payment the losses of B, C, and D are counted; in C’s payment only D is counted.

Confusing total and per-click payment. The VCG formula gives αj · pj, that is, the total payment. For the payment per click, the total is divided by the slot’s click count: for B, 700/200 = 3.5.

Comparing revenues under the wrong profile. “GSP brings in 50% more revenue than VCG (3600 versus 2400).” This comparison gives the two rules the same bids. In GSP the bids change; at the lowest envy-free equilibrium the two rules have the same revenue.

6.5Reputation and competition

Platforms bring strangers together. A buyer buys a product from a seller they have never met; a rider gets into the car of a driver they have never seen. Where does this trust come from? Largely from reputation systems. Reputation systems reduce information asymmetry, but they must be designed carefully to rank items with few reviews fairly and to make fake reviews unprofitable. Which platform users stay on is determined by competition, regulation, and recommendation systems.

6.5.1Reputation systems and information asymmetry

Definition 6.12

If in a transaction one party holds important information the other does not know, there is information asymmetry. The typical situation in an online market is this: the seller knows the quality of the product, and the buyer does not know it in advance.

Akerlof (1970) showed with the example of used cars how information asymmetry can collapse a market. When quality is unknown, the buyer offers an average price. This price does not satisfy the good seller, who withdraws from the market. Only bad products remain on the market. Since defective cars are called “lemons” in American slang, this situation is called the market for lemons.

Example 6.14

Suppose half the cars on the market are good and half are bad. A good car is worth 8 to its seller and 10 to a buyer (in thousands of TL). A bad car is worth 3 to its seller and 4 to a buyer. For every car the buyer’s value is higher than the seller’s; that is, every car changing hands benefits both sides.

  1. If quality were known. A good car would sell at a price between 8 and 10, and a bad car at a price between 3 and 4. All cars would be sold.
  2. If quality is unknown. The buyer cannot tell whether a car is good or bad. With a fifty-fifty probability the expected value is 0.5 · 10 + 0.5 · 4 = 7. The buyer offers at most 7.
  3. The good seller withdraws. 7 is below 8, the good seller’s value. The good seller does not sell; only bad sellers accept 7.
  4. The buyer anticipates this. A buyer who knows that only bad cars remain on the market offers at most 4. In the end only bad cars change hands; the gain of 2 on each good car is lost.

Example 6.14 applies to online markets too. If the buyer cannot know that a seller is good, they will not pay a price worth the good seller’s effort, and the good seller leaves the platform. A reputation system exists to fill this information gap, and it does two jobs:

  • Selection: finding the good seller. The experiences of earlier buyers give a new buyer information about the seller’s quality.
  • Discipline: punishing bad behavior in the future. A seller who ships a poor-quality product today loses customers tomorrow because of a low rating. This is the logic of cooperation in repeated games: today’s cheating is paid for with the loss of future earnings.

Reputation systems also give rise to their own strategic problems:

Fake reviews
Purchased positive reviews or negative reviews written about a rival. Luca and Zervas (2016) showed, on the restaurant review site Yelp, that fake reviews are related to competitive conditions: businesses whose reputation is weakening and that face intense competition resort to fake reviews more.
Fear of retaliation
In mutual rating (the host rates the guest and the guest rates the host), bad experiences go unreported, because whoever reports fears getting a bad rating in return.
Rating inflation
If everyone gets 4.8 and above, ratings are not discriminating. A rating of 4.6 means “below average.”
Selective participation
Those who are moderately satisfied mostly stay silent; reviews cluster at the extremes. A new seller, meanwhile, may receive no reviews at all; without reviews they cannot stand out, and because they cannot stand out they receive no sales or reviews. This is called the cold start problem.

There are design measures against these problems: a verified-purchase requirement (only someone who actually bought the product can write a review), giving less weight to old reviews, accounting for the uncertainty of a rating with few reviews (the Bayesian average), and, in mutual rating, revealing both sides' ratings at the same time. The last measure removes the fear of retaliation: nobody gives their rating after seeing the other’s, so a rating cannot be a tool of retaliation.

6.5.2The Bayesian average

In ranking products, one often meets this question: is a product with a 5.0 rating from 2 reviews better, or one with a 4.7 rating from 200 reviews? By raw average the first is ahead. But two reviews are very little information; two happy customers, or two fake reviews, can make the average 5.0.

Definition 6.13

Bayesian average: the score of a product with n reviews is computed as

(C · m + Σ ratings) / (C + n)

Here m is the platform’s overall average (3.5 in the example), C is a confidence constant (10 in the example), and Σ ratings is the sum of the ratings the product received.

The formula is easy to interpret: each product is treated as if C “average” virtual reviews (with rating m) had been added to it. The virtual reviews pull the average of a product with few reviews toward the platform average. A product with many reviews, on the other hand, stays almost at its own average, because 10 virtual reviews are small next to hundreds of real ones.

The same formula can also be written as a weighted average:

Bayesian score = w · (raw average) + (1 − w) · m, w = n / (n + C)

The weight w shows how much the product’s own data is trusted. With 2 reviews, w = 2/12 ≈ 0.17: only 17% of the score comes from the product’s own average. With 200 reviews, w = 200/210 ≈ 0.95. The name “Bayesian” comes from this: the platform average is used as a prior (prior belief), and the product’s reviews are the evidence that updates this belief. As the evidence grows, the posterior approaches the evidence.

Example 6.15

Three products: X (2 reviews, average 5.0), Y (200 reviews, average 4.7), Z (20 reviews, average 4.2). m = 3.5, C = 10, so C · m = 35.

  1. Raw ranking. X (5.0) > Y (4.7) > Z (4.2). X has only 2 reviews.
  2. Rating sums. X: 2 · 5 = 10; Y: 200 · 4.7 = 940; Z: 20 · 4.2 = 84.
  3. Bayesian scores. X: (35 + 10)/(10 + 2) = 45/12 = 3.75. Y: (35 + 940)/(10 + 200) = 975/210 ≈ 4.64. Z: (35 + 84)/(10 + 20) = 119/30 ≈ 3.97.
  4. New ranking. Y (4.64) > Z (3.97) > X (3.75). X, with few reviews, is pulled toward the average and falls to last.
  5. A fake review attempt. Suppose 3 fake 5★ reviews are added to X: 5 reviews, total 25. The raw average is still 5.0; X stays at the top of the raw ranking. The Bayesian score is (35 + 25)/(10 + 5) = 60/15 = 4.0; X is still below Y.

Common mistake

Writing the average in place of the sum in the Bayesian formula. The numerator is C · m + Σ ratings, that is, the sum of the product’s ratings is added. For Y the numerator is not 35 + 4.7 but 35 + 940. If the average is given, first multiply by the number of reviews to get the sum.

6.5.3When are fake reviews profitable?

Although fake reviews are prohibited, some sellers resort to them. From the mechanism design viewpoint the question is: under what conditions are fake reviews profitable for a seller? Fake reviewing can be modeled as an investment decision: it has a return, a cost, and an expected penalty.

Example 6.16

A seller has 20 reviews and an average of 3.6. Sellers with a rating of 4.0 or above are shown near the top of search results; a top position brings the seller an extra profit of G = 600 TL per month. A fake 5★ review costs 15 TL each. The platform detects fraud with probability ρ; a seller who is caught loses L = 3000 TL (the account is suspended and sales are lost).

  1. How many fake reviews are needed (raw average)? The rating sum is 20 · 3.6 = 72. After adding k fake 5★ reviews the average is (72 + 5k)/(20 + k). The condition is (72 + 5k)/(20 + k) ≥ 4. Multiplying by the denominator gives 72 + 5k ≥ 80 + 4k, that is, k ≥ 8. Check: for k = 8, 112/28 = 4.0 ✓; for k = 7, 107/27 ≈ 3.96, which is not enough.
  2. Cost. 8 × 15 = 120 TL.
  3. Expected gain. The seller expects an extra profit of 600 TL, pays 120 TL, and with probability ρ loses 3000 TL: 600 − 120 − ρ · 3000.
  4. Profitability condition. Cheating is profitable if the expected gain is positive: 480 > ρ · 3000 ⟺ ρ < 0.16. For example, at ρ = 0.1 the expected gain is 480 − 300 = 180 TL; at ρ = 0.2 it is 480 − 600 = −120 TL.
  5. With the Bayesian score. With m = 3.5 and C = 10, the numerator is 35 + 72 = 107 and the denominator is 30; the seller’s current Bayesian score is 107/30 ≈ 3.57. The condition is (107 + 5k)/(30 + k) ≥ 4: 107 + 5k ≥ 120 + 4k, that is, k ≥ 13. The cost is 13 × 15 = 195 TL. Profitability: 600 − 195 > ρ · 3000 ⟺ ρ < 0.135.

Figure 6.15 compares the two curves. The Bayesian curve starts below the raw curve (3.57 versus 3.6) and rises more slowly, because each fake review has to outweigh the 10 virtual reviews along with the real ones. The same threshold therefore requires 13 fake reviews; cheating becomes more expensive and the profitability boundary falls to ρ < 0.135.

The model clearly shows the three levers in the platform’s hands. The expected gain has the form G − (cost of reviews) − ρ · L, and the platform can influence every term:

  • Raise ρ: a verified-purchase requirement, anomaly detection (many 5★ reviews arriving on the same day, reviews from new accounts).
  • Raise L: closing the account, wiping the seller’s accumulated reputation, fines.
  • Lower G or raise the cost of reviews: robust scoring (the Bayesian average) reduces the effect of fake reviews; more fake reviews are needed for the same ranking gain.

The mechanism design view is clear here: the platform does not content itself with merely banning cheating; it pushes the expected return of cheating below zero. A rational seller looks not at the rule itself but at the rule’s effect on their own gain.

Common mistake

Not rounding a fractional k up. If the inequality gives k ≥ 7.5, 7 fake reviews are not enough; since the number must be an integer, 8 are needed.

Forgetting the probability of punishment. “A fake review brings 600 TL for 120 TL, so it is always profitable.” The expected penalty ρ · L must be taken into account; when ρ ≥ 0.16, cheating brings a loss.

6.5.4Platform competition and regulation

Network effects determine not only the growth of a single platform but also competition between platforms. When the tipping dynamics of Section 6.2 are applied to a market with two platforms, the result is this: users tend to go to whichever is more crowded, and the more crowded one becomes more crowded still. This shapes three features of competition.

Winner takes all
Because of tipping, a single platform can take most of the market. Even if the second platform’s product is good, its value is low if its users are elsewhere.
Switching costs
Friends, past ratings, accumulated data, and habits keep users in place. A seller with hundreds of positive reviews does not want to start from scratch on a new platform. Switching costs let a platform raise its fees without losing its users.
Multi-homing
Users can use several platforms at once: a driver can work for both Uber and a rival. Multi-homing weakens monopolization. Since the small platform can reach the same drivers, its value to riders does not fall; tipping weakens.

Examples from Türkiye and around the world show this dynamic: Hepsiburada and Trendyol, Uber and Lyft, Instagram and TikTok. In these pairs both platforms have managed to survive. One reason is multi-homing: sellers sell on two marketplaces at once, and users use two apps at once. Another reason is local network effects: a platform can be strong in some cities or among some groups of users.

The concentration created by network effects also raises ethical and regulatory questions:

  • Data privacy and security: large platforms collect the data of a great many people.
  • The risk of monopolization and self-preferencing: if a marketplace is also a seller, it can promote its own products in the ranking. The platform becomes both referee and player.
  • Transparency of ranking and recommendation algorithms: a seller or content creator cannot know why they are invisible.

The European Union’s Digital Markets Act (DMA) is a regulation aimed at these problems. The act defines platforms that have a very large user base and that stand in a key position between business users and consumers as gatekeepers, and imposes obligations on them. For example, a gatekeeper may not rank its own products above those of its rivals and must allow users to take their data to other platforms. From a game-theoretic viewpoint, the regulation is a constraint imposed from outside on the rules the platform sets for itself: a higher-level mechanism that the mechanism designer must also obey.

Data portability and interoperability weaken tipping by lowering switching costs. In terms of the network effect model, this means that users can take the value they have accumulated on one platform to a new platform; the new platform’s initial expectation x0 then does not start from zero.

6.5.5Recommendation, loyalty, and privacy

A platform’s recommendation and ranking algorithm allocates visibility: it decides who stands out. This is an allocation rule, just as an ad auction allocates slots. Participants choose their strategies according to this rule. Four topics stand out.

The platform’s objective
If the algorithm maximizes short-term engagement or revenue, it can conflict with the user’s long-term benefit. Clickbait headlines and infinite feeds raise engagement metrics but consume the user’s time and attention. The platform’s objective function and the user’s utility are not the same thing.
Creators are players too
Creators and sellers compete for visibility and gravitate toward the format the algorithm rewards (title, tags, video length). When everyone chases the same reward, content can become uniform. This is the collective outcome of participants best-responding to the rule.
Loyalty programs
Loyalty points, paid memberships (for example fast shipping), and gamification (badges, streaks) deliberately raise switching costs and reduce multi-homing. A user who has paid for a membership tends to concentrate their shopping on that platform, because the value of the membership grows only as they shop there.
Data and privacy
The price of a “free” service is data. More data allows better recommendations but also raises the risk of leaks and misuse. The user also chooses strategically how much data to share: they trade off better recommendations against less privacy.

The common question of these four topics is the question of the whole chapter: are the platform’s goals aligned with the interests of users and creators? If they are not, users following their own interests may produce outcomes that neither the platform nor society wants. The task of mechanism design is to establish this alignment.

6.6Discussion

The concepts of the chapter answer three general questions. Each question rests on a model computed in these notes.

6.6.1Why do some platforms grow fast while others collapse?

Critical mass and tipping answer this question. If the expectation is above the threshold, the feedback loop grows the platform; if below, it collapses it. In the adoption model, the difference of 60 users between two starting points turned into the difference between 800 users and zero. Ease of use, trust, and early incentives make it easier to cross the threshold: ease of use and incentives lower p, while trust and promotion raise x0. The same mechanism works in reverse: when a platform that is losing users falls below the threshold, the collapse accelerates.

6.6.2How do platforms shape behavior?

Bonuses and discounts increase particular behaviors: extra pay for drivers in a busy area draws drivers to that area. Recommendation algorithms steer preferences: what is visible is chosen. Spam and fake-review controls limit manipulation: the probability of detection and the penalty lower the expected return of cheating. In every case the user best-responds to the platform’s rule; behavior is a function of the rule.

6.6.3Can a design be fair to everyone?

Perfect fairness is hard. The first reason is that users' preferences differ: a price structure that subsidizes buyers costs sellers more. The second reason is that network effects favor large groups: a user in a large city gets better service than one in a small city, because network effects are local. The third reason is strategic manipulation: even a fair rule can be abused. Still, the situation can be improved with incentives, rules, and regulation. The Bayesian average both protects and penalizes products with few reviews; the quality score rewards the ad that gets clicked; the DMA limits a platform’s favoring of its own products.

Common mistake

Seeing platform problems only as technical faults or ill intent. Fake reviews, bonus abuse, and content uniformity are often the result of rational behavior, that is, of the best response to the rule. The solution is usually not to blame the users but to change the rule.

6.7Summary

  • An online platform is an intermediary that brings the sides together; its value comes from the sides' access to one another (a two-sided market). The platform’s rules (who joins, who sees whom, who pays what) form a mechanism; user behavior is the Nash equilibrium under these rules.
  • A network effect can be direct, cross-side, negative, or local. A positive network effect sets up a feedback loop, and the market can tip in one direction.
  • In the adoption model (θ ~ U[0, 1], utility θx − p) the fraction that joins is F(x) = 1 − p/x. The equilibria are the solutions of F(x) = x: for p = 0.16, 0 and 0.8 are stable and 0.2 is unstable. The unstable equilibrium is the critical mass: if the expectation is above 0.2 the market goes to 0.8, and if below, to 0. If p > 0.25, the market never forms.
  • The price structure is how the fee is split between the sides. In the linear model the profit-maximizing price is pA = nA − eB nB: the markup minus the value an A participant adds to side B. In the marketplace example pA = −5, pB = 50, Π = 2800; the buyer is subsidized because the value buyers add for sellers (45) exceeds the markup (40).
  • The participation equilibrium can be found by letting the sides react to each other in turn (participation()); if the product of the cross effects is less than 1 (0.25 · 0.75 = 0.1875), the deviation shrinks by this factor each round and the process converges.
  • In GSP the slot j advertiser pays the bid of the one below per click; truthful bidding is not dominant (A bids 6 instead of 10 and raises its utility from 600 to 1000). In VCG the advertiser pays the loss imposed on the others; truthful bidding is dominant. With the same bids, GSP brings in 3600 and VCG 2400.
  • At locally envy-free equilibria GSP revenue is at least VCG revenue; at the lowest, the payments are the same as in VCG (bids 10, 5, 3.5, 2 → 5 / 3.5 / 2 TL per click).
  • The quality score (b · q) changes the ranking and makes the payment scorej+1/qi; in the example it raises clicks from 340 to 380 and revenue from 1,880 to 2,000.
  • In online allocation with budgets the greedy rule gets half the optimum in the worst case; for budgets with small bids the MSVV rule guarantees 1 − 1/e ≈ 63% of it.
  • Reputation systems reduce information asymmetry (the market for lemons). The Bayesian average (C · m + Σ ratings)/(C + n) pulls a product with few reviews toward the average and makes fake reviews more expensive: the number of fake reviews needed for the top position rises from 8 to 13, and cheating is profitable only for ρ < 0.135 instead of ρ < 0.16.
  • Competition: tipping leads to “winner takes all”; switching costs strengthen a monopoly, and multi-homing weakens it. Regulations such as the DMA impose obligations on gatekeeper platforms.

6.9Test Yourself

  1. In the adoption model (θ ~ U[0, 1], utility θx − p) the cost of joining is p = 0.1875. What are the equilibria, and what is the critical mass? For initial expectations x0 = 0.3 and x0 = 0.2, where do the first steps of the dynamics lead?

    Answer

    x2 − x + 0.1875 = 0: the discriminant is 1 − 0.75 = 0.25, with square root 0.5; the roots are (1 ± 0.5)/2, that is, 0.25 and 0.75. Also x = 0. The stable equilibria are 0 and 0.75; the critical mass is 0.25 (250 users). x0 = 0.3 is above the threshold: x1 = 1 − 0.1875/0.3 = 0.375, participation rises and goes to 0.75. x0 = 0.2 is below the threshold: x1 = 1 − 0.1875/0.2 = 0.0625; since this is smaller than p, x2 = 0 and the platform collapses.

  2. In a marketplace, nA = 30 + 0.25 nB − pA and nB = 60 + 0.75 nA − pB (marginal cost 0). What are the profit-maximizing participation levels, prices, and profit? Is the buyer subsidized? Does the result obey the rule pA = nA − eB nB?

    Answer

    Π = 30 nA + 60 nB + nAnB − nA2 − nB2. Partial derivatives: 30 + nB − 2nA = 0, 60 + nA − 2nB = 0. From the first, nA = (30 + nB)/2; substituting into the second gives 120 + 30 + nB − 4nB = 0, that is, nB = 50, nA = 40. The prices are pA = 30 + 12.5 − 40 = 2.5, pB = 60 + 30 − 50 = 40; the profit is 100 + 2000 = 2100. The rule: 40 − 0.75 · 50 = 2.5 ✓, 50 − 0.25 · 40 = 40 ✓. The buyer is not subsidized: the value buyers add for sellers (37.5) is smaller than the markup (40). The buyer’s fee is nonetheless much lower than the seller’s.

  3. Three slots get 400, 250, and 50 clicks per thousand impressions. Four advertisers bid their value per click: A 9, B 7, C 4, D 3. What are the total payment of each slot and the platform revenue under GSP and VCG? What are the payments per click in VCG?

    Answer

    GSP: A 400 · 7 = 2800, B 250 · 4 = 1000, C 50 · 3 = 150; revenue 3950. VCG from the bottom up: C (50 − 0) · 3 = 150; B (250 − 50) · 4 + 150 = 950; A (400 − 250) · 7 + 950 = 1050 + 950 = 2000; revenue 3100. Per click: A 2000/400 = 5, B 950/250 = 3.8, C 150/50 = 3. In every slot GSP ≥ VCG (7 ≥ 5, 4 ≥ 3.8, 3 ≥ 3).

  4. In the GSP of question 3 everyone bids truthfully. What is A’s (value 9) utility? Can A raise its utility by changing its bid? Is the truthful profile a Nash equilibrium?

    Answer

    With the truthful bid, A pays 7 in slot 1: 400 · (9 − 7) = 800. With a bid between 4 and 7, A drops to slot 2 and pays C’s 4: 250 · (9 − 4) = 1250 > 800. Between 3 and 4, A drops to slot 3 and pays 3: 50 · 6 = 300. The best response is a bid between 4 and 7 (utility 1250). Since A can deviate profitably, the truthful profile is not a Nash equilibrium. The difference in clicks between slots 1 and 2 (150) is small and the difference in price (7 versus 4) is large, so the lower slot looks attractive.

  5. Two slots get 100 and 50 clicks per thousand impressions; the number of clicks of i in slot j is αj qi. Bids and qualities: A 5 (q = 0.6), B 4 (q = 0.9), C 6 (q = 0.4), D 2 (q = 1). In GSP with quality scores, what are the order, the payments per click, the total clicks, and the revenue? What changes compared with GSP that ranks by bid alone?

    Answer

    Scores: A 3, B 3.6, C 2.4, D 2 → order B, A, C, D. B in slot 1 pays 3/0.9 ≈ 3.33 TL per click, and A in slot 2 pays 2.4/0.6 = 4 TL per click. Clicks: B 100 · 0.9 = 90, A 50 · 0.6 = 30; total 120. Revenue 90 · 10/3 + 30 · 4 = 300 + 120 = 420. Ranking by bid alone gives C (6), A (5): C gets 100 · 0.4 = 40 clicks and pays 5 (200), and A gets 50 · 0.6 = 30 clicks and pays B’s 4 (120); total 70 clicks, revenue 320. The quality score raises clicks from 70 to 120 and revenue from 320 to 420; C, the highest bidder, gets no slot.

  6. The Bayesian average is computed with m = 3.5, C = 10. Product P has 5 reviews (average 4.8), and product R has 50 reviews (average 4.4). What are the raw and Bayesian rankings? At least how many fake 5★ reviews must be added to P for its Bayesian score to reach R’s?

    Answer

    Raw ranking: P (4.8) > R (4.4). Bayesian: P (35 + 24)/(10 + 5) = 59/15 ≈ 3.93; R (35 + 220)/(10 + 50) = 255/60 = 4.25. The Bayesian ranking is R > P. With fake reviews: (59 + 5k)/(15 + k) ≥ 4.25 ⟺ 59 + 5k ≥ 63.75 + 4.25k ⟺ 0.75k ≥ 4.75 ⟺ k ≥ 6.33; since it must be an integer, k = 7 (94/22 ≈ 4.27). 6 fake reviews are not enough (89/21 ≈ 4.24).

  7. A seller has 30 reviews with an average of 3.7. Sellers rated 4.0 or above move to the top position and earn an extra profit of G = 500 TL per month. A fake 5★ review costs 10 TL each; the detection probability is ρ, and the loss when caught is L = 2000 TL. With the raw average and with the Bayesian score (m = 3.5, C = 10), how many fake reviews are needed, and for which values of ρ is cheating profitable?

    Answer

    The rating sum is 30 · 3.7 = 111. Raw: (111 + 5k)/(30 + k) ≥ 4 ⟺ 111 + 5k ≥ 120 + 4k ⟺ k ≥ 9. The cost is 90 TL; profitable ⟺ 500 − 90 > 2000ρ ⟺ ρ < 0.205. Bayesian: (35 + 111 + 5k)/(40 + k) ≥ 4 ⟺ 146 + 5k ≥ 160 + 4k ⟺ k ≥ 14. The cost is 140 TL; profitable ⟺ 360 > 2000ρ ⟺ ρ < 0.18. The Bayesian score raises the number of fake reviews needed from 9 to 14 and narrows the range of profitability.

  8. A’s budget is 80 TL and B’s is 40 TL. For “book” searches both bid 1 TL; for “notebook” searches only A bids 1 TL. First 80 book searches arrive, then 80 notebook searches. How much revenue do the greedy rule (ties to A), the Balance rule (to the one with the larger remaining budget; ties to A), and the optimum with knowledge of the future bring in?

    Answer

    Greedy: all 80 book searches go to A and A’s budget runs out; nobody is left for the notebooks: revenue 80. Balance: the book searches go to A until A’s remainder falls to 40 (40 searches); after that, since the remainders are equal, the remaining 40 book searches are split 20–20. A has spent 60 and B 20. In the notebook searches A’s remaining 20 TL is used: revenue 80 + 20 = 100. Optimum: 40 of the book searches to B and 40 to A; 40 of the notebook searches to A: revenue 120 (the sum of the two budgets). Greedy gets 2/3 of the optimum and Balance gets 5/6.
