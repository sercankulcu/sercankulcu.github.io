Chapter 4: Mechanism Design

Game theory usually takes the rules of a game as given and predicts what the players will do in equilibrium. Mechanism design turns the question around: the desired outcome is fixed, and the task is to find rules that produce it. The difficulty is that players can hide private information such as values, preferences, or costs from the designer. These notes start with direct mechanisms and the revelation principle, then move on to incentive compatibility, individual rationality, and Myerson’s lemma. Next comes VCG, the most important general mechanism that uses money, followed by the Gale–Shapley, median, Boston, and TTC mechanisms for settings where money is not available. The last topic is Bayesian games with incomplete information.

4.1Basic concepts

The question of this chapter is the following. If everyone in a group pursues their own interest, and each person knows something the others do not, what rules let us reach a good decision for the group? A small bargaining game shows how rules shape behavior.

4.1.1A first game: the Ultimatum Game

Two players must split 10 tokens. The first player is the proposer and the second is the responder. The proposer offers the responder a share, for example “3 tokens for you, 7 for me”. The responder either accepts or rejects. If the responder accepts, the split is carried out. If the responder rejects, both get 0. There is no haggling: the offer is made once. The game takes its name from this feature, since the offer is an “ultimatum”, take it or leave it.

Figure 4.1 shows the structure of the game. The proposer (P) first chooses a number x, the number of tokens offered to the responder. The responder (R) then sees this number and decides. If the offer is accepted, the payoffs are (10 − x, x), where the first number is the proposer’s payoff and the second is the responder’s. If it is rejected, the payoffs are (0, 0).

Two questions arise. Should the responder accept an offer of 1 token? How many tokens should the proposer offer? Both answers depend on what the player cares about. For a responder who cares only about the number of tokens, 1 token is better than 0, so rejecting looks irrational. Yet many people treat an offer of 1 token as an insult and reject it. This tension brings out the central question of the chapter: how do rules affect players’ behavior, and how should a rule maker, taking that effect into account, choose the rules?

The Ultimatum Game is a small mechanism: the proposer presents the responder with the rule “this split or nothing”. The game-theoretic solution is given in 4.2.5.

4.1.2What is mechanism design?

In the usual approach of game theory, the game is given: the players, their strategies, and their payoffs are known. The question is what will happen in this game. Mechanism design reverses this approach. The game is not given; the designer builds it. The designer has a desired outcome and looks for rules that produce it. The rules determine two things: who gets what (the allocation) and who pays what (the payments).

This task is harder than it first appears, because the designer does not know the players’ private information. A seller who wants to give an item to the person who values it most does not know who values it how much. A municipality does not know how much residents value a park. This information is held only by the players. The designer has to ask the players for it, and the players may lie if their interests call for it.

The goal of mechanism design is therefore this: to reach the desired outcome even when players are selfish and strategic, that is, even when they pursue their own interests. The designer cannot order the players to “tell the truth”; the designer can only choose the rules. The rules must be chosen so that telling the truth, or behaving as desired, is in the player’s own interest.

Figure 4.2 illustrates this situation. Three people value an item at 70, 55, and 85, respectively. These numbers are in the players’ heads; the designer cannot see them (“the designer sees: ?, ?, ?”). The mechanism computes the outcome and the payments from the numbers b that the players report. The reports need not equal the true values. In a good mechanism, reporting the true value is in the player’s own interest.

The importance of the field has also been recognized with scientific prizes. The 2007 Nobel Prize in Economics went to Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin, and Roger Myerson “for having laid the foundations of mechanism design theory”. In 2012 Alvin Roth and Lloyd Shapley were honored for their work on stable matching and market design.

4.1.3Analysis and synthesis

Game theory and mechanism design are the same road traveled in opposite directions. Game theory is a tool of analysis: the rules are given, the players’ behavior (their equilibrium strategies) is found, and the outcome that follows from that behavior is predicted. Mechanism design is a tool of synthesis: the desired outcome (the goal) is given, and we look for rules under which the players will behave in a way that leads to that goal.

Figure 4.3 puts the two directions side by side. The top row asks: “What will the players do under these rules, and who will win?” The bottom row asks: “Which mechanism is needed to obtain this outcome in equilibrium?” In short, in game theory the flow runs from rules to outcome; in mechanism design it runs from outcome to rules.

Working in the direction of synthesis has an important consequence. The designer cannot force the players; the players themselves decide which strategy to play. The designer’s only tool is the rules. Every proposal in mechanism design is therefore tested with an equilibrium question: under the proposed rules, will the players actually behave as intended? If they do not, the goal is not reached, however well meant the rules may be.

4.1.4Incentives

Mechanism design rests on a simple observation: people respond to incentives. If the rules make a certain behavior profitable, players move toward that behavior. If a parking fee goes up, people drive less. If wrong answers lose points on an exam, students leave the questions they are unsure about blank. In every case the rule maker should ask: “Which behavior does this rule reward?”

A good mechanism rewards honest behavior and deters strategic lying. A mechanism in which telling the truth is in the player’s own interest is called incentive compatible. The formal definition of the concept is in 4.2.4. Two familiar examples show what incentive incompatibility leads to.

The free-rider problem. Suppose a park is to be built in a neighborhood and its cost is to be shared according to how much each resident values the park. Each resident states for themselves how much they value it. A resident who understates the value pays less, and if the park is built anyway, enjoys it just as much. Everyone is then inclined to understate. A person who wants to benefit from what others pay is called a free rider. If everyone acts as a free rider, the reports in total may not cover the cost, and the park that everyone wants is never built.

Strategic voting. In an election, a voter whose favorite candidate has no chance of winning may vote for a second choice who does. This is the voter hiding their true preference. The rule (the candidate with the most votes wins) rewards this behavior. As a result, the election outcome may fail to reflect the voters’ true preferences.

The same chain operates in both examples: incentive incompatibility pushes players to give false information, and false information leads to inefficient or unfair outcomes. Mechanism design tries to break this chain at the start: giving true information must be in the player’s own interest.

Common mistake

“If players are honest, any rule works well, so let us simply assume honesty.” This is exactly the assumption that mechanism design does not make. It accepts that players will pursue their own interests and chooses the rules so that they are robust to this behavior. Honest behavior is not an assumption; it is the result of a good mechanism.

4.1.5Real-world applications

The problems of mechanism design appear in many areas of everyday life. In all the examples below, an institution makes decisions that depend on the private information of individuals.

  • Tax systems. A person’s income is private information. The government cannot see income directly; it relies on declarations and audits. Tax rates and audit rules should encourage honest declarations.
  • Public goods financing. For goods that everyone benefits from, such as parks, roads, or libraries, there are two questions: should the project be built, and who should pay how much? Both questions depend on residents’ hidden values. The mechanism-based solution is VCG in 4.3.
  • Procurement and auctions. Public purchases are made through tenders; spectrum licenses and works of art are sold at auction. The seller does not know the highest price the buyers are willing to pay; the rules try to bring this information out.
  • Online advertising. A search engine runs an auction for ad slots on every query, within milliseconds. The value an advertiser places on a click is private.
  • Matching markets. In school placement, medical residency matching, and dorm room exchange, money is not used, but the rules still matter. Students’ preference lists are private information, and the rules should reward writing honest lists (4.4).
  • Grading group projects. If students report their own contributions to a group project, the grading rule should discourage exaggeration.

The common structure of these examples is the basis of the formal model: the players, the players’ private information (types), the possible outcomes, and the designer’s goal. These four elements are defined in 4.2.1.

4.2Direct mechanisms

The formal language of mechanism design rests on four concepts: a player’s private information (the type), the designer’s goal (the social choice function), the direct mechanism in which players report only their types, and the revelation principle, which justifies focusing on such mechanisms. Whether a mechanism is good is tested against the criteria of incentive compatibility, individual rationality, efficiency, and budget balance. In single-parameter settings, Myerson’s lemma characterizes incentive-compatible mechanisms exactly.

4.2.1Types and the social choice function

A mechanism has n players, numbered 1, 2, …, n. Each player has private information known only to that player: the value placed on an item, a ranking of the alternatives, or the cost of doing a job. This private information is called the player’s type.

Definition 4.1

The type θi of player i is an element of the set of possible types Θi: θi ∈ Θi. The type is private information. The list of all players’ types, θ = (θ1, …, θn), is called the type profile. The set of possible outcomes is denoted O. A social choice function

f : Θ1 × … × Θn → O

is a rule that assigns a “desired” outcome to each type profile.

The symbols in the definition may look unfamiliar at first. Θ is the capital form of the Greek letter “theta” and denotes a set; the lowercase θ is an element chosen from that set. The sign × in Θ1 × … × Θn is the Cartesian product: the set of all lists that can be formed by choosing one type from each player. For example, if each of two players can be of type “low” or “high”, there are four type profiles: (low, low), (low, high), (high, low), (high, high). The social choice function assigns an outcome to each of these four profiles.

The outcome set O depends on the problem. If a single item is being sold, the outcome answers the question “who gets the item?”: O = {Ayşe, Bora, Cem, nobody}. If a municipality is choosing one of several projects, the outcome is the chosen project: O = {Park, Library, Gym}. In a school placement, the outcome is an assignment that says which school each student attends.

The social choice function expresses the designer’s goal. The designer answers the question “if I knew the types, what would I want to do?” The most common goal is efficiency: the outcome should maximize the sum of the players’ values. In selling a single item, this means giving it to the player who values it most.

Example 4.1

Ayşe, Bora, and Cem value an item at 70, 55, and 85, respectively. The type profile is θ = (70, 55, 85). The efficient social choice function is the rule “the item goes to the player with the highest value”.

  1. Compare the values: 85 > 70 > 55.
  2. The largest value is Cem’s; f(θ) = “Cem gets it”.
  3. The social welfare of this outcome, that is, the value of the person who gets the item, is 85. Had the item gone to Ayşe the welfare would have been 70, and had it gone to Bora, 55. The efficient outcome picks the largest of these three.

The problem is that the designer cannot see these three numbers and has to ask the players. If the rule is only “whoever reports the highest value gets it” and there is no payment, every player says “my value is 1000”. The reports then carry no information, and f cannot be applied.

The example brings out the central question of mechanism design: are there rules that implement f even when the players pursue their own interests? Sometimes the answer is yes and sometimes no. For selling a single item the answer is yes: it is enough to choose the price the winner pays correctly (4.2.4). In some settings, however, no rule can achieve everything we want at once (4.2.7).

4.2.2Direct mechanisms and quasi-linear utility

The designer has to get the information from the players. The most direct way is to ask each player for their type; mechanisms of this kind are called direct mechanisms.

Definition 4.2

In a direct mechanism, each player i submits a report bi ∈ Θi. The report may or may not equal the true type. From the report profile b = (b1, …, bn) the mechanism computes an outcome x(b) and a payment pi(b) for each player. The player’s quasi-linear utility is

ui = vi(x) − pi

Here vi(x) is the value that a player of type θi places on outcome x. With a single item, vi = θi if the player gets the item and 0 otherwise.

Figure 4.5 shows a direct mechanism as a box. The input to the box is the reports and its output is the outcome and the payments. Everyone knows the rules inside the box, and the players decide what to report accordingly.

What does “quasi-linear” mean? The money part of the utility is linear: paying 1 TL more reduces utility by exactly 1 unit. The value part vi(x) can be any function. This assumption ensures that value and money are measured in the same unit. A player who gets an item worth 85,000 TL for 70,000 TL has utility 15,000 TL; a player who gets nothing and pays nothing has utility 0. Quasi-linear utility is the standard model for settings in which money can be transferred between people; all the mechanisms with payments in this chapter rest on this assumption.

Example 4.2

Let the values of Ayşe, Bora, and Cem be 70, 55, and 85. Suppose a direct mechanism uses this rule: the highest report wins, the winner pays their own report, and the losers pay nothing.

  1. If everyone reports honestly, b = (70, 55, 85). Cem wins and pays 85. Cem’s utility is 85 − 85 = 0; Ayşe’s and Bora’s utilities are 0.
  2. If Cem reports 71, his is still the highest report. Cem pays 71 and his utility is 85 − 71 = 14.
  3. By lying, Cem gained 14 units. In this mechanism an honest report gains Cem nothing, while a lie pays off.

If the rule stays the same and only the payment is changed to “the winner pays the second-highest report”, the situation is different. Whether Cem reports 85 or 71, he pays 70 and his utility is 15. Since the payment does not depend on Cem’s own report, changing the report does not help him. The graph of this difference is in 4.2.4.

Indirect mechanisms. Not every mechanism asks players for their types. In an English auction the price rises slowly, and players show by raising a hand whether they are still in. In a negotiation the parties make offers in turn. Mechanisms of this kind are called indirect mechanisms: the player’s strategy is not to state a type but to take some other action (raising a hand, making an offer, writing down a ranking). Indirect mechanisms are very common in practice, but in theory they can be reduced to direct mechanisms. This reduction is provided by the revelation principle.

4.2.3The revelation principle

The mechanism designer faces infinitely many possible rules: auctions, sequential offers, complicated voting procedures, and so on. Examining all of them is impossible. The revelation principle narrows this search space considerably: it is enough to look at direct mechanisms in which honest reporting is an equilibrium.

Theorem 4.1 (Revelation principle)

If an outcome can be implemented as an equilibrium of an indirect mechanism M, then, under suitable conditions, a direct mechanism M* in which honest reporting is an equilibrium can implement the same outcome.

The idea behind the principle is shown in Figure 4.6. Let M be an indirect mechanism and let the players’ equilibrium strategies be s1, …, sn. Each strategy is a rule: a player of type θi chooses the action si(θi). The designer builds the following new mechanism: “Tell me your type; I will play, on your behalf, the equilibrium strategy you would play at that type.” This new mechanism M* is direct, because it asks the player only for their type.

Here is why honest reporting is an equilibrium in M*. Let the other players report honestly. Then the designer plays sj(θj) on their behalf, which is their equilibrium behavior in M. If player i reports θi honestly, the designer plays si(θi) for that player. If player i reports some other bi, the designer plays si(bi) for that player; this is the same as a player of type θi in M choosing a different action instead of the equilibrium strategy. Since s is an equilibrium, such a deviation is not profitable in M. So lying is not profitable in M* either. Hence honest reporting is an equilibrium in M*, and under honest reporting M* produces the equilibrium outcome of M. □

The consequence of this simple observation is powerful: to answer the question “is there a mechanism that implements this goal?”, it is enough to look at direct mechanisms in which honest reporting is an equilibrium. If there is no such direct mechanism, there is no indirect mechanism either.

Example 4.3

In an English auction the price starts at zero and rises slowly. Each bidder stays in until the price reaches their own value and then drops out. Let the values be 70, 55, and 85.

  1. When the price reaches 55, Bora drops out, because winning at a higher price would give negative utility.
  2. When the price reaches 70, Ayşe drops out. Only Cem remains.
  3. Cem gets the item for about 70, that is, the second-highest value. His utility is 85 − 70 = 15.

Now let the designer say to the players: “Write your value on a piece of paper; I will stay in on your behalf until the price reaches your value.” This is a direct mechanism. When the papers are opened, the highest value wins and pays the second-highest value. This is the second-price (Vickrey) auction. By the revelation principle, writing the true value is best in this direct mechanism, because writing a wrong value is the same as dropping out at the wrong moment in the English auction.

The principle also has limits. The revelation principle does not say that “honest reporting is a dominant strategy in every mechanism”. It says only that honest reporting is an equilibrium in the constructed mechanism M*. What kind of equilibrium it is depends on the equilibrium concept used in M. If the strategies in M are dominant strategies, honesty is a dominant strategy in M*. If the strategies in M form only a Bayes–Nash equilibrium (4.5.1), honesty in M* is also only a Bayes–Nash equilibrium. These two cases are defined below with two separate incentive conditions.

Common mistake

“By the revelation principle direct mechanisms are better, so they should always be used in practice.” The principle is an existence and reduction result; it does not say that a direct mechanism is better in practice. Indirect mechanisms may be easier to understand, may ask for less information, or may protect players’ privacy. In an English auction the winner never reveals their own value; in a second-price auction they have to write it down.

4.2.4Incentive compatibility and individual rationality

Two conditions are required for a direct mechanism to work well. First, telling the truth must be in the player’s own interest. Second, the player must be willing to participate in the mechanism. The first condition is called incentive compatibility, the second individual rationality.

To make the notation easier, we write ui(θi; bi, b−i). This expression means: player i with true type θi reports bi, the others report b−i, and this is the player’s true utility. b−i means “the reports of everyone other than i”. The θi before the semicolon shows which true value the utility is measured against, and what comes after it shows the reports.

Definition 4.3

A direct mechanism is dominant-strategy incentive compatible (DSIC) if, for every player i, every true type θi, every lie bi, and every report profile b−i of the others, the following inequality holds:

ui(θi; θi, b−i) ≥ ui(θi; bi, b−i)

That is, whatever the others report, telling the truth is at least as good as lying.

DSIC is a very strong condition. The player need not know or guess anything about the others. Whether the others are honest or lying, smart or mistaken, an honest report is best for the player; that is, it is a dominant strategy. DSIC mechanisms therefore give players simple advice: “Say what you think, and do not worry about the rest.”

Definition 4.4

Suppose types are drawn from a probability distribution known to everyone. A direct mechanism is Bayesian incentive compatible (BIC) if, when the other players report honestly, telling the truth maximizes each player’s expected utility. The expectation is taken over the others’ unknown types.

The BIC condition is weaker than DSIC in two ways. First, it requires that telling the truth be best only when the others are honest; it says nothing if the others lie. Second, it requires utility to be maximal not in every case but on average (as an expected value). The expected value is the sum of the utility in each possible case multiplied by the probability of that case. For example, if the rival’s value is 4 or 8 with equal probability, and with some report the player gets 6 in the first case and 0 in the second, the expected utility is ½ · 6 + ½ · 0 = 3.

Theorem 4.2 (DSIC ⇒ BIC)

Every DSIC mechanism is BIC. The converse is not true in general.

Let the mechanism be DSIC. Whatever the others report, an honest report gives player i at least as much utility as lying. When the others are honest, their reports are their types, and this is one of the “any b−i” cases. For every type profile, the honest utility is known to be at least the lying utility. If these inequalities are multiplied by the same probabilities and summed, the inequality is preserved. Hence an honest report is also best in expected utility. □

The first-price auction shows why the converse fails (4.5.4).

Definition 4.5

A mechanism is individually rational (IR) if participation gives no player lower utility than staying out of the system. If the utility of staying out is taken to be 0, then under honest reporting every player must satisfy

ui(θi; θi, b−i) ≥ 0

.

The IR condition is about participation. If the mechanism cannot force people to take part, participants must not lose out. If participating makes the player worse off, the player stays out, and the mechanism never gets their information. For example, if the winner of an auction is forced to pay more than their value, IR is violated.

Incentive compatibility is easiest to see with a numerical example. Suppose an item is sold between two bidders. Let the bidder’s value be 10 and the rival’s bid 6. Two payment rules are compared. Under the first-price rule the winner pays their own bid. Under the second-price rule the winner pays the rival’s bid. In both rules the highest bid wins.

Example 4.4
  1. A bid below 6. Under both rules the bidder loses; the utility is 0.
  2. First price, bid b above 6. The bidder wins and pays b. The utility is 10 − b. The truthful bid b = 10 gives utility 10 − 10 = 0. A bid of b = 7 gives 10 − 7 = 3. Bidding below one’s value pays off; the rule is not IC.
  3. Second price, bid above 6. The bidder wins and pays the rival’s bid, 6. The utility is 10 − 6 = 4. Whether the bid is 7, 10, or 50, the utility is the same: 4.
  4. Second price, a lower bid. If the bid drops below 6, the bidder loses and gets 0 instead of 4. The truthful bid (10) is never worse.

The red line in the graph shows that under the first-price rule the bidder wants to move away from truthfulness: the closer the bid gets to 6, the more the bidder gains. The green line shows that under second price the bid determines whether the bidder wins, but does not change the price paid.

The rival bid of 6 in the example is an arbitrary choice. Whatever the rival bid r, the same logic works under the second-price rule. If r < 10, the truthful bid wins and gives utility 10 − r > 0; a lower bid either gives the same utility or loses. If r > 10, the truthful bid loses and gives 0; a bid above r wins but gives utility 10 − r < 0. In every case the truthful bid is best; the second-price rule is DSIC. Moreover, the winner never pays more than their value and the loser pays nothing; the rule is IR.

Common mistake

Confusing IC with IR. IC is about what the player reports: is telling the truth better than lying? IR is about whether the player participates: is taking part better than staying out? A mechanism can satisfy one without the other. For example, add to the second-price auction a rule that everyone first pays a 3 TL entry fee. Since the fee does not depend on the bid, the truthful bid is still best (IC is preserved). But a winner with value 5 and a rival bid of 4 has utility 5 − 4 − 3 = −2, and the losers’ utility is −3: IR is violated.

4.2.5Back to the Ultimatum Game

With incentive compatibility and individual rationality defined, the Ultimatum Game can be revisited. In the game, the proposer presents the responder with a “take it or leave it” mechanism. The responder’s IR condition is that what they get from accepting is no less than what they get from rejecting.

The theoretical answer. If the responder cares only about the number of tokens, accepting an offer of x tokens gives x and rejecting gives 0. As long as x ≥ 0, accepting is the best response; this is the responder’s IR condition. At x = 0, accepting and rejecting both give 0, so the responder is indifferent; both options are best responses.

The proposer anticipates this behavior and chooses the offer accordingly. Because the game is sequential, the solution is found by backward induction: first determine what the responder would say to each offer, then let the proposer choose, knowing that. The equilibria found this way are called subgame perfect equilibria. If offers are whole numbers, there are two subgame perfect equilibria in pure strategies.

Example 4.5
  1. The responder also accepts 0. The responder then accepts every offer. To maximize their own share 10 − x, the proposer offers x = 0. The outcome is (10, 0).
  2. The responder rejects 0. Since the responder is indifferent, rejecting 0 is also a best response. Offers x ≥ 1 are then accepted and 0 is rejected. If the proposer offers 0 they get 0; if they offer 1 they get 9. The best is x = 1 and the outcome is (9, 1).
  3. There is no other equilibrium in pure strategies: since the responder always accepts 1 and above, the proposer does not want to offer more than 1. If mixed strategies are counted too, the responder may accept 0 with probability p; the proposer earns 10p from offering 0 and 9 from offering 1, and can mix the two at p = 0.9.

What happens in reality? Since the first experiment on the game (Güth, Schmittberger, and Schwarze, 1982), a similar picture has emerged in hundreds of experiments. The most common offer is 40–50% of the pie. About half of the offers below 20% are rejected (Camerer, 2003). People punish offers they find unfair at a cost to themselves. This behavior does not fit the model of a player who “cares only about tokens”.

The “Theory” row of the table corresponds to the (9, 1) equilibrium in which the responder rejects 0; if the responder also accepts 0, the proposer offers 0 and gets 10. Against the fairness-minded responder, the proposer cannot offer less than 5; if they do, they are rejected and get 0. The proposer’s payoff falls as the responder’s acceptance threshold rises.

The lesson for mechanism design is this: when designing an incentive-compatible mechanism, the player’s utility function must be chosen correctly. If the player also cares about fairness, the IR condition is not “x ≥ 0” but “x reaches the share the player considers fair”. A mechanism built on a wrong utility model looks IR on paper but is rejected in practice. The discussion of behavior and ethics (4.6.1) returns to this topic.

4.2.6Proportional cost sharing, step by step

The free-rider problem (4.1.4) can also be examined with numbers. Three residents, Ayşe, Bora, and Cem, want a shared park. The park costs 60,000 TL. The residents’ true values for the park are 30, 25, and 20 thousand TL, respectively. The values sum to 75. Since 75 > 60, building the park is efficient: the park creates more value than it costs.

The municipality applies the following rule. Each resident reports the value they place on the park. If the reports sum to 60 or more, the park is built and the cost is shared in proportion to the reports: resident i pays 60 · bi / (b1 + b2 + b3). If the total is less than 60, the park is not built and no one pays. The rule looks fair: whoever values the park more pays more. The question is whether the rule is incentive compatible.

  1. Everyone honest. The reports are 30, 25, 20; the total is 75 ≥ 60, so the park is built. The payments are 60 · 30/75 = 24, 60 · 25/75 = 20, and 60 · 20/75 = 16; they sum to 60. The utilities are 30 − 24 = 6, 25 − 20 = 5, 20 − 16 = 4. Everyone’s utility is at least 0, so IR holds. The total utility is 15, which equals the difference between the sum of values, 75, and the cost, 60.
  2. Cem lies. While Ayşe and Bora stay honest, Cem reports a value of 5. The total is 30 + 25 + 5 = 60, so the park is still built. The payments equal the reports: 60 · 30/60 = 30, 25, and 5. Cem pays 5 but still enjoys the park at a value of 20. His utility is 20 − 5 = 15. When honest it was 4; the lie gained Cem 11 units. The rule is not IC.
  3. Cem’s most profitable lie. Let Cem’s report be c. For the park to be built, we need 55 + c ≥ 60, that is, c ≥ 5. When the park is built, Cem’s payment is 60c/(55 + c), which increases with c. Since Cem wants the park built and wants to pay as little as possible, he chooses c = 5: the total is exactly 60.
  4. Everyone acts on the same logic. Each resident, relying on the others’ reports, wants to lower their own report. If everyone reports half their value, the total is 15 + 12.5 + 10 = 37.5 < 60. The park is not built; the total utility falls from 15 to 0.

Free riding is therefore not only a fairness problem but an efficiency problem. A park that everyone wants and whose total value exceeds its cost may not be built because of the incentives the rule creates.

The source of the problem is clear: a resident’s report directly affects their own payment even when the outcome does not change. When Cem says 5 instead of 20, the park is still built; Cem’s lie does not change the outcome, it only lowers his own payment. The idea for an incentive-compatible rule is this: a player’s report should be able to affect their payment only by changing the chosen outcome. If the outcome stays the same, the payment should stay the same. This idea is the basis of the VCG mechanism (4.3).

Common mistake

“Everyone gains when honest, so the rule is good.” IR holding under honest reporting does not show that the rule is IC. To test IC, we must check whether each player, holding the others fixed, could gain more utility with another report. Under proportional sharing, Cem deviating alone gets 15 instead of 4.

4.2.7Design criteria and trade-offs

Four basic criteria are used to evaluate a mechanism. The first two concern incentives, the last two the quality of the outcome.

  • Incentive compatibility (IC): revealing the true information is the best option. It can be required at the dominant-strategy level (DSIC) or at the Bayesian level (BIC).
  • Individual rationality (IR): participating is no worse than staying out.
  • Efficiency: under honest reporting, the chosen outcome is the one with the highest social welfare. The social welfare of an outcome x is SW(x) = Σi vi(x), that is, the sum of the values the players place on that outcome.
  • Budget balance: this concerns the sum of the payments. Weak budget balance means the sum of the payments is at least 0: the mechanism needs no money from outside. Strong budget balance means the sum of the payments is exactly 0: no outside money is needed and no money is left over.

The symbol Σ means “sum”. The expression Σi vi(x) means that i runs over all players and the values vi(x) are added up. It is the same as writing v1(x) + v2(x) + v3(x) for three players.

Figure 4.8 compares four mechanisms against these criteria. VCG and proportional sharing come from the public project example, and the fixed price and the midpoint from bilateral trade. In bilateral trade there is one seller and one buyer. The seller’s cost of giving up the good is c, and the buyer’s value for the good is v. Both are private information. Trade is efficient only when v ≥ c: the good should end up with the one who values it more.

Example 4.7

Fixed price. An intermediary announces the price in advance as 50. The buyer says “I buy” or “I don’t buy”, the seller says “I sell” or “I don’t sell”; if both say yes, trade takes place at 50.

  1. The buyer’s best response is “I buy” if v ≥ 50. The seller’s best response is “I sell” if c ≤ 50. These responses do not depend on what the other side says: the rule is DSIC.
  2. When trade takes place, the buyer gains v − 50 ≥ 0 and the seller gains 50 − c ≥ 0: IR holds. What the buyer pays goes to the seller: there is strong budget balance.
  3. But let v = 45 and c = 40. Trade is efficient (45 > 40), but it does not happen because the buyer is unwilling to pay 50. The rule is not efficient.

Midpoint rule. The buyer reports a value and the seller reports a cost. If the buyer’s report is at least the seller’s, trade takes place at the price midway between the two reports.

  1. Let v = 60 and c = 40, and both report honestly. The price is (60 + 40)/2 = 50. The buyer gains 10 and the seller gains 10. Under honest reporting, trade takes place exactly when it is efficient and no one loses.
  2. If the buyer reports a value of 52, trade still takes place, but the price becomes (52 + 40)/2 = 46. The buyer’s utility is 60 − 46 = 14 > 10. Understating the value paid off: the rule is not IC.

The trade-off in these examples is no coincidence. Myerson and Satterthwaite (1983) showed that it is a general result.

Theorem 4.3 (Myerson–Satterthwaite)

In bilateral trade where the buyer’s value and the seller’s cost are independent and drawn from overlapping ranges, no mechanism is simultaneously efficient, (Bayesian) incentive compatible, individually rational, and budget balanced.

The proof of the theorem is beyond the scope of these notes. What matters is the meaning of the result: a designer who wants all four criteria at once is asking for the impossible. Mechanism design is therefore often a problem of trade-offs. The designer decides which criterion to give up and by how much. For example, VCG preserves efficiency and DSIC but runs a budget deficit in bilateral trade: the buyer pays the seller’s cost, the seller receives the buyer’s value, and someone from outside must cover the difference; a fixed price preserves the budget and incentives but misses some efficient trades.

Common mistake

Reading the Myerson–Satterthwaite theorem as “trade is impossible”. The theorem does not say that trade cannot happen; it says only that no rule satisfies all four properties at once. The fixed-price rule is used every day and makes many efficient trades possible; it just misses some.

4.2.8Implementation theory: is truth-telling the only equilibrium?

The revelation principle says that honest reporting is an equilibrium. But the same mechanism may also have other, unwanted equilibria. The rules make a good equilibrium possible; they do not guarantee that the players will meet at that equilibrium.

Requiring that all equilibria of a mechanism lead to the desired outcome is a stronger demand. The field that studies such mechanisms is called implementation theory. One of its basic results is Maskin’s work characterizing the social choice functions that can be implemented in Nash equilibrium (Maskin, 1999). An example shows why the question matters.

Example 4.8

In a second-price (Vickrey) auction let Ayşe’s value be 10 and Bora’s value 5. Ayşe bids 0 and Bora bids 100.

  1. Outcome. The highest bid is Bora’s; Bora wins and pays the second-highest bid, which is 0. Bora’s utility is 5 − 0 = 5 and Ayşe’s is 0.
  2. Can Ayşe deviate? With any bid below 100, Ayşe loses and gets 0. To win she has to exceed 100; she would then pay Bora’s bid of 100 and her utility would be 10 − 100 = −90. Ayşe’s deviation is not profitable.
  3. Can Bora deviate? Bora is currently winning for free and gets 5. No bid can give him more than 5, since his value for the item is 5 and the payment cannot fall below 0.
  4. Conclusion. No one wants to deviate alone: the bids (0, 100) form a Nash equilibrium. But the item goes to Bora, who has the lower value; the outcome is not efficient.

This bad equilibrium does not contradict the mechanism being DSIC. The truthful bid is still a dominant strategy for both players. The bad equilibrium is built from weakly dominated bids: Bora’s bid of 100 is never better than the truthful bid of 5 and in some cases (for example if Ayşe bids 50) is worse. Ayşe’s bid of 0 is likewise weakly dominated by the truthful bid of 10.

The lesson is twofold. In DSIC mechanisms a player need not guess the rival; honesty is a safe choice in every case. DSIC designs are therefore more robust than designs that rest only on Nash equilibrium. Even so, DSIC gives no guarantee of a unique equilibrium. If players do not play their dominant strategies, odd equilibria can arise.

4.2.9Single-parameter settings: Myerson’s lemma

In many important problems each player’s private information is a single number. In selling a single item, this number is the value placed on the item. When several identical items are sold and each player wants at most one, a single number again suffices. Settings of this kind are called single-parameter environments.

In a single-parameter environment, each player has a unit value vi. From the reports b, the mechanism chooses for each player an allocation xi(b) and a payment pi(b). The allocation says how much “good” the player receives: with a single item it is 0 or 1; if the item is given by lottery, it can be a probability between 0 and 1. The player’s utility is vi · xi − pi.

Myerson (1981) proved a result that characterizes DSIC mechanisms in single-parameter environments exactly. The result answers two questions: which allocation rules can be used in a DSIC mechanism, and what must the payments then be?

Definition 4.6

An allocation rule x is monotone if, for every player i and every report profile b−i of the others, xi(z, b−i) is non-decreasing in z. That is, a player who raises their bid must not receive less of the good.

Theorem 4.4 (Myerson’s lemma)

In a single-parameter environment:

  1. An allocation rule x can be used in a DSIC mechanism if and only if it is monotone.
  2. If x is monotone, there is a unique payment rule that makes the mechanism DSIC subject to pi(0, b−i) = 0:

pi(b) = bi · xi(b) − ∫0bi xi(z, b−i) dz

The integral sign (∫) in the formula may look intimidating, but here it only expresses an area. ∫0bi xi(z, b−i) dz is the area under the curve xi on the horizontal axis from 0 to bi. In all the examples in this chapter the curve is a step function. The area under a step curve is found by adding up the areas of rectangles: width × height for each rectangle.

The geometric reading of the formula is as follows. bi · xi(b) is the area of the rectangle with width bi and height xi(b). When the area under the curve is subtracted from this rectangle, what remains is the area to the left of the curve, between heights 0 and x(b). The payment is this shaded area. The area under the curve, in turn, is the player’s utility: under honest reporting, vi xi − pi = ∫0vi xi(z) dz.

Example 4.9

A single item (left of the figure). Let the highest bid of the others be 70. If the player bids above 70, they get the item (x = 1); if they bid below, they do not (x = 0). The player’s bid is b = 85.

  1. b · x(b) = 85 · 1 = 85.
  2. Area under the curve: between 0 and 70 the height is 0, so the area is 0; between 70 and 85 the height is 1, so the area is 15 · 1 = 15. The total is 15.
  3. The payment is 85 − 15 = 70. The utility is 85 − 70 = 15, which is the area under the curve.

A step allocation (right of the figure). Let the allocation be 0 below a bid of 3, 0.4 between 3 and 5, and 1 from 5 on. The bid is b = 6.

  1. b · x(b) = 6 · 1 = 6.
  2. Area under the curve: 0 on [0, 3); 2 · 0.4 = 0.8 on [3, 5); 1 · 1 = 1 on [5, 6]. The total is 1.8.
  3. The payment is 6 − 1.8 = 4.2.
  4. The same result can be found by a shorter route. For a step allocation, the payment is the sum, over all jumps, of the size of the jump times the bid at which it occurs: 0.4 · 3 + 0.6 · 5 = 1.2 + 3 = 4.2.

The reason for the step formula is this: each jump in the allocation is an extra “piece of the good” that the player obtains by crossing that threshold. The player pays exactly the threshold for each unit of that piece. By crossing 3 the player gets 0.4 units of the good and pays 3 for each unit; by crossing 5 the player gets 0.6 units more and pays 5 for each unit.

The critical bid. If the allocation can only be 0 or 1, there is a single jump. The payment is then the lowest bid the player needs in order to win. This bid is called the critical bid. In selling a single item, the critical bid is the highest bid of the others. Myerson’s lemma therefore says: for a single item, the only DSIC payment rule that gives the item to the highest bidder is the Vickrey rule, in which the winner pays the second-highest bid (losers pay 0).

What if the payment is not the critical bid? Let the threshold be 50 and the winner’s payment a fixed 20. A player with value 35 who bids honestly stays below the threshold and gets 0. If the player bids above 50, they get the item and pay 20; the utility is 35 − 20 = 15. Overstating paid off. In general, every player whose value lies between the payment and the threshold (here between 20 and 50) wants to overstate; IC fails. Had the payment been larger than the threshold, winners whose value lies between the threshold and the payment would lose money and would want to lower their bids below the threshold.

Why is monotonicity necessary? A short argument shows it. The honest report of a player with value y must be no worse than reporting z; the honest report of a player with value z must likewise be no worse than reporting y:

y · x(y) − p(y) ≥ y · x(z) − p(z) and z · x(z) − p(z) ≥ z · x(y) − p(y)

Adding the two inequalities side by side cancels the payments and leaves y · x(y) + z · x(z) ≥ y · x(z) + z · x(y). This can be written as (z − y)(x(z) − x(y)) ≥ 0. If z > y, we must have x(z) ≥ x(y): a higher bid cannot receive a smaller allocation. So DSIC forces monotonicity. The other direction of the lemma, that every monotone allocation is DSIC with the payment in the formula, is proved step by step in Roughgarden’s (2016) lecture notes.

Common mistake

Mistaking the Myerson payment for the area under the curve. The area under the curve is the player’s utility. The payment is what remains when this area is subtracted from the rectangle b · x(b): the area to the left of the curve. In the single-item example the area under the curve is 15 (the utility) and the area to the left is 70 (the payment).

4.3The VCG mechanism

The failure of proportional cost sharing (4.2.6) left us with an idea for a solution: a player’s report should affect their payment only by changing the chosen outcome. The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism turns this idea into a general rule. The mechanism combines William Vickrey’s (1961) single-item auction, Edward Clarke’s (1971) work on public goods, and Theodore Groves’s (1973) general framework.

4.3.1Efficient choice and the Clarke pivot payment

VCG works in two steps. In the first step the outcome is chosen; in the second, the payments are computed. The choice of outcome is efficient: the outcome with the highest social welfare under the reported values is selected. The payment equals the harm that each player’s presence does to the others.

Definition 4.7

The VCG mechanism (Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism) asks each player for the value placed on every possible outcome; bi(x) is the value that player i reports for outcome x. The mechanism chooses the outcome

x* = argmaxx Σi bi(x)

and charges each player according to the Clarke pivot rule:

pi = maxx Σj≠i bj(x) − Σj≠i bj(x*)

It helps to read the two formulas in words. argmax returns the option that gives the largest value, while max returns the largest value. x* is the outcome that maximizes the sum of the values reported by all players. In the payment formula, Σj≠i means “sum over all players other than i”. The formula has two parts:

  • First term: if i were absent, the other players would choose the best outcome among themselves and would get this much welfare from it. In short, “the others’ best welfare without i”.
  • Second term: the welfare the others get at the outcome x* chosen when i is present. In short, “the others’ welfare with i”.

The difference is the externality that i’s participation imposes on the others. An externality is the harm (or benefit) that one person’s decision causes to others without that person paying for it. A factory polluting the air is a negative externality. VCG makes each player pay for the negative externality they create. If a player does not change the outcome in their own favor, the first and second terms are equal and the player pays nothing. A player who changes the outcome, the “pivot” player, pays the cost of that change to the others. This is where the rule gets its name.

Figure 4.10 shows this idea with a pair of columns. The left column shows the welfare the others would get without Bora, and the right column the welfare they get with Bora. Bora’s presence shifts the choice from the Park to the Gym and lowers the others’ welfare from 50 to 45. Bora pays for this loss of 5 units. All the numbers of this example are in 4.3.2.

Why is VCG DSIC? The first term of the payment formula depends only on the others’ reports; it does not change whatever i reports. Call this term hi. Let the others report b−i and let i’s report lead to outcome x. Player i’s true utility is then:

ui = vi(x) − pi = vi(x) + Σj≠i bj(x) − hi

Theorem 4.5 (Incentive compatibility of VCG)

In the VCG mechanism, honest reporting is a dominant strategy for every player. Clarke pivot payments are never negative; if values are also non-negative, the mechanism is individually rational.

DSIC. The last term of the utility above, hi, does not depend on i’s report. To maximize utility, i therefore wants the outcome x that maximizes vi(x) + Σj≠i bj(x). This sum is i’s true value plus the sum of the others’ reports. The mechanism chooses the outcome that maximizes bi(x) + Σj≠i bj(x). If i reports honestly (bi = vi), the two sums are identical and the mechanism chooses exactly the outcome i most wants. A false report can lead the mechanism either to the same outcome or to an outcome that makes the sum i wants smaller. No lie can do better than honesty, and this holds whatever the others report.

Payments are non-negative. The first term is the largest welfare the others can get over all outcomes. The second term is the welfare the others get at one particular outcome (x*). A maximum cannot be smaller than any particular value; hence pi ≥ 0.

IR. Under honest reporting, ui = Σj vj(x*) − maxx Σj≠i vj(x). Let x' be the others’ best outcome without i. Since x* maximizes total welfare, Σj vj(x*) ≥ Σj vj(x') = vi(x') + Σj≠i vj(x'). Since values are non-negative, vi(x') ≥ 0, and so ui ≥ 0. □

The main idea of the proof can be put in one sentence: in VCG, a player’s utility is social welfare minus a term that is independent of that player. Every player therefore wants social welfare to be as large as possible, and the mechanism does exactly that. The player’s interest and society’s interest are aligned.

Interpreting the results reinforces the logic of the mechanism. Ayşe wanted the Park most, but her preference did not change the outcome; Ayşe pays nothing. Bora and Cem are each pivotal on their own: without either of them the Park would be chosen. So both pay. Cem’s payment is larger because his presence changes the choice in a more costly way: without Cem, Ayşe and Bora would get 40 from the Park, whereas with the Gym they get only 20.

The 25 units collected are not redistributed to anyone. This means VCG produces a budget surplus. If the money were returned to the residents, the incentives could break, because the shares returned would depend on the players’ reports. Since payments are non-negative, the mechanism needs no outside money: weak budget balance holds, strong budget balance does not.

Common mistake

Taking “the others’ best welfare with i present” in step (c). Step (c) is always computed at x*, the project chosen with everyone; not at the project the others like best at that moment. For Bora, (c) is the 45 at the Gym, not the 50 at the Park. Another common mistake is to leave i’s own value in the sum in step (b).

4.3.3Code trace: vcg()

The whole VCG computation can be written as a short Python function. The function takes two inputs. v is a dictionary: v[i][a] is the value player i reports for outcome a. A is the list of possible outcomes. The function returns the chosen outcome and the payments.

def vcg(v, A):
  N = list(v)            # players
  def w(a, S):           # welfare of S at a
    return sum(v[i][a] for i in S)
  x = max(A, key=lambda a: w(a, N))
  p = {}
  for i in N:
    D = [j for j in N if j != i]
    w_out = max(w(a, D) for a in A)  # i absent
    w_in = w(x, D)                  # i present
    p[i] = w_out - w_in
  return x, p

The code works line by line as follows:

  • N = list(v) extracts the list of players from the dictionary keys: ['Ayşe', 'Bora', 'Cem'].
  • The inner function w(a, S) is the sum of the values that the players in the set S place on outcome a. w(a, N) is everyone’s welfare, and w(a, D) is the welfare of the set D alone.
  • x = max(A, key=lambda a: w(a, N)) picks, among the outcomes in A, the one that maximizes w(a, N). This is the Python counterpart of the argmax operation.
  • p = {} is an empty payment dictionary.
  • In the loop, for each player i, D is the list of players other than i. w_out is the largest welfare of D over all outcomes: the first term of the payment formula. w_in is the welfare of D at the chosen outcome x: the second term. The payment is w_out − w_in.

In the public project example the input is v = {'Ayşe': {'P': 40, 'L': 5, 'G': 0}, 'Bora': {'P': 0, 'L': 30, 'G': 20}, 'Cem': {'P': 10, 'L': 20, 'G': 45}} and A = ['P', 'L', 'G']. Table 4.4 shows each step of the loop.

Two details about the code matter. First, on ties max picks the outcome that comes first in the list; this fixed tie-breaking rule does not affect DSIC. Second, for |A| outcomes and n players, the code does |A| · n additions to find x, and repeats about the same work for each player’s payment. The total work is O(n² · |A|). This is very fast when the set of outcomes is small. But when the outcomes are combinatorially numerous, for example when they are all possible ways of distributing 30 items among the players, |A| grows astronomically. This problem is taken up in 4.3.6.

  3. Bora reports 60 for the Library. The totals are Park 50, Library 5 + 60 + 20 = 85, Gym 65; the Library is chosen. Bora’s payment: without Bora the Park would be chosen and the others would get 50; at the Library the others get 5 + 20 = 25. The payment is 50 − 25 = 25. Bora’s true value for the Library is 30; his utility is 30 − 25 = 5 < 15.

Bora’s lie is interesting. Bora really did prefer the Library to the Gym (30 > 20). By lying he got his favorite project built. But the price rose by exactly the harm he did to the others: the Library is 20 units worse for the others than the Gym. Bora’s gain (10 more units of value at the Library) does not cover this price. This is exactly how VCG works: changing the outcome is allowed, but the cost of the change to others is paid. If the change is not good for society, it is not good for the liar either.

These four cases are only examples. A wider experiment can also be run on a computer: let one player report each of 0, 10, …, 100 for each project while the other two stay honest. Under none of these reports can any player obtain higher utility than by reporting honestly. This is a numerical check of Theorem 4.5.

4.3.5VCG for a single item: the Vickrey auction

VCG is a general mechanism; selling a single item is a special case. In this special case, VCG can be shown to reduce to exactly the second-price (Vickrey) auction. To see this, the same vcg() function is applied to a single item.

The general reason for the result is clear. Without the winner, the item would go to the player with the second-highest value, and the others’ welfare would be that second-highest value. With the winner present, the others get nothing. The difference is the second-highest value. If a losing player were absent, the item would still go to the same person; the loser’s externality is zero. This result is also consistent with Myerson’s lemma (4.2.9): for a single item, the DSIC payment is the critical bid, and the critical bid is the second-highest bid.

4.3.6Properties and limits of VCG

VCG is a very attractive mechanism in theory. Its strengths are:

  • DSIC: honest reporting is a dominant strategy; players need not guess one another.
  • Efficiency: the outcome with the highest social welfare is chosen.
  • IR and non-negative payments: with the Clarke pivot and non-negative values, no participant loses out and the mechanism never pays anyone.
  • Generality: for a single item it reduces to the Vickrey auction, and for several identical items to the rule “pay the highest value among the losers”.

Nevertheless, VCG is rarely used in practice. There are several reasons:

  • It is not strongly budget balanced. In the public project example 25 units are left over. The mechanism runs no deficit (weak budget balance), but it cannot redistribute the surplus to the players. In public good examples this money is as if “burned”.
  • Computation can be hard. Computing the argmax can be NP-hard in combinatorial problems with many outcomes. For example, finding the best allocation in an auction in which many items are distributed among bidders who each want different bundles of items belongs to the class of hardest known optimization problems. If the computation is approximate rather than exact, VCG’s incentive properties can fail.
  • Collusion and false identities. If players collude secretly, or if one player takes part under several false identities, lying can become profitable. VCG’s incentive guarantee covers only individual deviations.
  • Revenue can be low. Because payments equal externalities, the seller’s revenue can be very low, even zero, when there is little competition.
  • Privacy. Players must reveal the value they place on every outcome. A company may not want to reveal to its rivals and to the seller the highest price it is willing to pay for a product.
Example 4.13

In a single-item Vickrey auction let the values be Ayşe 70, Bora 55, Cem 85. With honest bids Cem wins and pays 70.

  1. Ayşe and Cem secretly agree: Ayşe bids 0 and Cem stays honest.
  2. The bids are 0, 55, 85. Cem still wins, but the second-highest bid is now Bora’s 55. Cem pays 55.
  3. Cem’s utility rises from 85 − 70 = 15 to 85 − 55 = 30. Ayşe got 0 when honest too; she loses nothing from the agreement. The 15 units gained can be shared between them.

If Ayşe alone had lowered her bid, her own utility would not have changed (0). The DSIC definition therefore does not rule out this deviation. But two players deviating together gain as a group. The seller’s revenue falls from 70 to 55.

Because of these limits, simpler rules are preferred over VCG in real applications. For example, sponsored search ads mostly use the generalized second price (GSP) rule: ads are ranked by their bids and each advertiser pays the bid of the one directly below. GSP is not DSIC, but it is simple and advantageous in terms of revenue. VCG keeps its importance as a theoretical reference point: it shows whether an efficient and incentive-compatible outcome is possible in a problem, and at what cost.

4.4Mechanisms without money

In the earlier mechanisms, incentives were corrected with payments. VCG made honesty profitable by making each player pay for the externality they create. But in many important problems using money is either forbidden or inappropriate. The main mechanisms that work without money are: Gale–Shapley for stable matching, the median rule for facility location, serial dictatorship and the Boston mechanism for school placement, and TTC for room swaps.

4.4.1Design without money

In school placement, students are not asked for money to enter a school. In dorm room swaps, students do not pay one another. In an election, votes cannot be bought. In organ transplantation, buying and selling organs is prohibited. In these settings the designer has only one tool left: the outcome rule. Incentives cannot be corrected with payments; the rule must be chosen so that telling the truth does not make the outcome worse for the player.

The term used for incentive compatibility without money is strategy-proofness. If a rule is strategy-proof, no player, whatever the others report, can obtain a better outcome by reporting something other than their true preferences. This is the counterpart of DSIC in a setting without money.

For general preferences there is bad news. Gibbard (1973) and Satterthwaite (1975), independently of each other, showed the following:

Theorem 4.6 (Gibbard–Satterthwaite)

In a setting with at least three alternatives, in which every alternative can be the outcome at some preference profile and players can rank the alternatives in any order, every strategy-proof rule is a dictatorship: the outcome is always the favorite alternative of the same single person.

A dictatorship is of course not an acceptable rule. The theorem says that, for general preferences, there is no reasonable voting rule that guarantees honesty. Mechanism design without money therefore proceeds by relaxing one of the theorem’s assumptions. There are two ways out:

  • Use the structure of preferences. If preferences cannot be arbitrary rankings, the theorem does not apply. For example, if the alternatives are arranged on a line and each player has a single favorite point (single-peaked preferences), the median rule is strategy-proof (4.4.7).
  • Change the goal. Instead of looking for the “best” alternative, we can look for another criterion. In two-sided markets the goal is stability (Gale–Shapley). When everyone holds a good, the goal is trade (TTC).

4.4.2The stable matching problem

A department has three students (A, B, C) and three capstone projects (X, Y, Z). Each project takes one student. Each student ranks the projects, and each project’s supervisor ranks the students, in order of preference. The preference orders are given in Table 4.7. The notation “X ≻ Y ≻ Z” means “prefers X to Y and Y to Z”.

A matching is a list that assigns each student to a different project. For three students and three projects there are 3! = 3 · 2 · 1 = 6 different matchings. The question of which of them is “good” is answered by asking whether a matching would fall apart.

Definition 4.8

In a matching, a student–project pair who prefer each other to their current partners is called a blocking pair. A matching with no blocking pair is called a stable matching.

A blocking pair can undermine the matching. Both the student and the supervisor are unhappy with the current situation and want each other; they can set the rule aside and make a deal among themselves. In a stable matching, no one wants to break the arrangement and walk away. For a pair to be blocking, two conditions must hold together: the student must prefer the project to their current project and the project must prefer the student to its current student. If only one of them wants it, the pair is not blocking.

Example 4.14

Is the matching {A–X, B–Y, C–Z} stable? Of the nine student–project pairs, the six that are not in the current matching are checked.

  1. A. A’s first choice is X, and A already has X. A prefers no project to X; there is no blocking pair involving A.
  2. (B, X). B prefers X to the current project Y (X ≻ Z ≻ Y). X prefers B to its current student A (B ≻ A ≻ C). Blocking.
  3. (B, Z). B prefers Z to Y. Z prefers B to its current student C (A ≻ B ≻ C). Blocking.
  4. (C, Y). C prefers Y to the current project Z (Y ≻ X ≻ Z). Y prefers C to its current student B (A ≻ C ≻ B). Blocking.
  5. (C, X). C prefers X to Z, but X prefers its current student A to C. Not blocking.

The matching has three blocking pairs; it is not stable. Checking all nine pairs turns up no other blocking pair.

Does a stable matching always exist? If so, how is one found? Checking all six matchings is possible in a small example. But for n students there are n! matchings; for 20 students this number exceeds 2 · 1018. A cleverer method is needed. Gale and Shapley (1962) gave an algorithm that answers both questions at once.

4.4.3Gale–Shapley: deferred acceptance

In the Gale–Shapley algorithm, one side makes proposals and the other side evaluates them. Here the students propose and the projects accept or reject. The algorithm proceeds in rounds:

  1. Each free student proposes to their best project that has not yet rejected them.
  2. Each project tentatively keeps the best of the incoming proposals and the candidate it already holds, and rejects the others.
  3. Rejected students become free. When no free student remains, the algorithm stops and the tentative matches become final.

The algorithm’s other name is deferred acceptance. The name comes from the word “tentatively” in the second step. When a project accepts a proposal it makes no firm commitment; if a better candidate comes along it can drop the earlier one. Firm acceptance is deferred to the end of the algorithm. This detail is the key to why the algorithm produces a stable result.

Example 4.15

With the preferences in Table 4.7, the algorithm runs as follows.

  1. Start. Everyone is free and the projects are empty.
  2. Round 1 proposals. Each student proposes to their first choice: A→X, B→X, C→Y.
  3. Round 1 decision. X receives two proposals. X’s order is B ≻ A ≻ C; B is kept and A is rejected. Only C proposes to Y; C is tentatively accepted.
  4. Round 2 proposals. Only A is free. X rejected A; the next choice is Y: A→Y.
  5. Round 2 decision. Y holds C, and now A arrives. Y’s order is A ≻ C ≻ B; A is better. Y keeps A and rejects C. C, who had been “accepted” earlier, is now free: the acceptance was tentative.
  6. Round 3 proposals. Y rejected C; the next choice is X: C→X.
  7. Round 3 decision. X holds B. X prefers B to C; C is rejected.
  8. Round 4. C’s next choice is Z: C→Z. Z is empty; C is accepted.
  9. End. Everyone is matched and the algorithm stops. Result: A–Y, B–X, C–Z.

Stability check. B has the first choice X; no complaint. A is with Y and prefers only X to Y, but X prefers B to A. C is with Z and prefers X and Y to Z, but X prefers B to C, and Y prefers A to C. There is no blocking pair: the matching is stable.

Stability does not mean “everyone gets their favorite”. C was left with the last choice Z, because X and Y, which C wanted more, are matched to students they like better than C.

4.4.4Code trace: gale_shapley()

The Python version of the algorithm works with a queue instead of rounds. At each step one free student leaves the queue and makes a proposal. As input, the dictionary stu holds each student’s preference list (best to worst) and the dictionary proj holds the rank each project gives to the students: proj[p][s] is the rank that project p gives to student s, where 0 means best. For example, proj['X'] = {'B': 0, 'A': 1, 'C': 2}.

def gale_shapley(stu, proj):
  free, match = list(stu), {}
  nxt = {s: 0 for s in stu}
  while free:
    s = free.pop(0)
    p = stu[s][nxt[s]]        # next choice
    nxt[s] += 1
    r = match.get(p)              # p’s holder
    if r is None or proj[p][s] < proj[p][r]:
      match[p] = s                # accept
      if r is not None: free.append(r)
    else: free.append(s)    # reject
  return match

The parts of the code are:

  • free is the queue of free students; at the start everyone is in it. The dictionary match stores the student each project is tentatively holding.
  • nxt[s] is the index of the next project in student s’s preference list. It starts at 0, the most preferred project.
  • In the loop, the student s at the head of the queue is removed, proposes to the next choice p, and nxt[s] is incremented. A student therefore never proposes to the same project twice.
  • r is the candidate p currently holds; it is None if the project is empty.
  • If the project is empty, or if p prefers s to r (the rank number is smaller), s is accepted. If there was an old candidate r, it returns to the queue.
  • Otherwise s is rejected and goes back to the queue.

Although the proposals are processed in a different order (in the rounds A and B propose at the same time, while in the code they propose one after the other), the result does not change. The result of Gale–Shapley does not depend on the order in which proposals are processed; this is a well-known property of the algorithm.

How many proposals? Each student proposes to each project at most once, because nxt[s] only increases. For n students and n projects the total number of proposals is at most n · n = n². If each proposal is processed in constant time, the running time of the algorithm is O(n²). (To keep the code short it uses list.pop(0); this operation shifts the list and so is not constant time. For O(n²), use collections.deque and popleft().) For an example with 20 students, at most 400 proposals suffice; this is incomparably faster than checking the 20! matchings one by one.

4.4.5Properties of Gale–Shapley

Theorem 4.7 (Gale and Shapley, 1962)

The Gale–Shapley algorithm terminates on every input and returns a stable matching. Hence, when the numbers of students and projects are equal, a stable matching always exists.

Termination. Each proposal uses up one student–project pair, and there are at most n² pairs. The algorithm therefore stops after at most n² proposals. It is also impossible for a student to be rejected by every project: once a project has received a proposal, it always holds someone; if a student had been rejected by all projects, all projects would be full, but since the number of projects equals the number of students, this student would then have to be in a project as well.

Stability. Suppose that at the end student s prefers project p to their own partner. Because s proposes following their preference order, s proposed to p before reaching their partner and was rejected by p. A project rejects a student only when it holds a better candidate. The candidate a project holds only improves over time. So at the end p’s partner is better than s in p’s eyes, and p does not want s. (s, p) cannot be a blocking pair. Since this holds for every pair, the matching is stable. □

The algorithm’s other important properties are:

  • Best for the proposing side. A problem may have more than one stable matching. In Gale–Shapley every proposing player gets the best partner they can get in any stable matching. The receiving side, in turn, gets the worst partner it can get in any stable matching. Which side proposes is therefore an important design decision.
  • DSIC for the proposers. For the proposing side, reporting the true preference order is a dominant strategy (Roth, 1982). The receiving side can sometimes gain by lying (4.4.6).
  • Applications. In the United States, the placement of medical school graduates into residency positions (NRMP) is carried out with a version of this algorithm. The Boston and New York school choice systems are also based on deferred acceptance. Roth and Shapley received the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economics for this work.

When all six matchings are tried with the preferences in the example, only one stable matching is found: A–Y, B–X, C–Z. So the result would be the same if the projects proposed. When the projects propose, X’s first choice is B, and Y’s and Z’s first choice is A. A keeps Y between Y and Z; Z is rejected and proposes to its next choice B. B rejects Z because B prefers X to Z; Z finally proposes to C. The result is again A–Y, B–X, C–Z.

Common mistake

Implementing deferred acceptance as “first come, first served”. Y’s acceptance of C in round 1 is not final; when A arrives in round 2, C is dropped. An implementation that treats acceptance as final (for example, Y keeps C and rejects A) can lead to an unstable matching: A and Y want each other but stay apart.

4.4.6The receiving side can gain by lying

Gale–Shapley is DSIC for the proposers but not for the receiving side. A new example shows this. The students propose again. The preferences are: A: X ≻ Y ≻ Z, B: Y ≻ X ≻ Z, C: X ≻ Y ≻ Z. The projects’ true preferences are X: B ≻ A ≻ C; Y and Z: A ≻ B ≻ C.

Example 4.16

Honest reporting.

  1. A→X: X is empty, A is held. B→Y: Y is empty, B is held.
  2. C→X: X prefers A to C; C is rejected. C→Y: Y prefers B to C; C is rejected. C→Z: accepted.
  3. Result: A–X, B–Y, C–Z. X gets A, who is 2nd in its true order.

X lies and reports the order B ≻ C ≻ A; it puts A last.

  1. A→X: X is empty, A is held. B→Y: B is held.
  2. C→X: in the order X reported, C is better than A. X keeps C and rejects A.
  3. A→Y: Y prefers A to B. A is held, B is rejected.
  4. B→X: X prefers B to C. B is held, C is rejected.
  5. C→Y: Y prefers A to C; C is rejected. C→Z: accepted.
  6. Result: A–Y, B–X, C–Z. X now has its true 1st choice B. The lie paid off.

How the lie works can be seen by comparing Figure 4.17 with Figure 4.18. By rejecting A, X starts a rejection chain. The rejected A goes to Y and takes B’s place there. B, displaced, comes around to its next choice X. X’s lie has built a chain that brings the student it wants back to itself.

Checking the six matchings shows that these preferences have two stable matchings: A–X, B–Y, C–Z, which is best for the students, and A–Y, B–X, C–Z, which is best for the projects. Student-proposing Gale–Shapley finds the first. X’s lie moves the result to the second, the one best for the projects. This is a special case of a general result: no stable mechanism can be DSIC for both sides at once (Roth, 1982). The designer has to choose which side to guarantee honesty for. In school placement the students are usually made the proposing side; the schools’ preferences mostly come from legal priority rules and cannot be changed strategically.

4.4.7Facility location: mean or median?

Five villages, A, B, C, D, and E, lie on a straight road at kilometers 2, 5, 9, 14, and 20, respectively. A single facility, for example a hospital, is to be built along the road. Each village wants the facility to be close to it; a village’s cost is its distance to the facility. The villages’ locations are treated as private information: each village reports its own location (or its most preferred point) and the facility is placed according to the reports.

The preferences in this setting have a special structure. Each village has a single favorite point on the road (its own location), and its satisfaction falls as the facility moves away from that point. Preferences of this kind are called single-peaked preferences: the satisfaction graph has a single peak. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem does not assume this structure; a strategy-proof rule that is not a dictatorship can therefore be found here.

Two rules are compared. The mean mechanism places the facility at the average of the reports. The median mechanism sorts the reports and picks the middle one. With five reports, the median is the third number in the sorted order.

  1. Mean, everyone honest. (2 + 5 + 9 + 14 + 20)/5 = 50/5 = 10. The facility is built at km 10; E’s distance is 20 − 10 = 10 km.
  2. Mean, E lies. E reports its location as 40: (2 + 5 + 9 + 14 + 40)/5 = 70/5 = 14. The facility moves to km 14; E’s distance falls to 20 − 14 = 6 km. The lie paid off: the mean can be manipulated.
  3. E’s most profitable lie. If E wants to pull the facility exactly to its own location, the mean must be 20: (2 + 5 + 9 + 14 + x)/5 = 20, that is, 30 + x = 100 and x = 70. By reporting 70, E reduces its distance to 0.
  4. Median, everyone honest. The sorted reports are 2, 5, 9, 14, 20; the middle one is 9. E’s distance is 20 − 9 = 11 km.
  5. Median, E reports 40. The order becomes 2, 5, 9, 14, 40; the median is still 9. An extreme value cannot pull the median; E’s distance stays at 11.
  6. Median, E reports 8. If E crosses to the other side of the median, the order becomes 2, 5, 8, 9, 14 and the median falls to 8. E’s distance rises to 20 − 8 = 12 km. The lie did harm.

The weakness of the mean is that every report affects the result directly. If a village raises its report by 5 units, the mean rises by 1 unit. A village at the edge can pull the mean wherever it wants by exaggerating its location. The median, on the other hand, looks only at the ranking. A village to the right of the median stays on the right however much it inflates its report, and the middle element of the ranking does not change.

The median has another advantage. The total distance is 7 + 4 + 0 + 5 + 11 = 27 at the median and 8 + 5 + 1 + 4 + 10 = 28 at the mean. The median is the point that minimizes the total distance. The reason is this: if the facility is at a point to the right of the median, moving it one unit to the left brings each village on the left one unit closer and takes it one unit farther from each village on the right. To the right of the median, more villages are on the left than on the right, so the move reduces the total. The same argument works from the left. The total can be reduced no further only at the median. So the median is both strategy-proof and best in terms of total cost. When all reports between 0 and 60 are tried for each village on a computer, no lie is found to pay off under the median.

4.4.8Why is the median strategyproof?

Theorem 4.8 (Median mechanism)

With single-peaked preferences (here: each village wants to minimize its distance to the facility), the median mechanism (the middle report when the number of villages is odd; a predetermined one of the two middle reports, for example the left one, when it is even) is strategy-proof: for each village, reporting its true location is a dominant strategy (Moulin, 1980).

The proof consists of three cases, depending on where the village stands relative to the median. Let village i’s true location be xi, the median when the others are honest m, and the village’s false report r. The argument holds whatever the others report; to make it concrete, the villages of the example are used.

Case 1: xi < m. In the example B = 5 and the median is 9. When B is honest the distance is 9 − 5 = 4. Any report by B that is less than or equal to 9 keeps B to the left of (or at) the median in the ranking; the median stays at 9 and the distance is 4. A report by B greater than 9 moves B to the right of the median; the median then shifts right (to B’s report or at most to 14) and away from B. No report by B can pull the median to the left, toward B, because there are already B and A to the left of the median, and B changing its place there does not change the middle of the ranking. No r can bring the distance below 4.

Case 2: xi > m. In the example D = 14. This case is the mirror image of the first. Any report by D greater than or equal to 9 leaves the median unchanged; the distance stays at 14 − 9 = 5. A report by D less than 9 pulls the median left, away from D.

Case 3: xi = m. In the example C = 9. The facility is exactly at C’s location and the distance is 0. This is the smallest possible distance; no lie can do better. Any lie can only move the median away from C or leave it in place.

In all three cases the honest report gives the best result, whatever the others report. The median mechanism is strategy-proof. □

In all three graphs the distance curve never falls below the honest report: the flat parts are reports where the lie changes nothing, and the rising parts are reports where the lie does harm.

The same idea carries over to voting. If the alternatives can be ordered along a line (for example candidates on a left–right axis, or budget sizes) and each voter has a single-peaked preference, the median voter’s favorite alternative beats every other alternative in a pairwise vote. This result is known as Duncan Black’s median voter theorem. One way to escape the bad news of Gibbard–Satterthwaite is to recognize the cases in which preferences have this structure.

Common mistake

Taking the median to be “the average of the two middle ones” when the number of villages is even. This brings back the manipulability of the mean: one of the two middle villages can change its report and pull the facility toward itself. For strategy-proofness, with an even number of reports the rule must pick one of the two middle ones (for example the left one).

4.4.9Serial dictatorship and the Boston mechanism

In school placement, students rank schools and each school has limited capacity. Schools may also have priority orders over students: for example, those who live near the school or have a sibling there may get priority. Two simple mechanisms create very different incentives in this setting.

Serial dictatorship. Students are lined up in an order. The student whose turn it is takes their favorite among the remaining schools. A student cannot affect the choices of those before them, and their own choice depends only on their own preference list. Reporting true preferences is therefore a dominant strategy: the mechanism is DSIC. The outcome is also Pareto optimal: no other assignment can make someone better off without making anyone worse off. If the order is determined by lottery, the mechanism is called random serial dictatorship (RSD). It differs from the dictatorship of Gibbard–Satterthwaite in that the “dictator” is not a single person; the order gives everyone a turn to choose.

The Boston mechanism, or immediate acceptance. In each round, unplaced students apply to the next school on their list. Each school gives its open seats to the students applying to it in that round, in priority order. A seat once given is not taken back in later rounds. This is where it differs from deferred acceptance: acceptance is final, not tentative. A student who loses their first choice may therefore find their second choice already full when applying to it.

In the example there are three students (A, B, C) and three schools (X, Y, Z) with one seat each. The preferences are A: X ≻ Y ≻ Z, B: X ≻ Y ≻ Z, C: Y ≻ X ≻ Z. The priorities are A, B, C for X and Z, and B, A, C for Y.

The reason for the lie is final acceptance. If B guesses that it cannot get X anyway, it does not want to “waste” the first round, because in the first round Y’s seat will be filled. Under the Boston mechanism, students have to guess their chances and arrange their preference lists accordingly. This gives an advantage to families with access to information and advice, while families who write their preferences as they are may lose out.

On the same profile, student-proposing Gale–Shapley with honest reports gives A–X, B–Y, C–Z. In round 1, A and B propose to X and C to Y; X keeps A and rejects B. B proposes to Y; since B is ahead of C in Y’s priority order, B is held and C is rejected. C loses to A at X and is placed at Z. Under deferred acceptance, C’s seat at Y in round 1 was tentative, so B can get Y by staying honest. Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez (2003) showed that writing preferences as they are can hurt a student under the Boston mechanism, and brought the mechanism design viewpoint to school choice. The city of Boston switched to student-proposing deferred acceptance for school placement in 2005.

4.4.10Swapping dorm rooms: TTC

This time each student holds a dorm room and no money is used. Students rank other students’ rooms along with their own. The goal is to find a redistribution, through voluntary swaps, in which everyone is at least as well off as with what they hold. This problem is called the housing market problem. The problem was defined by Shapley and Scarf (1974), who attributed the following algorithm to David Gale.

The Top Trading Cycles (TTC) algorithm works as follows:

  1. Each remaining person points to the owner of their favorite room among the remaining rooms (draws an arrow). If they like their own room best, they point to themselves.
  2. Since exactly one arrow leaves each person, this graph contains at least one cycle. Everyone in a cycle gets the room they point to and leaves the market with their room.
  3. Repeat until no one is left.

The claim about a cycle in the second step rests on a simple observation. Start from any person and follow the arrows; since there are finitely many people, at some point we return to someone already visited. This return forms a cycle. A cycle can also consist of a single person: a person who points to their own room keeps it.

  2. Round 1, cycle. Start at A and follow the arrows: A→C, C→B, B→A. We are back at A; the cycle is A → C → B → A.
  3. Round 1, trade. Everyone in the cycle gets the room they point to: A gets C’s room, C gets B’s room, B gets A’s room. All three got their first choice and leave with their rooms. The arrows of D and E (to C and to A) were not in the cycle; they stay.
  4. Round 2, arrows. D and E remain. D’s first choice, C, is gone; the next is E: D→E. E’s first choice, A, is gone; the next is D: E→D.
  5. Round 2, cycle and trade. D → E → D is a cycle. D gets E’s room and E gets D’s room. No one is left.
  6. Result. A ← C’s room, B ← A’s room, C ← B’s room, D ← E’s room, E ← D’s room. A, B, and C traded in a three-way cycle; D and E in a two-way cycle.

TTC has three important properties:

  • DSIC. Reporting true preferences is a dominant strategy for every person (Roth, 1982). A person gets their favorite among the remaining rooms in the round in which they enter a cycle. The rooms of those who left earlier cannot be changed by this person’s report, because those cycles formed independently of the person’s arrow.
  • Pareto optimality. No other assignment can make someone better off without making anyone worse off. Those who left in the first round got their favorite room; improving on that is impossible. Those who left in a later round got their favorite among those remaining; to improve on that they would need the room of someone who left earlier, and this would make that person worse off.
  • The core. The outcome is in the core: no group, by trading only their own rooms among themselves, can reach an outcome that is at least as good for every member and better for at least one (Roth and Postlewaite, 1977). In particular, everyone gets a room at least as good as their own; IR holds.

When all possible false preference lists of each person in the example are tried on a computer, none gives a better room. The same rule also fits swaps of duty or shifts. Its most impressive real-world application is kidney exchange: patients with an incompatible donor trade donors in cycles (Roth, Sönmez, and Ünver, 2004).

Common mistake

Leaving the rooms of those who exited in one round as targets of arrows in later rounds. In round 2, D’s first choice is C’s room, but C’s room went to A in round 1 and left the market. D now chooses from those remaining, that is, between E’s room and its own. The arrows are redrawn in every round among those remaining only.

4.4.11Code trace: ttc()

The Python version of TTC collects the arrows in a dictionary in each round, finds a cycle by starting from one person and following the arrows, and removes those in the cycle. The input is the dictionary pref: pref[i] is the list that ranks the room owners from best to worst for i, and it includes their own name.

def ttc(pref):
  left, assign = list(pref), {}
  while left:
    g = {i: next(j for j in pref[i]
             if j in left) for i in left}
    i, path = left[0], []
    while i not in path:          # follow arrows
      path.append(i); i = g[i]
    cycle = path[path.index(i):]
    for k in cycle:              # trade
      assign[k] = g[k]; left.remove(k)
  return assign

The parts of the code are:

  • left is the list of people who have not yet traded, and assign is the result dictionary: assign[k] is the owner of the room that k receives.
  • The dictionary g holds this round’s arrows: for each remaining person i, the first person in the list pref[i] who is among those remaining. next(...) returns the first element that satisfies the condition.
  • i = left[0] starts from the first remaining person. The inner loop adds i to the path if it has not been added before and follows its arrow (i = g[i]). It stops when it reaches someone seen before.
  • cycle = path[path.index(i):] is the cyclic part of the path: from the place where the repeated person first appeared to the end. The people at the start of the path may be people who lead into the cycle but are not on it; this slicing leaves them out.
  • Each person in the cycle gets the room they point to and is removed from left.

4.5Bayesian games

So far the private information has been between the designer and the players: the designer did not know the types. But the players often do not know one another’s types either. In an auction a bidder does not know the rivals’ values; in a negotiation the buyer does not know the seller’s cost. To study such situations of incomplete information, game theory uses Bayesian games. Bayesian games are also the framework on which the BIC concept (4.2.4) rests.

4.5.1Incomplete information and Bayesian games

Solving a game of incomplete information looks impossible at first sight: a player does not even know which game the rival is playing. John Harsanyi (1967–68) solved this problem with an elegant idea. A “chance” or “Nature” move, outside the players, is added to the game. At the start of the game, Nature chooses each player’s type according to a probability distribution. Each player sees only their own type. The distribution itself is known to everyone. The problem of an “unknown game” thus becomes the problem of “a known game with chance in it”. This method is called the Harsanyi transformation.

Definition 4.9

A Bayesian game consists of the following elements: the players; for each player an action set and a type set Θi; a common prior π over type profiles, known to everyone; and, for each player, a payoff function ui(a, θ) that depends on the action profile a and the type profile θ.

In a Bayesian game the notion of strategy changes. A player decides after learning their own type; but the person doing the analysis (and the rivals) does not know the player’s type. A strategy is therefore a rule that says which action to choose for each type. For example, the strategy of a player with two types is a mapping such as “if I am of type S, play C; if I am of type A, play M”. The rival knows this rule but does not know which line will be applied.

Definition 4.10

A strategy profile is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium if every player, at each of their types, maximizes their expected payoff against the other players’ strategies. The expected payoff is taken over the types the player does not know, using the common prior.

The definition is a natural extension of Nash equilibrium. In Nash equilibrium we say “no one wants to deviate alone”. In Bayes–Nash equilibrium the same is said, except that the gain from deviating is measured by expected value and each type is checked separately. If a player has two types, each of the two types must play its own best response.

As an example, consider a meeting game. P1 will go in the evening either to a concert (C) or to a match (M) and prefers the concert. P2 is one of two types: type S wants to meet P1, and type A wants to avoid P1. P1 does not know P2’s type; each type has probability 1/2. P2 knows their own type.

Figure 4.26 shows the game tree. The dashed ellipse shows that P1 cannot tell the two nodes apart: P1 must choose the same action without knowing which type of P2 it is playing against. This is called P1’s information set. P2, on the other hand, decides separately on each branch, so it can have a type-dependent strategy.

The Bayesian version of the revelation principle links this to mechanism design. Every outcome that can be implemented in a Bayes–Nash equilibrium can also be implemented by a direct mechanism in which honest reporting is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium, that is, by a BIC direct mechanism. The proof is the same as in 4.2.3; the only difference is that the sentence “the deviation is not profitable” is stated for expected payoffs.

Common mistake

Mistaking P2’s strategy for a single action. “P2 plays C” is not a Bayesian strategy; what each type will do must be stated separately. Also, when computing P1’s expected payoff, each type’s own choice must be used: in the candidate P1 = C, type S is at C and type A is at M; putting both types in the same place gives a wrong result.

4.5.3How the equilibrium changes with the probability

If the prior changes, the equilibrium can change too. Let the probability that P2 is of type S be a general number π; the probability of type A is 1 − π. P2’s best responses do not depend on π, because P2 knows its own type. Only P1’s expected payoffs change.

  • Candidate 1 (P1 = C; S→C, A→M). If P1 stays at C it gets π · 2 + (1 − π) · 0 = 2π; if it switches to M it gets π · 0 + (1 − π) · 1 = 1 − π. For equilibrium we need 2π ≥ 1 − π, that is, π ≥ 1/3.
  • Candidate 2 (P1 = M; S→M, A→C). If P1 stays at M it gets π · 1 = π; if it switches to C it gets (1 − π) · 2. For equilibrium we need π ≥ 2(1 − π), that is, π ≥ 2/3.

If type S is very unlikely (π < 1/3), P2 is very probably of type A and runs from P1 wherever P1 goes, so P1 wants to switch to the other place every time. In this case there is no pure equilibrium; by Nash’s existence theorem there is an equilibrium in mixed strategies. If type S is very likely (π ≥ 2/3), the game comes close to a coordination game and two pure equilibria appear.

At the threshold values π = 1/3 and π = 2/3, P1 is indifferent between its two actions. For example, at π = 1/3 in candidate 1, both C and M give 2/3. In the case of a tie, P1 staying at C is a best response; this is why the inequalities are written with “≥”.

4.5.4Mechanisms from a Bayesian viewpoint

Bayesian games are used in mechanism design where DSIC is not enough or is too strict. Four examples show why this viewpoint is needed.

The first-price auction. In a first-price auction, an honest bid leaves the winner with zero utility (4.2.4). In equilibrium, therefore, everyone bids below their value. How far below? The answer depends on beliefs about the rivals’ values; the equilibrium is therefore Bayesian. In a standard example the values of two bidders are uniformly distributed between 0 and 1 (every value is equally likely). If the rival bids half its value, the probability that a bidder with value v wins with a bid b is the probability that the rival’s value is below 2b, that is, 2b (for b ≤ 1/2). The expected utility is (v − b) · 2b. This expression is largest at b = v/2. So the strategy “bid half your value” is a best response to itself for two players: it is a Bayes–Nash equilibrium.

Example 4.21

The revelation principle builds a direct mechanism from the first-price equilibrium: “Report your value; the highest report wins and pays half of its report.” If values are uniform between 0 and 1, this mechanism is BIC. An example shows that it is not DSIC.

  1. Let the bidder’s value be 0.8 and let the rival report 0.9. An honest report (0.8) loses; the utility is 0.
  2. If the bidder reports 0.95, they win and pay 0.95/2 = 0.475. The utility is 0.8 − 0.475 = 0.325 > 0.
  3. Against this rival report, lying paid off. Honesty is not best whatever the rival reports; the mechanism is not DSIC. But when the rival is honest and values are uniform, honesty is best on average.

This example shows why the converse of the relation DSIC ⇒ BIC is not true in general.

DSIC or BIC? DSIC is independent of the players’ beliefs and is robust: even if the type distribution is estimated wrongly, honesty remains best. BIC is weaker but allows more goals. For example, VCG cannot achieve strong budget balance in the public goods problem; yet at the BIC level there is a public goods mechanism that is both efficient and strongly budget balanced (d’Aspremont and Gérard-Varet, 1979; Arrow, 1979). In this mechanism the payments are adjusted according to the expected effect of each player’s report on the others, and the money collected is redistributed to the players.

Maximum revenue. The auction that maximizes the seller’s expected revenue is also a Bayesian question, because “expected revenue” depends on the distribution of the buyers’ values. Myerson (1981) solved this question. Under standard conditions the answer is an auction with a reserve price: the seller sets a minimum price, and no sale is made below it. A reserve price sometimes causes no sale at all, which hurts efficiency, but in return it raises the expected revenue. This trade-off between efficiency and revenue is one of the basic topics of auction design.

Poker and bluffing. In poker the rival’s hand (type) is hidden. Bluffing, that is, a large bet with a weak hand, is part of a mixed Bayesian strategy: if strong hands are always played with a large bet, the size of the bet gives the hand away.

4.6Review

The assumptions and limits of mechanism design, a comparison of the chapter’s mechanisms, and a few short problems are collected below.

4.6.1Behavior, fairness, and ethics

Do people always respond to incentives? The theory assumes that players maximize their utilities. In reality, emotions, morality, habit, and a sense of fairness also influence decisions. The rejection of low offers in the Ultimatum Game (4.2.5) is the best-known example. Incentives are strong but not the only determinant. This observation does not invalidate mechanism design; it brings two cautions. First, the utility function should reflect what people actually care about. Second, rules should be simple and understandable. For example, the English auction gives the same outcome as Vickrey, but it is much easier to see there why honest behavior is best.

Ethical problems in mechanism design. Even good rules can lead to bad outcomes:

  • Manipulation and withheld information. Under rules that are not DSIC, those with access to information and advice have an advantage. Under the Boston mechanism, families who know how to write strategic lists can get ahead of families who write their preferences as they are.
  • The advantage of powerful players. Collusion and false identities give organized groups power that the rule did not foresee.
  • Unfair distribution of income. Efficiency maximizes the sum of values; but value depends on ability to pay. A rich player’s “high value” often shows only that they have more money.
  • Excluding those who cannot pay. Mechanisms that use money shut out those who cannot pay from the start. This is why money is not used in areas such as school placement and organ transplantation.

Ethical limits must therefore be set by society and its institutions. A good mechanism must be not only efficient but also fair and ethical. Mechanism design is a tool in this debate: it shows clearly which goal can be achieved with which rules and at what cost; which goal to pursue is for society to decide.

4.6.3Practice problems

The following five problems bring together the chapter’s computational methods in short situations.

Example 4.22

Two identical items, three bidders. Each bidder wants at most one item; the values are 10, 8, and 5. Under VCG, who wins and who pays what?

  1. The efficient outcome. The two items go to the two highest values: 10 and 8 win; the welfare is 18.
  2. Payment of the bidder with value 10. Without this bidder the items would go to 8 and 5: the others’ best welfare is 8 + 5 = 13. With this bidder present, the others get only the item of the bidder with value 8: welfare 8. The payment is 13 − 8 = 5.
  3. Payment of the bidder with value 8. Without this bidder the others’ welfare would be 10 + 5 = 15; with this bidder, 10. The payment is 15 − 10 = 5.
  4. The bidder with value 5. The outcome is the same without this bidder; the payment is 0.

Each winner pays the highest value among the losers (5). This is the generalization of the single-item second-price rule to several identical items.

Example 4.23

Bora’s most profitable report. In the proportional cost sharing example (park 60; values 30, 25, 20), what should Bora report if Ayşe and Cem are honest?

  1. The sum of Ayşe’s and Cem’s reports is 30 + 20 = 50. For the park to be built, Bora must report at least 10.
  2. If Bora reports 10, the total is exactly 60 and Bora pays 60 · 10/60 = 10. The utility is 25 − 10 = 15.
  3. When honest the utility was 5. The lie gains 10 units; the rule is not IC.
Example 4.24

Villages at 3, 7, 8, 12, 30. What are the median and the mean? Under the mean rule, what should the village at km 30 report to pull the facility exactly to itself?

  1. In the sorted list the middle number is 8: the median is 8.
  2. The mean is (3 + 7 + 8 + 12 + 30)/5 = 60/5 = 12.
  3. For the mean to be 30 we need (3 + 7 + 8 + 12 + x)/5 = 30: 30 + x = 150, x = 120. By reporting 120 the village pulls the facility to its own location. Under the median, a report of 120 leaves the median at 8.
Example 4.25

If the probability of type S were 1/4, would (C; S→C, A→M) still be a Bayes–Nash equilibrium?

  1. P2’s strategy does not change: type S goes to C and type A goes to M.
  2. If P1 stays at C it gets ¼ · 2 + ¾ · 0 = 0.5; if it switches to M it gets ¼ · 0 + ¾ · 1 = 0.75.
  3. 0.75 > 0.5: P1 deviates to M. The profile is not an equilibrium. Since 1/4 < 1/3, by Table 4.16 there is no pure equilibrium; a mixed equilibrium is needed.
Example 4.26

Three-person TTC. The preferences are A: B ≻ C ≻ A, B: A ≻ C ≻ B, C: A ≻ B ≻ C. Who gets which room?

  1. Round 1 arrows: A→B, B→A, C→A. A and B point to each other: A ↔ B is a cycle. A gets B’s room and B gets A’s room, and both leave.
  2. Round 2: only C remains. Since A’s and B’s rooms are gone, C points to itself and keeps its own room.

C would most have liked A’s room, but A does not prefer C’s room to B’s. C’s room is nobody’s first choice; C finds no one to trade with.

4.7Summary

  • Mechanism design is game theory in reverse: the desired outcome is given, and we look for rules (an allocation and a payment) that produce it in equilibrium. The difficulty is that the players’ types (values, preferences, costs) are hidden from the designer.
  • A social choice function f assigns the desired outcome to each type profile. In a direct mechanism the players report their types; the mechanism computes the outcome x(b) and the payments p(b); utility is quasi-linear: ui = vi(x) − pi.
  • The revelation principle: an outcome implemented in equilibrium by an indirect mechanism can also be implemented by a direct mechanism in which honest reporting is an equilibrium. If the equilibrium concept is dominant strategies we get DSIC; if it is Bayes–Nash we get BIC; DSIC ⇒ BIC.
  • IR means that participating is no worse than staying out. IC and IR are different conditions. In the Ultimatum Game, theory gives the equilibria (10, 0) and (9, 1); in experiments, low offers are rejected: the utility model must include fairness.
  • Proportional cost sharing is not IC: in the park example Cem raises his utility from 4 to 15 by reporting 5; if everyone reports half, the park is not built. Myerson–Satterthwaite: if values and costs are independent and come from overlapping ranges, efficiency, BIC, IR, and budget balance cannot all hold in bilateral trade.
  • Implementation theory: honesty need not be the only equilibrium; in Vickrey, the bids (0, 100) form a Nash equilibrium that gives the item to the lower-valued player.
  • Myerson’s lemma: in a single-parameter environment, an allocation can be used in a DSIC mechanism if and only if it is monotone. The payment is uniquely determined: p = b · x(b) − ∫0b x(z) dz; for a 0/1 allocation it is the critical bid.
  • VCG chooses the outcome with the highest social welfare and makes each player pay for their externality: pi = (the others’ best welfare without i) − (the others’ welfare with i). It is DSIC, efficient, and (with the Clarke pivot) IR; it lacks strong budget balance. In the public project example the Gym is chosen and the payments are 0, 5, 20. For a single item, VCG = Vickrey.
  • In design without money, Gibbard–Satterthwaite allows only dictatorship for general preferences and at least three alternatives, among rules in which every alternative can win. Gale–Shapley always finds a stable matching (O(n²)), is best for the proposers and DSIC for them; the receiving side can gain by lying.
  • The median is strategy-proof with single-peaked preferences and minimizes the total distance; the mean can be manipulated. The Boston mechanism is not DSIC; serial dictatorship and TTC are DSIC. TTC is Pareto optimal and in the core.
  • In a Bayesian game, Nature chooses types according to a common prior; a strategy is a rule from types to actions; in a Bayes–Nash equilibrium every type maximizes its expected payoff. In the meeting game, for P(S) = 1/2 the only pure equilibrium is (C; S→C, A→M).

4.9Test Yourself

  1. A playground costs 40,000 TL. The values are Ayşe 20, Bora 16, Cem 14. The rule is proportional cost sharing: the playground is built if the reports sum to at least 40, and the cost is shared in proportion to the reports. What are the payments and utilities when everyone is honest? When Bora and Cem are honest, what is Ayşe’s most profitable report?

    Answer

    The total is 50 ≥ 40. The payments are 40 · 20/50 = 16, 40 · 16/50 = 12.8, 40 · 14/50 = 11.2; the utilities are 4; 3.2; 2.8 (IR holds). Bora’s and Cem’s reports sum to 30; for the playground to be built, Ayşe must report at least 10. If Ayşe reports 10, the total is 40 and she pays 40 · 10/40 = 10; her utility is 20 − 10 = 10 > 4. The rule is not IC.

  2. Three residents report the following values for three projects (Park, Library, Gym): Ayşe (20, 5, 10), Bora (0, 25, 15), Cem (15, 10, 30). Which project does VCG choose? What are each resident’s payment and utility?

    Answer

    The totals are Park 35, Library 40, Gym 55: the Gym is chosen. Without Ayşe the totals are 15, 35, 45; the best is 45, and at the Gym the others get 45: payment 0. Without Bora, 35, 15, 40; the best is 40, and at the Gym the others get 40: payment 0. Without Cem, 20, 30, 25; the best is the Library (30), and at the Gym the others get 10 + 15 = 25: payment 30 − 25 = 5. The utilities are Ayşe 10 − 0 = 10, Bora 15 − 0 = 15, Cem 30 − 5 = 25. The budget surplus is 5.

  3. Four bidders for a single item have values 40, 65, 50, and 30. In a Vickrey auction, who wins and how much do they pay? How does the outcome change if the bidders with values 65 and 50 collude and the bidder with value 50 bids 0?

    Answer

    With honest bids, 65 wins and pays the second-highest bid, 50; the utility is 15. With collusion the bids are 40, 65, 0, 30. 65 still wins, but the second-highest bid is 40; the winner pays 40 and the utility rises to 25. The group gains 10 units, and the seller’s revenue falls from 50 to 40. A deviation by one player alone would not pay, but collusion does.

  4. In a single-parameter environment, with the others’ bids fixed, a player’s allocation is as follows: 0 below a bid of 3, 1/4 between 3 and 6, and 1 from 6 up. By the Myerson payment, what are p(4) and p(8)? If the player with value 8 bids honestly, what is the utility?

    Answer

    The allocation is monotone. ∫04 x = ¼ · 1 = 0.25, p(4) = 4 · ¼ − 0.25 = 0.75 (check: jump × threshold = ¼ · 3). ∫08 x = ¼ · 3 + 1 · 2 = 2.75, p(8) = 8 − 2.75 = 5.25 (check: ¼ · 3 + ¾ · 6 = 0.75 + 4.5 = 5.25). The utility is 8 − 5.25 = 2.75, which is the area under the curve.

  5. Student preferences are A: Y ≻ X ≻ Z, B: X ≻ Y ≻ Z, C: Y ≻ Z ≻ X; project preferences are X: A ≻ B ≻ C, Y: B ≻ C ≻ A, Z: C ≻ A ≻ B. When the students propose, how many proposals does Gale–Shapley make and what is the result? What if the projects proposed?

    Answer

    The students propose: A→Y, B→X, C→Y; Y holds C and rejects A (C ≻ A). A→X; X holds A and rejects B. B→Y; Y holds B and rejects C. C→Z: accepted. With 6 proposals the result is A–X, B–Y, C–Z. If the projects propose, X→A, Y→B, Z→C and all three are accepted: the same result with 3 proposals. This matching is the only stable matching. Every student gets their second choice and every project gets its first choice.

  6. Five villages are at km 1, 4, 6, 11, and 18. Where is the facility built under the mean and the median rules, and what is the total distance? Under the mean rule, what must the village at km 18 report to pull the facility exactly to itself?

    Answer

    Median 6: the total is 5 + 2 + 0 + 5 + 12 = 24. Mean 40/5 = 8: the total is 7 + 4 + 2 + 3 + 10 = 26. For the mean to be 18, (1 + 4 + 6 + 11 + x)/5 = 18, that is, x = 90 − 22 = 68. Under the median, a report of 68 leaves the median at 6.

  7. Four students’ room preferences: A: B ≻ C ≻ A, B: C ≻ A ≻ B, C: B ≻ D ≻ C, D: A ≻ B ≻ D. Applying TTC, who gets which room?

    Answer

    Round 1 arrows: A→B, B→C, C→B, D→A. Starting from A, the path is A → B → C → B; the cycle is B ↔ C. B gets C’s room and C gets B’s. Round 2: A’s first two choices are gone, so A points to itself; of D’s first two choices, A remains: D→A. The cycle is A → A: A keeps its own room. Round 3: D points to itself and keeps its own room. The result is B ← C, C ← B, A ← A, D ← D.

  8. In the meeting game, let the probability of type S be 2/5. Are (C; S→C, A→M) and (M; S→M, A→C) Bayes–Nash equilibria?

    Answer

    First candidate: C → 2/5 · 2 = 4/5, M → 3/5 · 1 = 3/5; C is better, so it is an equilibrium ✓. Second candidate: C → 3/5 · 2 = 6/5, M → 2/5 · 1 = 2/5; P1 deviates to C, so it is not an equilibrium. Since 1/3 ≤ 2/5 < 2/3, there is a single pure equilibrium.
