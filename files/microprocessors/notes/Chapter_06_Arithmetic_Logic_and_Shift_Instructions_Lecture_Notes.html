Chapter 6: Arithmetic, Logic and Shift Instructions

The real job of a processor is to compute. In the 8086 this job is done by the arithmetic logic unit (ALU) inside the execution unit: it adds two numbers, subtracts one from the other, passes their bits through AND, OR and exclusive-OR gates, and shifts them left or right. At the end of every operation the ALU not only writes the result to a register or to memory; it also records how the result came out in the six status bits of the flags register. Later instructions, especially conditional jumps, make their decisions by looking at these bits. These notes take up the arithmetic and logic instructions of the 8086 one by one and show which flag each instruction changes and how. First the two readings, unsigned and signed, of the same bit pattern are explained, together with the difference between the carry and overflow flags. Then come addition, subtraction, comparison and the logic instructions, the implicit registers of multiplication and division, sign extension, the single-operand instructions, and shifts and rotates. How 32-bit addition, subtraction and multiplication are done on a 16-bit processor is traced step by step. The last parts examine array summation, binary-coded decimal (BCD) arithmetic and temperature conversion programs at register and flag level.

6.1Flags and the result of an operation

6.1.1Where does the ALU write its result?

The inside of the 8086 is divided into two units. The bus interface unit (BIU) fetches instruction bytes from memory, places them in the prefetch queue, and reads and writes memory operands. The execution unit (EU) decodes and executes the instruction it takes from the queue. All calculations such as addition, subtraction, AND, OR and shifts are done in the 16-bit arithmetic logic unit (ALU) inside the EU. The ALU has two inputs and one output. The inputs come from registers, from the immediate value in the instruction, or from a number the BIU fetched from memory. The output goes to the destination register or, through the BIU, to memory.

The ALU has a second output as well: a few bits that describe the properties of the result. Is the result zero, is its leftmost bit 1, did a carry come out of the leftmost bit? The yes/no answers to these questions are written to the status bits of the 16-bit flags register (FLAGS). Figure 6.1 shows the bit layout of the flags register. Nine of the sixteen bits are used. Six are status flags: CF, PF, AF, ZF, SF and OF describe the result of the last arithmetic or logic operation. Three are control flags: TF, IF and DF govern the behavior of the processor and arithmetic instructions do not touch them.

Flags are a bridge that carries information between instructions. The CMP AL, BL instruction compares two numbers but writes the result to no register; it only changes the flags. A conditional jump right after it, such as JZ, JC or JO, makes its decision by looking at these flags. In multiword addition, the carry of the first addition waits in CF and the second addition takes it up with ADC. So two questions are asked of every instruction: where does it write its result, and which flags does it change and how?

Table 6.1 collects the status and control flags, the condition under which each becomes 1, and a short example of each. A detailed description of each flag and how it is produced in hardware is given in the notes on the 8086 registers (Chapter 2.2). The hardware counterpart of the status flags is simple. The ALU's adder adds the bits from right to left with a chain of full adders. The carry that bit 7 produces in a byte operation, or bit 15 in a word operation, goes directly to CF. The carry that bit 3 produces goes to AF. SF is a copy of the most significant bit of the result. ZF is the output of a NOR gate that takes all the bits of the result as input. PF passes the eight bits of the low byte through an exclusive-OR tree and inverts the result; even for a word result it looks only at the low byte. OF is the exclusive-OR of the carry into the most significant bit and the carry out of that bit. This last rule is seen with numbers in the examples below.

In subtraction the ALU does not use a separate subtractor. It performs A − B by adding A + (the one's complement of B) + 1; this is addition with a two's complement. A carry out of the leftmost bit in this addition actually means that no borrow was needed. The 8086 therefore inverts the carry in subtraction and writes it to CF. As a result, CF behaves in subtraction like a borrow flag: in the unsigned reading, CF = 1 if a larger number was subtracted from a smaller one.

6.1.2CF or OF?

The eight bits in a register are, by themselves, neither an unsigned nor a signed number. The sequence 1001 0110b means 150 if read as unsigned, and −106 if read as signed two's complement. The processor does the addition on the bits only and produces the same result for both readings. It is the programmer who knows which reading applies. The processor takes both readings into account and keeps two separate flags: CF says whether the result is correct in the unsigned reading, OF in the signed reading.

Definition 6.1

The carry flag (CF) shows that, when the operation is done with the unsigned reading, the result does not fit the size of the destination. CF = 1 if in byte addition the true sum exceeds 255, or in subtraction the result falls below 0.

The overflow flag (OF) shows that, when the operation is done with the signed two's complement reading, the result does not fit the range. For a byte the range is −128…127 and for a word it is −32,768…32,767. OF = 1 if the sum of two numbers of the same sign comes out with the opposite sign.

In the first row the unsigned sum is 256 and does not fit in a byte. 256 − 256 = 0 stays in AL and the carry that comes out falls into CF. In the signed reading, however, −1 + 1 = 0 is entirely correct; OF = 0. This row shows that CF = 1 is not an error in signed arithmetic. The second row is the opposite. 150 fits in a byte as unsigned, CF = 0. But a signed byte can be at most 127; the sum of two positive numbers has come out 96h, and the sign bit of 96h is 1. In the signed reading this means −106 and is wrong: OF = 1.

In the third row the two readings again part ways. Unsigned 300 does not fit, CF = 1. In the signed reading, one of the numbers is negative (−56) and the other positive (100). The sum of two numbers with different signs is smaller than the larger of them in absolute value; so it can never fall outside the range. The result 44 is correct and OF = 0. In the fourth row both readings are wrong: 256 does not fit in a byte, and −256 does not fit either. Both flags are 1 together. In the last row both readings are correct and both flags are 0.

The rule for OF can also be stated in terms of the carries at the most significant bit. Let us add the second row bit by bit: 0110 0100b + 0011 0010b. At bit 5, 1 + 1 = 10b; 0 is written and a carry of 1 passes to bit 6. At bit 6, 1 + 0 + 1 = 10b; 0 is written and the carry enters bit 7. At bit 7, 0 + 0 + 1 = 1 is written and no carry goes out. The carry into bit 7 is 1 and the carry out is 0. Because the two differ, OF = 1. In the fourth row, no carry enters bit 7 at all but 1 + 1 produces a carry out: carry in 0, carry out 1, again OF = 1. In the first row a carry enters bit 7 and a carry also goes out: because the two are the same, OF = 0.

Rule (overflow flag)

In addition, OF = (carry into the most significant bit) XOR (carry out of the most significant bit). In terms of signs this reads: if the sum of two numbers of the same sign comes out with the opposite sign, OF = 1; when two numbers of different signs are added, OF is never 1. In subtraction (A − B) the rule takes this form: if the difference of two numbers of different signs has a sign different from that of A, OF = 1.

Common mistake

Reading CF as "overflow" and looking at CF when working with signed numbers. In the addition −1 + 1, CF = 1, although the signed result is correct. A program that works with signed numbers looks at OF (and, in comparisons, at SF and OF); a program that works with unsigned numbers looks at CF. The processor does not know which one applies; the choice belongs to whoever writes the instructions.

The MOV in the third row changes no flag. This is a rule that is often forgotten: data movement instructions such as MOV, LEA and XCHG do not pass through the ALU, and the flags stay as whatever the previous arithmetic instruction left. CF = 1 and PF = 1 stay in place.

In the fourth row 80 + 80 = 160 is correct in the unsigned reading; CF = 0. But the sum of two positive numbers has come out A0h = 1010 0000b and the sign bit is 1. The INC AL in the seventh row adds 1 to FFh and AL = 00h. Because the result is zero, ZF = 1, and because F + 1 in the low nibble gives a carry, AF = 1. If the same operation were done with ADD AL, 1, a carry would come out and CF = 1. INC, however, never touches CF; the CF = 1 in the table is left over from the addition in the fifth row. The CMC (complement carry) in the last row only inverts CF. In the same family STC sets CF to 1 and CLC sets CF to 0.

6.1.4The flags of ADD step by step

The most reliable way to compute flags by hand is to do the addition on paper in binary. The instructions MOV AL, 64h and ADD AL, 32h add 100 and 50. Figure 6.2 shows the two operands, the result, and from which bit each flag is read.

  1. Result. 0110 0100b + 0011 0010b = 1001 0110b = 96h. In the unsigned reading 150 is correct; in the signed reading −106 is wrong.
  2. CF. No carry came out of the leftmost bit; 150 ≤ 255. CF = 0.
  3. OF. The carry into bit 7 is 1, the carry out is 0. The sum of two positive numbers looked negative. OF = 1.
  4. SF and ZF. Bit 7 of the result is 1, SF = 1. The result is not zero, ZF = 0.
  5. PF. 1001 0110b has four 1s; four is even, PF = 1.
  6. AF. The low nibbles are 4 + 2 = 6; no carry passes from bit 3 to bit 4, AF = 0.

Result: AL = 96h; CF = 0, ZF = 0, SF = 1, OF = 1, PF = 1, AF = 0. When this calculation is run in emu8086, the same values are seen in the flags window. The flag calculator in the slides sets up the same table for other numbers and other instructions.

Example 6.1

What are the flags after MOV AL, 0C0h and ADD AL, 0C0h?

C0h = 1100 0000b; 192 unsigned, −64 signed. The sum is 1 1000 0000b = 180h. 80h is written to AL and the carry that comes out falls into CF: CF = 1. The carry into bit 7 is 1 (from the 1 + 1 at bit 6) and the carry out is also 1; OF = 0. Indeed −64 + (−64) = −128 fits a signed byte exactly. SF = 1, ZF = 0. 80h has a single 1 bit: PF = 0. Since the low nibbles are 0 + 0, AF = 0.

6.2Instruction groups and operand rules

6.2.1Three groups

The arithmetic and logic instructions of the 8086 fall into three groups according to how they take their operands. This division eases memorization: instructions in the same group accept the same operand forms and are encoded similarly in machine code. Figure 6.3 shows the operand layout of the three groups side by side.

  • Group 1, two-operand instructions: ADD, SUB, CMP, AND, TEST, OR, XOR. ADC (add with carry) and SBB (subtract with borrow) are also added to this list. The form is instruction destination, source; the operation is done between the destination and the source and the result is written to the destination.
  • Group 2, multiplication and division: MUL, IMUL, DIV, IDIV. A single operand is written in the instruction. The other half of the operation and the place where the result goes are fixed: AL, AX or DX:AX. These are called implicit operands.
  • Group 3, single-operand instructions: INC, DEC, NOT, NEG. The operand is both read and written: INC CX reads CX, adds 1 and writes the result back to CX.

Table 6.4 summarizes what each instruction does in one line. Shifts and rotates also take a single operand, but because they give the shift count as a second operand they are treated as a separate family.

The rules resemble the rules of MOV and come from the same hardware constraint. Both operands cannot be in memory: ADD [BX], [SI] is invalid, because the ModR/M byte of the instruction can describe only a single memory address. The two operands must be the same size: ADD AL, BX tries to add a 16-bit number to an 8-bit register and the assembler rejects it. The destination cannot be a constant: ADD 5, AL could not know where to write the result. Segment registers cannot be arithmetic operands: ADD DS, AX is invalid; DS is changed only with MOV, PUSH and POP.

When a memory operand is used with a constant, the size cannot be told from either. In the form AND [SI], 0Fh, 0Fh could be a byte or a word; so BYTE PTR or WORD PTR must be specified. If one of the operands is a register or a variable of known type, the size is taken from it.

Common mistake

Trying to add two variables directly: ADD total, count. Both operands are in memory and this instruction does not exist. One of them is first moved into a register: MOV AX, count and ADD total, AX.

6.2.3Where is the result written, and how are the flags affected?

After a two-operand operation the result is always stored in the first operand, that is, the destination. The SUB AX, BX instruction computes AX − BX and writes it to AX; BX does not change. CMP and TEST are the two exceptions to this rule. CMP does the subtraction SUB does but discards the result. TEST does the bit-by-bit AND that AND does but discards the result. Both only change the flags and so make testing possible without spoiling a register.

The effect on the flags depends on whether the operation is arithmetic or logic. Table 6.6 compares the two families.

Arithmetic instructions set all six status flags according to the result. Logic instructions have no notion of a carry: each bit is processed on its own and nothing passes from one bit to another. So CF and OF are always set to 0. ZF, SF and PF are set according to the result. The value of AF is left undefined in Intel's documents; a program must not rely on AF after a logic instruction. Undefined does not mean the flag is random; on a real processor it takes a definite value, but that value is promised in no document and may come out differently on another x86 processor.

6.2.4Timing: time inside the processor and on the bus

The time an instruction takes depends closely on where its operands are. Two numbers in registers go straight into the ALU; ADD AX, BX takes 3 clock cycles on the 8086. If there is a memory operand, the EU first computes the effective address and then asks the BIU for a read bus cycle. A bus cycle is at least 4 clock cycles: the address is put on the bus, memory is read, and the data is taken into the chip. If the destination is in memory, another bus cycle is needed to write the result back. During this read-modify-write, the 8086's RD pin becomes active first and the WR pin later. Table 6.7 gives the times from Intel's 8086 manual; EA is the time to compute the effective address (5–12 cycles).

The most significant bit of the result is 1, SF = 1. In the low nibbles too, 7 − 9 needs a borrow, AF = 1. In the signed reading 7 − 9 = −2 is correct and OF = 0. Because this instruction uses BL, not AL, the short form cannot be chosen; the general 80 /5 form takes three bytes.

Line 5. CMP AL, 10h performs the subtraction 10h − 10h = 0. The result is zero, ZF = 1. The result is not stored: AL is still 10h. This is the essence of an equality test: if two numbers are equal, their difference is zero and ZF = 1. If a JE (or JZ, the other name of the same instruction) follows immediately, the jump is taken. The CF = 1 and AF = 1 left from the previous line are also erased; CMP rewrites all the status flags.

Line 6. CMP BL, 0 is FEh − 0. The result is FEh: not zero (ZF = 0), sign bit 1 (SF = 1), no borrow (CF = 0). These three flags carry two different readings. Read as unsigned, FEh = 254 > 0; CF = 0 says so. Read as signed, FEh = −2 < 0; SF ≠ OF says so.

Line 7. FEh + 2 = 100h. 00h stays in BL; CF = 1 and ZF = 1. In the signed reading −2 + 2 = 0 is correct, OF = 0. This line shows the difference between CF and OF once more: CF = 1 does not mean the signed calculation is wrong.

6.3.2Comparing magnitudes with CMP

The real job of CMP is to find not whether two numbers are equal but which is larger. CMP A, B computes A − B. For unsigned numbers, if A < B the subtraction needs a borrow and CF = 1. For signed numbers the situation is subtler. If the difference did not overflow (OF = 0), the sign of the result is correct and if A < B then SF = 1. If the difference overflowed (OF = 1), the sign bit has flipped; this time A < B is shown by SF = 0. The two cases combine into a single rule: signed A < B ⇔ SF ≠ OF.

6.3.332-bit addition with ADC

The 8086's ALU adds numbers of at most 16 bits. A number that does not fit in 16 bits, such as 131,071, is stored split into two words: DX holds the high word and AX the low word, and the pair is written DX:AX. To add two 32-bit numbers, the method of addition on paper is followed: first the digits on the right are added, and the carry is added to the digits on the left. Here a "digit" is a 16-bit word and the carry is carried in CF.

Definition 6.2

The logic instructions of the 8086 apply these rules not to a number as a whole but to its bits one by one. The AND AL, BL instruction is really eight independent AND gates: bit 0 of AL is ANDed with bit 0 of BL, bit 1 with bit 1, and the result is written to the same bit of AL. No carry or borrow passes between neighboring bits. In the ALU this operation is done with the adder's carry chain disabled and the output of the right gate selected at each bit. That is why logic instructions are as fast as addition: 3 clock cycles between registers.

A consequence of processing bit by bit is that carry and overflow are meaningless. The 8086 states this explicitly: after AND, OR, XOR and TEST, CF = 0 and OF = 0. ZF, SF and PF are set according to the result, AF is undefined. NOT is an exception: it changes no flag.

6.4.2Masking

The most common use of logic instructions is to change some bits of a number and preserve the others. For this a mask is given as the second operand. Each bit of the mask chooses what happens to the corresponding bit. This method is called masking.

  • Clearing bits with AND. x AND 0 = 0, x AND 1 = x. Bits that are 0 in the mask are cleared, those that are 1 are preserved.
  • Setting bits with OR. x OR 1 = 1, x OR 0 = x. Bits that are 1 in the mask become 1 for certain, those that are 0 are preserved.
  • Inverting bits with XOR. x XOR 1 = NOT x, x XOR 0 = x. Bits that are 1 in the mask are inverted, those that are 0 are preserved.
  • Testing bits with TEST. TEST does an AND but does not write the result. If the bit in the mask (or one of the bits) is 1 and the number also has a 1 there, the result is nonzero: ZF = 0. The number does not change.

In ASCII encoding, uppercase and lowercase letters differ only in bit 5: 'A' = 41h = 0100 0001b, 'a' = 61h = 0110 0001b. So the conversion of a letter is done with a single logic instruction. Figure 6.4 shows the four operations with the same letters.

The meaning of the mask values is plain when they are written in binary. DFh = 1101 1111b is a mask with only bit 5 equal to 0; with AND it clears bit 5. 20h = 0010 0000b is a mask with only bit 5 equal to 1; with OR it sets bit 5 and with XOR it inverts it. In the last figure BL = '7' = 37h = 0011 0111b is TESTed with the mask 01h. The result is 01h ≠ 0, so ZF = 0: bit 0 is one, that is, the number is odd.

6.5Multiplication and division

6.5.1MUL and IMUL

The product of two 8-bit numbers can be up to 16 bits, and that of two 16-bit numbers up to 32 bits: 255 × 255 = 65,025 does not fit in a byte, only in a word. The 8086 therefore always writes the result of a multiplication to a place twice the width of the operand. The other operand of the multiplication and the place of the result are fixed; only one operand is written in the instruction. Figure 6.5 shows the two sizes.

Definition 6.3

MUL does unsigned and IMUL signed multiplication. If the operand is a byte, AX ← AL × operand; if the operand is a word, DX:AX ← AX × operand. The operand can be a register or a memory operand (MUL BL, IMUL WORD PTR [SI]). The 8086 has no immediate operand: MUL 5 is invalid.

Whether the product is unsigned or signed changes the bits of the result; unlike addition, a single operation does not serve both readings. For AL = C8h and BL = 03h, MUL BL gives 200 × 3 = 600 = 0258h. With the same bits IMUL BL reads C8h as −56 and gives −56 × 3 = −168 = FF58h. The low bytes (58h) are the same, the high bytes differ. That is why the 8086 has two separate multiplication instructions.

After multiplication only CF and OF are meaningful, and the two are always equal. If the upper half of the result (AH or DX) is really needed, CF = OF = 1. For MUL "needed" means the upper half is nonzero. For IMUL it means the upper half differs from the sign extension of the lower half: if the result fits the lower half as a signed number, the upper half is only copies of the sign bit and carries no information. SF, ZF, AF and PF are undefined after multiplication.

According to Intel's manual, MUL with a register operand takes 70–77 clock cycles for a byte and 118–133 for a word; IMUL takes 80–98 for a byte and 128–154 for a word. If there is a memory operand, a few more cycles are added with the EA time. The time is given as a range because the microcode loop passes through different paths depending on the bits of the multiplier. Since an addition takes only 3 cycles, turning multiplication by a constant into shifts and additions is often worthwhile.

6.5.2DIV and IDIV

Division, as the inverse of multiplication, needs a dividend twice the width of the operand. If the divisor is a byte, the dividend is AX; if the divisor is a word, the dividend is DX:AX. The result is in two parts: the quotient and the remainder. Figure 6.6 shows word division.

IDIV truncates the quotient toward zero and leaves the remainder with the same sign as the dividend. For −23 / 5 the quotient is −4.6; truncated toward zero it becomes −4. The remainder is found from −23 = (−4) × 5 + remainder as −3. With this rule the equality dividend = quotient × divisor + remainder always holds and the absolute value of the remainder is smaller than the divisor.

Definition 6.4

A divide error occurs when the divisor is 0 or when the quotient does not fit in its destination (AL or AX). The processor does not complete the division; it raises interrupt number 0 (INT 0). The first entry of the interrupt vector table (0000:0000) points to the service routine of this interrupt. DOS prints the message "Divide overflow" in this routine and terminates the program.

Whether the quotient will fit is easy to test before the division. In byte division the quotient must not exceed 255; this holds only if AH < divisor. In word division the same rule takes the form DX < divisor. For AX = 1000h (4096) and BL = 2, AH = 10h ≥ 2: the quotient 2048 does not fit in AL and a divide error occurs. For DX:AX = FFFFFFFFh and BX = 1 too, although the divisor is not zero, DX = FFFFh ≥ 1 and the quotient does not fit. In IDIV the limit is slightly narrower: on the 8086 the byte quotient must be in the range −127…127 and the word quotient in −32,767…32,767; −128 and −32,768 also cause a divide error.

The return address that the 8086 pushes on the stack at a divide error carries one more detail. The IP pushed when the INT 0 service is called points to the instruction after the division instruction. On the 80286 and later processors it points to the division instruction itself, so that the instruction can be retried after the error is removed. This difference concerns only programs that write their own INT 0 routine.

6.5.4Preparing for division: CBW and CWD

Because the dividend is always twice the width of the divisor, the dividend must be extended before the division. The number to be divided is often only in AL or in AX; the programmer decides what the upper half (AH or DX) will be.

  • Before an unsigned division the upper half is zeroed: MOV AH, 0 or XOR DX, DX. The value of the number does not change; zeros are only added in front of it.
  • Before a signed division the sign is extended: the sign bit is copied into all the bits of the upper half. The 8086 has two instructions for this.
Definition 6.5

Sign extension is moving a signed number to a wider place while preserving its value: the most significant bit is copied into all the newly added bits. CBW (convert byte to word) extends AL into AX: if bit 7 of AL is 0, AH = 00h; if 1, AH = FFh. CWD (convert word to doubleword) extends AX into DX:AX: if bit 15 of AX is 0, DX = 0000h; if 1, DX = FFFFh. Neither instruction takes an operand and neither changes any flag.

F9h as a signed byte is −7. The same −7 written as a word is FFF9h: 65,536 − 7 = 65,529 = FFF9h. CBW does this conversion in a single instruction and takes 2 clock cycles. CWD takes 5. Both use implicit operands and are one-byte instructions (98 and 99).

Common mistake

Forgetting DX before a word division. A number left in DX from an earlier operation enlarges the dividend. If XOR DX, DX is written instead of CWD for signed −1000, the dividend becomes 0000FC18h = 64,536. IDIV then gives a meaningless result such as 64,536 / 7 = 9219 (2403h). If a large value is left in DX, the quotient does not fit and the program stops with a divide error.

6.5.932 × 32-bit multiplication

The 8086 multiplies at most 16 × 16 bits. The product of two 32-bit numbers is done with the method of multiplication on paper, using 16-bit "digits". If A = A1:A0 and B = B1:B0 are split into halves, then

A × B = A1·B1·232 + (A1·B0 + A0·B1)·216 + A0·B0

holds. Each of the four partial products is computed with one MUL and its 32-bit result lands in DX:AX. The partial products are added to the four words of the result (z3:z2:z1:z0, 64 bits in total) according to their positions. A product multiplied by 216 is shifted left by one word and is added to z2:z1; the one multiplied by 232 is shifted left by two words and is added to z3:z2.

In the example A = 9ABCDEF0h and B = EDCBFEDCh. The partial products are A0 × B0 = DDF1B640h, A1 × B0 = 9A0B8190h, A0 × B1 = CF14F850h and A1 × B1 = 8FBABF14h. In the z0 column there is only B640h. The sum of the z1 column is DDF1h + 8190h + F850h = 257D1h: 57D1h is written and a carry of 2 passes to z2. The z2 column is 9A0Bh + CF14h + BF14h + 2 = 22835h: 2835h is written and a carry of 2 passes to z3. z3 = 8FBAh + 2 = 8FBCh. The result is 8FBC 2835 57D1 B640h. Decimal check: 2,596,069,104 × 3,989,569,244 = 10,357,197,452,617,037,376.

A column carry can be 2, but an ADD gives at most a carry of 1. The code therefore does the additions one at a time and carries the carry of each ADD to the next word up at once with an ADC.

6.6Single-operand instructions

6.6.1INC, DEC, NOT and NEG

The four instructions in the third group take a single operand. The operand can be a register (AX, BX, …, SP or the 8-bit halves) or a memory operand; it cannot be a constant. With a memory operand the size must be specified: INC BYTE PTR [BX]. The instruction reads the operand, changes it, and writes it back to the same place.

  • INC adds 1 to the operand and DEC subtracts 1. They set ZF, SF, OF, PF and AF according to the result, but do not touch CF.
  • NOT inverts every bit. The result is the operand's one's complement. It changes no flag.
  • NEG changes the sign of the operand: it computes 0 − operand. The result is the two's complement: the bits are inverted and 1 is added. It sets all six status flags; CF is 1 if the operand is nonzero and 0 if it is zero.

That INC and DEC preserve CF is a design decision. In multibyte or multiword addition, the loop does an ADC on each iteration and carries the carry to the next iteration. The same loop advances the pointer with INC and decrements the counter with DEC. If these two instructions erased CF, the carry would be lost. The 8086 designers therefore left INC and DEC independent of CF.

INC and DEC are also very short instructions. INC SI, which increments a 16-bit register, is a single byte (46) and takes 2 clock cycles; ADD SI, 1 is three bytes and 4 cycles. On an 8-bit register INC is two bytes (such as FE C0) and 3 cycles, and in memory 15 + EA cycles. NEG and NOT take 3 cycles on a register and 16 + EA in memory.

6.7Shifts and rotates

6.7.1Shifts: SHL/SAL, SHR, SAR

Shift instructions move all the bits of a number in one direction. The bit that leaves at one end falls into CF, and a new bit enters at the other end. The instruction decides what the entering bit will be. It is the software counterpart of the shift register of digital design; the only difference is that the 8086 does the shift with an instruction rather than with a clock pulse. Figure 6.10 shows the bit flow of the three shifts.

Definition 6.7

SHL (shift logical left), and its other name SAL, shifts the bits left; 0 enters from the right and the most significant bit goes out to CF. Each shift multiplies an unsigned number by 2. SHR (shift logical right) is the logical shift: 0 enters from the left and the least significant bit goes out to CF; it divides an unsigned number by 2. SAR (shift arithmetic right) is the arithmetic shift: the most significant bit stays in place and is also copied to the right; it divides a signed number by 2.

With the example 96h = 1001 0110b the three shifts work as follows. SHL AL, 1: the bits move left, b7 (1) goes out to CF, 0 enters from the right: 0010 1100b = 2Ch, CF = 1. The unsigned 150 × 2 = 300 did not fit in a byte; CF shows this. SHR AL, 1: b0 (0) goes out to CF, 0 enters from the left: 0100 1011b = 4Bh = 75 = 150 / 2. SAR AL, 1: the sign bit 1 is copied: 1100 1011b = CBh = −53. In the signed reading 96h = −106 and −106 / 2 = −53 is correct.

The division by SAR rounds differently from that of IDIV. SAR always rounds toward minus infinity; IDIV truncates toward zero. For positive numbers the two give the same result; for negative odd numbers they part. For −7 = F9h, SAR AL, 1 gives FCh = −4 (−3.5 has been rounded down). For −7 / 2, IDIV gives −3. This difference must be kept in mind when turning division into shifting.

RCL and RCR are the key to multiword shifting. To multiply the 32-bit DX:AX by 2, the low word is shifted first and then the high word is rotated through CF: SHL AX, 1 sends b15 of AX out to CF, and RCL DX, 1 brings this bit into b0 of DX. For example with DX:AX = 0000FFFFh, after these two instructions DX:AX = 0001FFFEh. For division the reverse order is followed: SHR DX, 1 and then RCR AX, 1.

6.7.3Shift count, OF and timing

On the 8086 the shift count is given in only two forms: the constant 1 or the CL register. SHL AX, 1 and SHR AL, CL are valid. A constant greater than 1 such as SHL AL, 4 was added on the 80186 and later processors; in 8086 code one writes SHL AL, 1 four times, or MOV CL, 4 and SHL AL, CL. emu8086 accepts this form, but the code it produces does not run on a real 8086. The 8086 does not mask the number in CL; if CL = 255 the shift is done 255 times. The 80286 and later reduce the count to 5 bits (0–31).

OF is defined for shifts and rotates only when the count is 1. In a one-step left shift, OF says whether the sign bit changed: OF = (new b7) XOR CF. 40h = 0100 0000b shifted left becomes 80h; the sign bit turns from 0 to 1 and OF = 1. C0h shifted left also becomes 80h; but the sign bit stays 1 and OF = 0: −64 × 2 = −128 is correct. When the count is given in CL and is greater than 1, OF is undefined.

In terms of time, shifting is much faster than multiplication. According to Intel's manual, SHL reg, 1 on a register takes 2 clock cycles and SHL reg, CL takes 8 + 4n cycles (n is the shift count). In memory a one-step shift is 15 + EA and with CL 20 + EA + 4n cycles. Considering that a byte MUL takes over 70 cycles, it is understandable why multiplication and division by powers of 2 are left to shifting.

6.7.4Code trace: multiplying by shifting

Processors without a hardware multiplier do multiplication with the method on paper. The bits of the multiplier are examined one by one from right to left. If the bit is 1, the current multiplicand is added to the result; at each step the multiplicand is shifted left by one, that is, multiplied by 2. The program below computes 13 × 5 with this method.

org 100h
mov bl, mcand     ; 13

6.7.5Fast multiplication and division with shifts

Multiplication by a constant can be turned into shifts and additions according to the binary expansion of that constant. Since 3 = 2 + 1, AX × 3 = AX × 2 + AX:

; AX ← AX × 3 = AX×2 + AX
mov bx, ax     ; save
shl ax, 1      ; AX × 2
add ax, bx     ; ×2 + ×1
; unsigned ÷ 8 and remainder
mov dx, ax
and dx, 0007h  ; rem = low 3 bits
mov cl, 3
shr ax, cl     ; quotient
; signed ÷ 4
mov cl, 2
sar ax, cl     ; −7 → −2

For AX = 25 the first part gives BX = 25, AX = 50 and finally AX = 75. The total time is about 2 + 2 + 3 = 7 cycles; MUL takes over 118. The second part shows division by a power of 2. Shifting right by n bits divides by 2n; the low n bits that go out during the shift are the remainder. The remainder is taken before the shift with AND: the mask with the low three bits 1 is 0007h. For AX = 100 the remainder is DX = 4 and the quotient AX = 12; 12 × 8 + 4 = 100.

The third part divides a signed number with SAR. For AX = −7, SAR gives −2 in two steps; with IDIV, −7 / 4 would give −1. SAR rounds toward minus infinity and IDIV toward zero. When compilers turn signed division into shifting, they first add (2n − 1) to negative numbers to remove this difference.

6.8Example: array summation

6.8.1The program

Adding two arrays element by element and writing the result to a third array is the simplest example in which arithmetic instructions are used together with memory. The program below does vec3[i] = vec1[i] + vec2[i] for i = 0…3.

org 100h
jmp start
vec1 db 1, 2, 5, 6
vec2 db 3, 5, 6, 1
vec3 db ?, ?, ?, ?
start:
lea si, vec1
lea bx, vec2
lea di, vec3
mov cx, 4
sum:
mov al, [si]
add al, [bx]
mov [di], al
inc si
inc bx
inc di
loop sum
ret

The structure of the program, line by line, is as follows.

  • JMP start jumps over the data definitions. In a .COM program code and data are in the same segment and execution starts at 0100h. Without the jump the processor would try to decode the bytes of vec1 (01 02 05 06) as instructions.
  • vec3 db ?, ?, ?, ? reserves four bytes. The question mark means "the initial value does not matter"; emu8086 writes 0 to these bytes.
  • LEA (load effective address) loads not the value of a variable but its address (offset). SI, BX and DI become the pointers of the three arrays.
  • MOV AL, [SI] reads the memory byte SI points to; this is register indirect addressing. ADD AL, [BX] adds the element of the second array, and MOV [DI], AL writes the result to the third array.
  • The three INCs advance the pointers to the next element. Because the arrays are byte arrays each pointer increases by 1; with word arrays it would have to increase by 2.
  • LOOP sum builds the loop.

The LOOP instruction always uses CX as the loop counter. Each time it executes it first decrements CX by 1 and then looks at CX. If CX is nonzero it jumps back to the label (sum); if zero it goes on to the next instruction (RET). Because CX was loaded with 4 before the loop, the body runs four times: after LOOP, CX becomes 3, 2, 1 and 0 in turn and on the last time the jump is not taken. LOOP does not change the flags. On the 8086 a LOOP that jumps takes 17 clock cycles and one that does not takes 5.

6.9BCD arithmetic

6.9.1Binary-coded decimal numbers

People think in decimal, while the processor computes in binary. In devices that show decimal digits directly, such as calculators, cash register software, clock chips and seven-segment displays, converting the number from binary to decimal every time is expensive. Instead the number can be stored digit by digit.

Definition 6.8

In binary-coded decimal (BCD) representation each decimal digit is held in a separate 4-bit group with the values 0000–1001; 1010–1111 are not used. Unpacked BCD puts one digit in each byte; the high nibble is 0. Packed BCD puts two digits in a byte: the high nibble is the tens digit and the low nibble the ones digit.

Table 6.31: Four representations of the number 14.

In packed BCD the number 14 is written as the byte 14h; the hexadecimal notation reads the decimal digits directly. ASCII digits are unpacked BCD with 3 added to the high nibble. Thanks to this closeness, digits read from the keyboard become unpacked BCD with AND 0Fh, and unpacked BCD becomes a character to write on the screen with OR 30h.

The processor's adder does not know BCD; it adds two BCD numbers like binary numbers. The result is often an incorrect BCD. 9 + 5 gives 0Eh in binary; the correct BCD result is "14". The 8086 therefore offers BCD adjust instructions that convert the result into BCD after a binary operation. All of them work only on AL (and AH when needed); BCD arithmetic is therefore done byte by byte.

The idea of the adjustment rests on the number 6. A nibble can take 16 values while a decimal digit takes 10. When the sum of a digit exceeds 9, the binary sum falls 6 short of BCD: 9 + 5 = 0Eh, whereas "14" = 14h = 0Eh + 6. If the digit sum exceeds 15 (for example 9 + 8 = 17), a carry comes out of the nibble and AF = 1; in this case too 6 must be added: 9 + 8 = 11h, 11h + 6 = 17h. The adjust instructions check these two conditions (nibble value > 9 or AF = 1) and add or subtract 6 if needed.

Rule (AAA and DAA)

AAA: if the low nibble of AL is greater than 9 or AF = 1, 6 is added to AL, 1 is added to AH and AF = CF = 1; otherwise AF = CF = 0. In both cases the high nibble of AL is cleared. AAS makes the same check, but subtracts 6 from AL and 1 from AH.

DAA: First the low nibble is checked: if it is greater than 9 or AF = 1, 06h is added to AL and AF = 1. Then the high digit is checked: if AL before the operation was greater than 99h or CF = 1, 60h is added to AL and CF = 1. DAS subtracts 06h and 60h with the same checks.

The application of the rules is seen in a few examples. 38h + 45h = 7Dh; because the low nibble D > 9, DAA adds 06h and AL = 83h: 38 + 45 = 83. 99h + 01h = 9Ah; the low nibble A > 9, AL + 06h = A0h; because the original AL was greater than 99h, 60h is added too and AL = 00h, CF = 1: 99 + 1 = 100, "00" and the hundreds digit in CF. In subtraction 83h − 38h = 4Bh; because the low nibble B > 9, DAS subtracts 06h and AL = 45h: 83 − 38 = 45. 25h − 48h = DDh; both adjustments are needed and DAS gives AL = 77h, CF = 1. This reads like 125 − 48 = 77: CF = 1 says a borrow was taken from the high digit.

6.9.5AAM and AAD

The adjustment for multiplication and division works differently from that for addition, because adding 6 is not enough here; the number must really be divided by 10 or multiplied by 10. The 8086 offers two instructions that do these two jobs in microcode.

Definition 6.9

AAM (ASCII adjust after multiply) divides AL by 10: the quotient is written to AH and the remainder to AL. It splits the product of two unpacked digits (at most 9 × 9 = 81) into two unpacked digits. AAD (ASCII adjust before division) converts the tens digit in AH and the ones digit in AL to a binary number: AL ← AH × 10 + AL, AH ← 0. It is written before a division instruction. Both set SF, ZF and PF according to AL; OF, AF and CF are undefined.

mov al, 7
mov bl, 8
mul bl      ; AX = 0038h (56)
aam         ; AH = 05h, AL = 06h
mov ax, 0504h  ; '54' unpacked
aad            ; AX = 0036h (54)
mov bl, 6
div bl         ; AL = 09h, AH = 00h

In the first part 7 × 8 = 56 = 0038h. AAM divides 56 by 10: AH = 5, AL = 6. AX = 0506h is now unpacked "56". In the second part AX = 0504h is unpacked "54". AAD converts it to 5 × 10 + 4 = 54 = 36h and zeroes AH. With a normal DIV, 54 / 6 = 9: AL = 09h, remainder AH = 00h. If AAD had been forgotten, DIV would have divided 0504h = 1284 and the result would have been 1284 / 6 = 214.

The machine code of AAM and AAD is interesting: D4 0A and D5 0A. The second byte is 0Ah, that is 10. On the 8086 this byte is really used as the base; D4 10 divides AL by 16. Intel did not document this, and assemblers always produce 0Ah. D4 00 raises INT 0 because it is a division by zero. AAM takes 83 and AAD 60 clock cycles; they are a division and a multiplication done in microcode.

6.10.3Why IMUL and IDIV? The limits

Negative temperatures are the reason for the signed instructions. For c = −10, IMUL reads −10 = F6h as signed and gives AX = FFA6h = −90. IDIV computes −90 / 5 = −18 = EEh; with 32 added, 0Eh = 14: −10 °C = 14 °F is correct. If the same calculation were done with MUL and DIV, F6h would be taken as 246. MUL gives 9 × 246 = 2214 = 08A6h, and when DIV divides this number by 5 the quotient 442 does not fit in AL: the program stops with a divide error.

Integer division throws away the fractional part. Because IDIV truncates toward zero, c = 1 °C gives 33 °F with 9 / 5 = 1; the correct value is 33.8. If rounding is needed, the remainder in AH is examined: if the remainder is greater than half of the divisor, 1 is added to the quotient.

6.13Exercises and solutions

The eleven exercises in the slides are solved here with their intermediate steps.

1. What are AL, CF, OF, SF, ZF, PF after MOV AL, 0C0h and ADD AL, 0C0h?

C0h + C0h = 180h; AL = 80h, CF = 1. −64 + (−64) = −128 fits a signed byte: OF = 0. SF = 1, ZF = 0. 80h has a single 1 bit: PF = 0.

2. After CMP AL, BL, by which flag is unsigned "AL < BL" recognized, and by which flags signed "AL < BL"?

Unsigned: CF = 1 (the subtraction needed a borrow); JB tests this. Signed: SF ≠ OF; JL tests this. SF alone is not enough, because if the difference overflows the sign bit flips.

3. With AX = 0FFFFh, BX = 0FFFFh, what are DX, AX, CF after MUL BX?

65,535² = 4,294,836,225 = FFFE0001h. DX = FFFEh, AX = 0001h. Since the upper half is not zero, CF = OF = 1.

4. What happens with AX = 0100h, BL = 0 on DIV BL?

Division by zero: the processor raises INT 0 (divide error). AX and the flags do not change; control passes to the interrupt service routine.

5. Change the array summation program for word (DW) arrays.

The data are defined with dw, AX is used instead of AL, and the pointers advance by 2:

vec1 dw 1000, 2000, 3000, 4000
vec2 dw 5, 6, 7, 8
vec3 dw 4 dup(?)
; ...
sum:
mov ax, [si]
add ax, [bx]
mov [di], ax
add si, 2
add bx, 2
add di, 2
loop sum

With this data vec3 = 1005, 2006, 3007, 4008. INC SI can be written twice; but ADD SI, 2 changes CF and INC does not. If the loop uses ADC, the INCs are preferred.

6. Write the program that counts the 1 bits in a byte with SHL/TEST and a loop. Compare with PF.

mov al, 0B5h     ; the byte to count
xor bl, bl       ; counter
mov cx, 8
again:
test al, 80h     ; is the leftmost bit 1?
jz zero
inc bl
zero:
shl al, 1
loop again

For B5h = 1011 0101b, BL = 5. In each iteration TEST tests the leftmost bit and SHL brings the next bit to the far left. JZ (jump if ZF = 1) skips incrementing the counter if the bit is 0. PF says only whether the number of 1s is odd or even: for B5h PF = 0 (five is odd), but it does not give how many 1s there are.

7. What happens with DX = 0005h, AX = 0000h, BX = 0005h on DIV BX?

The dividend DX:AX = 50000h = 327,680. The quotient would be 65,536 and does not fit in AX. The rule says so too: DX = 5 ≥ the divisor 5. Even though the divisor is not zero, a divide error (INT 0) occurs.

8. What are AL and OF after MOV AL, 70h and ADD AL, 30h? How is the correct sum obtained in 16 bits?

AL = A0h, OF = 1: 112 + 48 = 160 > 127. The two numbers are extended separately with CBW and added in 16 bits: MOV AL, 70h · CBW · MOV DX, AX · MOV AL, 30h · CBW · ADD AX, DX → AX = 00A0h = 160.

9. Do 00012345h − 0000ABCDh with SUB/SBB. What is CF after the SUB?

2345h − ABCDh = 7778h, CF = 1 (borrow). 0001h − 0000h − 1 = 0000h. The result is 00007778h = 30,584.

10. In the 32 × 32-bit multiplication, to which words of the result are the four partial products added?

A0·B0 → z1:z0; A1·B0 and A0·B1 → z2:z1; A1·B1 → z3:z2. The carry of each ADD is carried to the next word up with ADC; after the third partial product an ADC [z+6], 0 that adds only the carry to z3 may be needed.

11. With DX = 0000h, AX = 0FFFh, CF = 0, what is DX:AX after SHL AX, 1 and RCL DX, 1?

SHL: AX = 1FFEh, the bit that came out (b15 = 0) goes to CF: CF = 0. RCL: 0 enters DX, DX = 0000h. DX:AX = 00001FFEh = 0FFFh × 2.

6.14Summary

  • Flags: arithmetic and logic instructions write the result to the destination and the properties of the result to the flags register. CF shows unsigned overflow (a carry in addition, a borrow in subtraction), OF shows signed overflow; OF = the XOR of the carries into and out of the most significant bit. ZF result zero, SF most significant bit, PF an even number of 1s in the low byte, AF carry from bit 3.
  • Two-operand instructions (ADD, ADC, SUB, SBB, CMP, AND, OR, XOR, TEST) write the result to the destination; CMP and TEST change only the flags. Two memory operands, different sizes, a constant destination and a segment register cannot be used. Register-register takes 3 clock cycles, memory destination 16 + EA.
  • Comparison: after CMP, unsigned "below" is CF = 1, signed "less" is SF ≠ OF.
  • Multiword arithmetic: ADD/SUB on the lowest word, ADC/SBB on the upper ones; the carry is carried in CF. A 32 × 32 multiplication is done with four MULs and a chain of ADD/ADC.
  • Logic instructions work bit by bit; they make CF = OF = 0. AND clears bits, OR sets them, XOR inverts them, TEST tests them. NOT changes no flag.
  • Multiplication and division: in byte operations AX = AL × operand, AL = AX / operand (AH remainder); in word operations DX:AX. MUL/IMUL set only CF = OF meaningfully; DIV/IDIV leave all flags undefined. INT 0 if the divisor is 0 or the quotient does not fit. IDIV truncates toward zero.
  • Sign extension: CBW (AL → AX) and CWD (AX → DX:AX) before signed division and signed addition; for unsigned data the upper half is zeroed.
  • INC and DEC preserve CF; NEG takes the two's complement and sets CF = 1 if the operand is nonzero; NEG 80h overflows.
  • Shifts and rotates: the count is 1 or CL. SHL ×2, SHR unsigned ÷2, SAR signed ÷2 (rounds toward minus infinity). In a rotate no bit is lost; RCL/RCR form a 9- or 17-bit ring with CF and make multiword shifting possible. Multiplying with shifts is much faster than MUL.
  • BCD: AAA/AAS after unpacked addition/subtraction, DAA/DAS after packed addition/subtraction, AAM after multiplication, AAD before division. The adjustment rests on adding or subtracting 6 (and 60h for packed); this is why AF exists.
  • Application: array summation is done with LOOP, temperature conversion with IMUL/IDIV; a signed byte result is correct only within a certain range.

6.16Test Yourself

  1. What are AL and the six status flags after MOV AL, 9Ch and ADD AL, 9Ch?

    Answer

    9Ch + 9Ch = 138h; AL = 38h, CF = 1. Signed −100 + (−100) = −200 does not fit a byte and the sum of two negatives has come out positive (38h): OF = 1. SF = 0, ZF = 0. 38h = 0011 1000b has three 1s: PF = 0. The low nibbles are C + C = 18h; a carry comes out of bit 3: AF = 1.

  2. What CF and OF values does SUB AL, BL give for AL = 30h, BL = 50h, and for AL = 80h, BL = 01h?

    Answer

    30h − 50h = E0h: unsigned 48 − 80 needs a borrow, CF = 1; signed 48 − 80 = −32 is correct, OF = 0. 80h − 01h = 7Fh: unsigned 128 − 1 = 127 is correct, CF = 0; signed −128 − 1 = −129 does not fit and a negative has turned into a positive, OF = 1.

  3. With AL = F0h, BL = 10h, what are AX and CF after IMUL BL and after MUL BL? What would IMUL give if AL = F8h?

    Answer

    IMUL: −16 × 16 = −256 = FF00h; −256 does not fit a byte, CF = OF = 1. MUL: 240 × 16 = 3840 = 0F00h; AH ≠ 0, CF = 1. For AL = F8h (−8) IMUL gives −128 = FF80h; AH is only the sign extension of AL and CF = OF = 0.

  4. The number −50 is divided by 7 and by 8 with IDIV (AX = −50). What are the quotients and remainders? What is found if −50 is shifted 3 bits with SAR?

    Answer

    IDIV 7: −50 / 7 = −7.14; toward zero −7 = F9h, remainder −50 − (−49) = −1 = FFh. IDIV 8: quotient −6 = FAh, remainder −2 = FEh. SAR 3 (÷ 8): CEh = 1100 1110b → 1111 1001b = F9h = −7; −6.25 has been rounded toward minus infinity to −7. IDIV and SAR give different results.

  5. With CF = 1 and AL = 81h, what are AL and CF after three consecutive RCL AL, 1 instructions?

    Answer

    1000 0001b, CF = 1 → 0000 0011b, CF = 1 → 0000 0111b, CF = 0 → 0000 1110b, CF = 0. AL = 0Eh, CF = 0. The bits have turned around the nine-bit ring and none has been lost.

  6. 58 + 67 is done in packed BCD: MOV AL, 58h, ADD AL, 67h, DAA. What are AL, AF and CF after the ADD and after the DAA?

    Answer

    ADD: 58h + 67h = BFh; in the low nibble 8 + 7 = 15 = Fh, no carry (AF = 0), CF = 0. DAA: low nibble F > 9 → +06h = C5h, AF = 1; the original AL BFh > 99h → +60h = 125h, AL = 25h, CF = 1. The result is "25" and 1 in the hundreds digit: 58 + 67 = 125.

  7. With AL = B6h, what are AL and PF after AND AL, 0F0h, OR AL, 05h, XOR AL, 0FFh?

    Answer

    AND: B0h (the low nibble is cleared). OR: B5h (bits 0 and 2 are set). XOR: 4Ah = 0100 1010b (all bits are inverted). There are three 1s: PF = 0. After logic instructions CF = OF = 0.

  8. Which three instructions are written to add 1 to the 48-bit number DX:BX:AX = 0001 FFFF FFFFh? What is the result?

    Answer

    ADD AX, 1 · ADC BX, 0 · ADC DX, 0. AX = 0000h and CF = 1; BX = FFFFh + 0 + 1 = 0000h and CF = 1; DX = 0001h + 0 + 1 = 0002h. The result is 0002 0000 0000h. If INC AX had been used, CF would not have changed and the carry would have been lost.

  9. What are AX, CF and OF after MOV AX, 8000h and NEG AX? Why?

    Answer

    AX stays 8000h; CF = 1 (the operand is not zero), OF = 1. 8000h is −32,768 as a signed word; +32,768 does not fit the word range (at most 32,767). NOT 8000h = 7FFFh, +1 = 8000h.

  10. What is the difference between INC AX and ADD AX, 1 in machine code, time and flags?

    Answer

    INC AX is a single byte (40) and takes 2 clock cycles; it does not touch CF. ADD AX, 1 is three bytes (05 01 00, or three bytes with the 83 form as well) and takes 4 cycles; it writes six flags including CF. With AX = FFFFh, ADD sets CF = 1; INC leaves CF as it was.
