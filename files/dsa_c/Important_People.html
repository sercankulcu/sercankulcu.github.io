Important People in Data Structures and Algorithms with C

Most of the structures and algorithms in this course carry the names of the people who found or popularized them: the AVL tree, the Kruskal and Prim algorithms, Dijkstra's shortest path, Shell sort, Floyd's cycle detection. The books the course builds on also belong to pioneers of the C language and of teaching data structures.

Each card gives the person's years, institution, the contribution that matches something in the course, and links to the slides where that contribution appears. The cards can be filtered by topic, searched, and sorted by surname or by year of contribution.

LE

Leonhard Euler

1707–1783 · St. Petersburg and Berlin academies of sciences

The bridges of Königsberg · 1736

He asked whether a walk exists that crosses each of the seven bridges of Königsberg exactly once. By treating the land masses as vertices and the bridges as edges, he showed that the answer depends only on the connections, not on the shape of the map, and proved that no such walk exists. This 1736 work is regarded as the beginning of graph theory; a closed Euler walk cannot exist unless every vertex has even degree.

WH

William George Horner

1786–1837 · Kingswood School; his own school in Bath

Horner's method · 1819

He is known for a method of approximating the roots of polynomials, which had appeared earlier in Ruffini's work. Today his name is attached mostly to the nested way of evaluating a polynomial: written as a₀ + x(a₁ + x(a₂ + …)), a polynomial of degree n takes only n multiplications. The course uses the same pattern in two places: finding the relative position of an element in an n-dimensional array, and hashing a string with the loop h = h * 31 + c.

AC

Arthur Cayley

1821–1895 · University of Cambridge

Number of labeled trees · 1889

In a short note published in 1889 he stated that n labeled vertices can form nⁿ⁻² different trees. The result first appears in Borchardt's 1860 work, but it is known today as Cayley's formula. The same number counts the spanning trees of the complete graph on n vertices: 4² = 16 for K₄.

WA

Wilhelm Ackermann

1896–1962 · University of Göttingen (doctorate); secondary schools in Burgsteinfurt and Lüdenscheid

Ackermann function · 1928

This logician, a student of Hilbert, defined a function that is computable but not primitive recursive. The two-variable form used in textbooks today is a simplification by Rózsa Péter and Raphael Robinson. The function grows astonishingly fast; its inverse α(n) grows so slowly that the cost of disjoint-set operations using the weighting rule and the collapsing rule is treated as practically constant.

VJ

Vojtěch Jarník

1897–1970 · Charles University, Prague

Growing a single tree for the minimum spanning tree · 1930

This Czech mathematician, whose main field was number theory, published in Czech in 1930 an algorithm that simplified Borůvka's result: the tree starts from a single vertex, and at each step the cheapest edge joining the tree to the rest of the graph is added. The algorithm was rediscovered by Prim (1957) and Dijkstra (1959), which is why it is also called the DJP algorithm or the Prim–Jarník algorithm.

OB

Otakar Borůvka

1899–1995 · Brno (Masaryk University)

The first minimum spanning tree algorithm · 1926

Prompted by the power-grid problem of an electric company in western Moravia, he published the first known minimum spanning tree algorithm in 1926. In each round every component picks the cheapest edge leaving it; since the number of components at least halves in every round, at most log n rounds suffice. His paper, written in Czech, went unnoticed for a long time; when Sollin rediscovered the method in 1965, the algorithm also became widely known as Sollin's algorithm. Because the rounds are independent of each other, it suits parallel computation.

JN

John von Neumann

1903–1957 · Institute for Advanced Study (IAS), Princeton

Merge sort · 1945

In 1945 he wrote a routine for the EDVAC computer that merges two sorted arrays into one sorted array; this routine was part of what is today called merge sort. The manuscript is the oldest surviving example of von Neumann's code. The algorithm is the standard example of the divide-and-conquer approach and of a stable sort that runs in O(n lg n) even in the worst case.

HC

H. S. M. Coxeter

1907–2003 · University of Toronto

Magic square rule · Coxeter groups

One of the leading geometers of the century, he worked on higher-dimensional polytopes and reflection groups. He prepared the editions of W. W. Rouse Ball's Mathematical Recreations and Essays that appeared after 1939; the textbook bases its magic square rule for odd n on him. The rule finishes in n² steps, whereas trying all 25! arrangements by brute force would take millions of centuries. In the course this difference is used to show that a good algorithm matters more than a faster machine.

BF

Richard Bellman and Lester R. Ford Jr.

1920–1984 · 1927–2017 · RAND Corporation

Bellman–Ford algorithm · 1956, 1958

Ford in 1956 and Bellman in 1958 published the single-source shortest path method that allows negative edge weights; the same idea also appears in Shimbel (1955) and Moore. All edges are relaxed for n − 1 rounds; if an edge can still be relaxed in round n, the graph contains a negative cycle. Bellman is one of the founders of dynamic programming, and Ford, together with Fulkerson, of maximum-flow theory.

RP

Robert C. Prim

1921–2021 · Bell Laboratories

Prim's algorithm · 1957

Working at Bell Laboratories at about the same time as Kruskal, he published in 1957 the algorithm that grows a single tree for the minimum spanning tree, rediscovering Jarník's 1930 method independently. Because it always picks the cheapest edge joining a vertex in the tree to one outside it, no cycle check is needed. The algorithm runs in O(n²) with arrays and drops to O(e log n) with a heap, which is faster on sparse graphs.

AL

Georgy Adelson-Velsky and Evgenii Landis

1922–2014 · 1921–1997 · Moscow (ITEP, Moscow State University)

AVL tree · 1962

In their 1962 paper “An algorithm for the organization of information” they defined the first self-balancing binary search tree, which keeps the height difference of the two subtrees of every node at most 1; the structure takes its name from the initials of their surnames. When the balance is broken, a single or double rotation restores it, and the height never exceeds ≈ 1.44 lg n. Adelson-Velsky was also one of the authors of the Kaissa chess program; Landis's main field was partial differential equations.

AP

Alan J. Perlis

1922–1990 · Carnegie Tech, Yale University

Threaded trees · 1960

One of the designers of ALGOL, he received the first Turing Award in 1966. In 1960, together with C. Thornton, he proposed that the null pointers of a binary tree could be used to point to the inorder predecessor and successor; these pointers are called threads. In a threaded tree, inorder traversal needs neither a stack nor recursion. The short maxims often quoted in the course also come from his “Epigrams on Programming,” published in 1982.

DS

Donald L. Shell

1924–2015 · General Electric; University of Cincinnati (doctorate)

Shell sort · 1959

He published his paper “A High-Speed Sorting Procedure” in Communications of the ACM in 1959. Insertion sort is applied to elements that are h positions apart, and h is reduced down to 1; because distant exchanges remove inversions quickly, the Ω(n²) bound of algorithms that exchange only neighbors is broken. The running time depends on the gap sequence, and the sort is not stable because of the distant exchanges.

JK

Joseph B. Kruskal

1928–2010 · Princeton (doctorate); Bell Laboratories

Kruskal's algorithm · 1956

In 1956 he published the method that finds a minimum spanning tree by sorting the edges by cost and adding to the tree every edge that does not form a cycle. The chosen edges first form a forest, which eventually merges into a single tree. The cycle check is done with disjoint sets: if the two ends of an edge are in different sets, the edge is taken and the sets are merged; the total time is O(e log e). He is also known in statistics for his work on multidimensional scaling.

ED

Edsger W. Dijkstra

1930–2002 · Mathematisch Centrum Amsterdam; Eindhoven; University of Texas at Austin

Shortest path algorithm · 1959

In a two-page note published in 1959 he gave both the single-source shortest path algorithm and the Jarník–Prim method for the minimum spanning tree. The shortest path algorithm is greedy: the vertex with the smallest distance is finalized, and the distances of its neighbors are updated through it. This choice is correct only for non-negative edge weights. A pioneer of structured programming, Dijkstra received the Turing Award in 1972.

TH

C. A. R. (Tony) Hoare

1934–2026 · Elliott Brothers; Queen's University Belfast; University of Oxford; Microsoft Research

Quicksort · 1961–1962

He devised quicksort in 1959 in Moscow while working on a dictionary translation; he published the algorithm in Communications of the ACM in 1961 and its detailed analysis in The Computer Journal in 1962. Partitioning around a pivot and sorting the two parts recursively gives an in-place sort that runs in O(n lg n) on average. He also developed Hoare logic for program correctness and received the Turing Award in 1980. He died in Cambridge on 5 March 2026.

SW

Stephen Warshall

1935–2006 · Technical Operations; Massachusetts Computer Associates

Transitive closure · 1962

In 1962 he published the algorithm that computes the transitive closure of a Boolean matrix in O(n³) time: for each k, the rule “if there is a path from i to k and from k to j, then there is a path from i to j” is applied to all pairs. Floyd carried the same scheme over to path lengths in the same year; the algorithm that finds shortest paths between all pairs is therefore called Floyd–Warshall.

RF

Robert W. Floyd

1936–2001 · Carnegie Tech; Stanford University

Floyd–Warshall · cycle detection

In 1962 he published the Floyd–Warshall algorithm, which finds the shortest paths between all pairs of vertices. The “tortoise and hare” method, which finds a cycle in a linked list with two pointers (one advancing one step, the other two), is also attributed to him through Knuth's account; it works with O(1) extra memory. In 1964 he gave the method that builds a heap bottom-up in O(n) time. He received the Turing Award in 1978 for his contributions to program verification.

DK

Donald E. Knuth

b. 1938 · Stanford University

The Art of Computer Programming · since 1968

With the series The Art of Computer Programming, whose first volume appeared in 1968, he made the mathematical analysis of algorithms a discipline; he received the Turing Award in 1974. Many results in the course rest on him: the expected probe counts for linear probing, the constant A ≈ (√5 − 1)/2 suggested for the multiplication method, the gap sequence 1, 4, 13, 40, … for Shell sort, and the account of Floyd's cycle detection. He is also a co-author of the Knuth–Morris–Pratt string search algorithm. Sedgewick is his doctoral student.

BM

Rudolf Bayer and Edward M. McCreight

b. 1939 (Bayer) · Boeing Scientific Research Laboratories; TU München (Bayer); Xerox PARC (McCreight)

B-tree · 1970–1972

Working together at Boeing, they designed the B-tree to keep large sorted indexes on disk; the paper was circulated as a report in 1970 and published in Acta Informatica in 1972. In this balanced m-way tree each node holds many keys and all leaves are on the same level; a node that overflows splits in two, sending its middle key up to its parent. The symmetric binary B-trees that Bayer defined in 1972 are the forerunners of red-black trees.

RT

Robert E. Tarjan

b. 1948 · Stanford (doctorate); Princeton University

Analysis of disjoint sets · 1975, 1984

In 1975 he proved that disjoint-set operations using the weighting rule together with path compression are bounded by the inverse Ackermann function α. His 1984 paper with Jan van Leeuwen (b. 1946, Utrecht University) gave a joint worst-case analysis of union and find methods of this kind. With Sleator he defined splay trees, and with Fredman Fibonacci heaps; he received the Turing Award in 1986.

DR

Dennis M. Ritchie

1941–2011 · Bell Laboratories

The C language · Unix

He developed the C language around 1972 at Bell Laboratories in order to write the Unix operating system; he designed Unix with Ken Thompson and shared the 1983 Turing Award with him. Every structure in the course is built with the tools C provides: struct for records, pointers for links, and malloc and free for run-time memory. The C Programming Language (1978), which he wrote with Kernighan, is the constant companion of the course.

Brian W. Kernighan

b. 1942 · Bell Laboratories; Princeton University

The C Programming Language · 1978

The C Programming Language (1978; second edition for ANSI C in 1988), which he wrote with Ritchie, is known as “K&R” after its authors' initials and became the de facto definition of C. With its short, readable examples, the book also set the model for how a programming language book should be written. He is a co-author of the AWK language (the K in its name is his); he has taught at Princeton since 2000.

Fundamentals of Data Structures · 1976

Fundamentals of Data Structures (1976) and Fundamentals of Computer Algorithms (1978), which he wrote with Sahni, were among the most widely used textbooks in the world for many years. HW

H. C. von Warnsdorff

Des Rösselsprunges einfachste und allgemeinste Lösung (1823)

Warnsdorff's rule for the knight's tour · 1823

In 1823 he proposed the oldest known heuristic for the knight's tour: at each step the knight moves to the square from which the fewest empty squares can be reached next. This order, which uses up the most cramped squares first, usually finds a complete tour on an 8×8 board without ever backtracking. Solving the same problem with plain backtracking takes far longer. The rule is a classic example of how much work good ordering of candidates saves in backtracking.

JŁ

Jan Łukasiewicz

1878–1956 · Universities of Lviv and Warsaw

Polish (prefix) notation · 1924

A Polish logician. In 1924 he invented prefix notation, which puts the operator before its operands so that logical formulas can be written without parentheses, and he used it in his publications from 1929 on. The notation is therefore called Polish notation; the postfix form, in which the operator is written last, is called reverse Polish notation. A postfix expression is evaluated from left to right with a single stack, needing neither parentheses nor precedence rules.

HS

Harold H. Seward

MIT Digital Computer Laboratory (master's thesis, 1954)

Counting sort · 1954

In his 1954 master's thesis at MIT he described counting sort and proposed using it in every pass of radix sort. The algorithm does not compare elements with each other: it first counts how many times each value occurs, then uses the prefix sums of these counts to find each element's place in the output. It sorts n integers in the range 0…k in O(n + k) time and does so stably.

JM

John McCarthy

1927–2011 · MIT, Stanford University

LISP · 1958

He designed the LISP language, which appeared in 1958 and was published in Communications of the ACM in 1960. In LISP, data are represented by generalized lists whose elements may be atoms or other lists; the head of a list is taken with car and its tail with cdr. He also invented garbage collection, which reclaims the memory of list nodes that can no longer be reached, for LISP. He is known as the person who coined the term artificial intelligence and received the Turing Award in 1971.

AK

Arthur B. Kahn

Communications of the ACM 5(11), 1962

Topological sort · 1962

In his paper “Topological sorting of large networks” (1962) he gave the algorithm that finds a topological order of a directed acyclic graph by repeatedly removing vertices of in-degree zero. For each removed vertex, the in-degrees of its successors are decreased by one; a vertex whose in-degree drops to zero joins those waiting. When the waiting vertices are kept in a queue, the method is called Kahn's algorithm. It runs in O(n + e) time; if not all vertices can be removed, the graph contains a cycle.

JW

J. W. J. Williams

1930–2012 · Elliott Brothers (London); Bell-Northern Research (Ottawa)

Binary heap and heap sort · 1964

He invented the binary heap and heap sort in 1963 while working at Elliott Brothers, and published the algorithm in 1964 in Communications of the ACM as “Algorithm 232: Heapsort.” A heap stores a complete binary tree in an array without gaps; parents and children are found by index arithmetic. Heap sort sorts n elements in O(n lg n) in the worst case without an extra array. The same year Floyd added the method that builds a heap bottom-up in O(n) time.

JM

Joseph M. Morris

Information Processing Letters 9, 1979

Morris traversal · 1979

In his paper “Traversing binary trees simply and cheaply” (1979) he showed how to traverse a binary tree inorder without a stack, recursion, or a flag field. A temporary thread is written into the empty right pointer of a node's inorder predecessor; the traversal returns along this thread and erases it. The tree is changed temporarily during the traversal and restored at the end. Extra memory is O(1) and time is O(n).

Thomas H. Cormen, Charles E. Leiserson, Ronald L. Rivest, and Clifford Stein

Dartmouth College, MIT, Columbia University

Introduction to Algorithms · 1990

The authors of Introduction to Algorithms, known as CLRS after their initials. The first edition appeared in 1990 under the names of Cormen, Leiserson, and Rivest; Stein joined the authors in the second edition in 2001. The book is among the supplementary sources of the course: asymptotic notation, recurrence relations, heaps, hash tables, graph algorithms, and the lower bound for comparison sorting are given here with proofs. Rivest shared the 2002 Turing Award with Shamir and Adleman for the RSA cryptosystem.

DM

David R. Musser

Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute

Introsort · 1997

He introduced introsort in his 1997 paper “Introspective Sorting and Selection Algorithms.” The algorithm starts with quicksort and tracks the recursion depth; when the depth exceeds about 2⌊lg n⌋, it finishes that part with heap sort. This keeps the average speed of quicksort while holding the worst case at O(n lg n). The method was developed for the C++ standard library and is common in std::sort implementations. Musser worked with Alexander Stepanov on generic programming and the STL.

TP

Tim Peters

Python core developer

Timsort · 2002

In 2002 he wrote Timsort for Python. The algorithm finds the already sorted stretches in the data (runs), extends short runs with insertion sort, and merges the runs with merge sort. It is stable, runs in O(n lg n) in the worst case, and in O(n) on already sorted data. It was Python's standard sort from version 2.3 through 3.11; Java SE 7 (for object arrays), Android, and V8 also adopted Timsort.
