Important People in Object-Oriented Programming with Java

The 66 people behind the ideas in the course, on 47 cards: from Euclid's algorithm, Babbage's Analytical Engine, Turing's universal machine, and ENIAC to Simula and Smalltalk, James Gosling's Java and the JVM, information hiding and Design by Contract, the Collections Framework, the java.util.concurrent package, the relational model, JUnit, and design patterns.

Each card gives the years the person lived, their country and institution, their best-known contribution, and a short summary; the links lead to the slides where that contribution appears. The cards can be filtered by chapter, searched, and sorted by year of contribution or by surname.

EU

Euclid (Eukleides)

c. 300 BC · Greek · Alexandria

Euclid's algorithm · Elements, c. 300 BC

A Greek mathematician who worked in Alexandria; very little is known about his life. He is the author of the Elements, a 13-book work that organizes geometry and number theory through axioms and proofs. Book VII gives the method for finding the greatest common divisor (GCD) of two numbers. In Euclid's account the smaller number is subtracted from the larger again and again; today's form, written with division and remainder, is a shorthand for the same method: when the remainder is 0, the last divisor is the GCD. It is one of the oldest known algorithms still in use. The relation GCD(a, b) = GCD(b, a mod b) turns into a one-line recursive method, and a class that reduces fractions uses the same method in a hidden helper method.

KH

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi

c. 780 – c. 850 · Baghdad · House of Wisdom

Calculation with Hindu numerals · c. 825

A mathematician and astronomer who worked at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad. His book on calculation with Hindu numerals, written around 825, explains step by step the decimal positional number system and the methods for the four operations with these numerals; the book helped this system spread through the Middle East and Europe. The 12th-century Latin translation of the work became known as Algoritmi de numero Indorum. The word “algorithm” derives from the Latinized form of the author's name, and the word “algebra” comes from his book titled al-Jabr.

BA

Charles Babbage and Ada Lovelace

1791–1871 · 1815–1852 · British · London

Analytical Engine · Note G, 1843

Starting in 1834, Babbage designed the Analytical Engine, a general-purpose machine to be programmed with punched cards; the machine was never completed. In her 1843 notes on the engine, Ada Lovelace gave a step-by-step method for computing Bernoulli numbers (Note G); this method is generally regarded as the first published computer program. Lovelace realized that such a machine could work on symbols other than numbers. The engine's separate “store” and “mill” units and its repeated sequences of operations anticipate today's concepts of memory, processor, and loop.

AC

Alonzo Church

1903–1995 · American · Princeton University

Lambda calculus · 1936

In the early 1930s he developed the lambda calculus, a model of computation based solely on defining anonymous functions and applying them. In 1936 he used this model to prove an undecidability result independently of Turing; this is why the Church–Turing thesis carries both names. Functional programming languages derive from his calculus. Lambda expressions such as x -> x * x, introduced with Java 8, take their name from this calculus as well.

AT

Alan Turing

1912–1954 · British · Cambridge, Manchester

Turing machine · 1936

In his paper “On Computable Numbers” (1936) he defined the Turing machine. He showed that a single universal machine can imitate every machine whose description it is given, and he proved that no algorithm can decide in general whether a program will halt (the halting problem). The universal machine is the idea of treating a program as data; stored-program computers turned this idea into reality. The JVM, a program that runs other programs supplied as bytecode, rests on the same principle. The halting problem is also the reason no Java tool can catch every infinite loop.

FY

Ronald A. Fisher and Frank Yates

1890–1962 · 1902–1994 · British · Rothamsted Experimental Station

Fisher–Yates shuffle · 1938

Two statisticians who worked together at Rothamsted on the design of experiments. In their 1938 book Statistical Tables for Biological, Agricultural and Medical Research they gave a pencil-and-paper way of shuffling a sequence at random: pick one of the items not yet chosen at random, cross it off the list, and write it at the end of the new sequence. The method produces each of the n! orderings of n items with equal probability. Richard Durstenfeld published the in-place swapping form for computers in 1964; Donald Knuth's The Art of Computer Programming popularized it, which is why it is also called the Knuth shuffle. Collections.shuffle uses this form: it moves from the end of the list toward the front and swaps the element at each position with one chosen at random from the elements from the start up to that position. The work takes O(n) time.

AB

John V. Atanasoff and Clifford Berry

1903–1995 · 1918–1963 · American · Iowa State College

Atanasoff–Berry Computer (ABC) · 1942

The physicist Atanasoff designed an electronic calculating machine in 1937 to solve systems of linear equations; with his graduate student Berry he built it at Iowa State College between 1939 and 1942. The ABC kept numbers in binary, carried out operations electronically with more than 300 vacuum tubes, and stored intermediate results in capacitors on rotating drums, a memory that was refreshed regularly. It was not programmable and did not keep its program in memory: it was a fixed-program machine built for a single job. In 1973 a US court invalidated the ENIAC patent and ruled that the idea of the electronic digital computer had been taken from Atanasoff's work.

TF

Tommy Flowers

1905–1998 · British · Post Office Research Station, Dollis Hill

Colossus · 1944

An engineer who worked on telephone exchanges at the British Post Office. He designed Colossus to break messages sent by the German army with the Lorenz cipher machine, and he and his team built it at Dollis Hill. The Mark 1, with 1500 vacuum tubes, was delivered to Bletchley Park in January 1944 and began operating in February; the Mark 2, with 2400 tubes, followed it. Colossus is one of the first programmable electronic digital computers, but its program was not loaded into memory: it was set up with switches and plugboard cables. The work remained a state secret for many years, so Flowers could not talk about what he had done.

ME

John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert

1907–1980 · 1919–1995 · American · University of Pennsylvania, Moore School

ENIAC · 1946

The physicist Mauchly and the engineer Eckert designed and built ENIAC at the University of Pennsylvania's Moore School of Electrical Engineering. The machine, with about 17,500 vacuum tubes, ran its first job in December 1945 and was presented to the public in February 1946. ENIAC was the first large general-purpose electronic computer, but its program was set up with cables and switches; for a new job the machine had to be rewired. This difficulty led to the EDVAC design, which puts the program into memory like data; von Neumann described this design, on which the same team was working, in his 1945 report. In 1946 the two founded their own computer company.

Herman Goldstine and John von Neumann

1913–2004 · 1903–1957 · American · Hungarian-born American · Princeton IAS

Flow diagrams · stored-program design

In the report series “Planning and Coding of Problems for an Electronic Computing Instrument” (1947–48) they planned programs with flow diagrams before writing any code; these diagrams are the forerunners of today's flowcharts. Von Neumann's 1945 “First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC” described a computer that keeps instructions and data in the same memory. Almost all computers, including the computer that runs the JVM, follow this design.

Grace Hopper

1906–1992 · American · US Navy, Remington Rand

A-0 compiler · 1952

In 1952 she wrote A-0, one of the first programs to translate symbolic code into machine code, and she argued that programs should be written with words resembling English; her FLOW-MATIC influenced COBOL (1959). The idea of having a program do the translation lives on every time javac runs. The moth taped into the Harvard Mark II logbook in 1947 with a note about a “bug” is associated with her team and helped popularize the word; engineers had used the word before that.

BN

John Backus and Peter Naur

1924–2007 · 1928–2016 · American · Danish · IBM, Copenhagen

FORTRAN · Backus–Naur Form

Backus led the IBM team that produced FORTRAN in 1957; FORTRAN was the first widely used high-level language, with an optimizing compiler that improved the code it generated. In 1959 he proposed a formal notation for syntax; Naur adopted and refined this notation in the ALGOL 60 report (1960), and it became known as Backus–Naur Form. The Java Language Specification defines Java's grammar with a similar notation, and a message such as ';' expected from javac reports that a rule of this grammar has been violated. Both received the Turing Award (1977, 2005).

JM

John McCarthy

1927–2011 · American · MIT, Stanford University

Lisp · garbage collection

In 1958 he created Lisp at MIT; in his 1960 paper on the language he described garbage collection: memory that the program can no longer reach is reclaimed automatically. Java relies on the same mechanism. When no reference points to an object any longer, the JVM's garbage collector frees it; Java has no operator for deleting an object by hand. He also coined the term “artificial intelligence”; he received the Turing Award in 1971.

Edsger W. Dijkstra

1930–2002 · Dutch · Eindhoven; Austin, USA

Semaphores · structured programming

In 1965 he introduced the semaphore with its P and V operations and posed the dining philosophers problem, the classic example of deadlock; java.util.concurrent.Semaphore offers the same idea through the acquire() and release() methods. In his 1968 letter “Go To Statement Considered Harmful” he argued for structured control flow; Java reserves goto as a keyword but gives it no meaning. He also stressed that testing can show the presence of bugs but never their absence. 1972 Turing Award.

TH

C. A. R. (Tony) Hoare

1934–2026 · British · Oxford, Microsoft Research

Null reference · monitors

In 1965, while designing ALGOL W, he added the null reference to the language; in a 2009 talk he called this decision his “billion-dollar mistake.” Every NullPointerException goes back to this decision. In his 1974 paper “Monitors: An Operating System Structuring Concept” he defined the monitor: a shared object whose procedures run one at a time and whose callers can wait for certain conditions. Java gives every object this kind of lock through synchronized, wait(), and notify(). He also invented quicksort; he received the Turing Award in 1980.

Corrado Böhm and Giuseppe Jacopini

1923–2017 (Böhm) · Italian · Rome

Structured program theorem · 1966

In their 1966 paper “Flow Diagrams, Turing Machines and Languages with Only Two Formation Rules” they showed that every program written as a flow diagram can be rewritten using only three structures: sequence, selection, and repetition. In Java the counterparts of these structures are statements written one after another, the if/switch statements, and the while/for statements; no jump statement is needed to express an algorithm. This result became the theoretical foundation of structured programming.

Ole-Johan Dahl and Kristen Nygaard

1931–2002 · 1926–2002 · Norwegian · Norwegian Computing Center, Oslo

Simula 67 · classes and inheritance

They designed Simula starting from simulation problems. Simula 67 introduced classes, objects created from classes, subclasses that inherit from a class, and virtual procedures that a subclass can redefine. These are the basic ideas of object-oriented programming: Java's class, new, extends, and overridable methods come from here, and C++ began by adding Simula's classes to C. They shared the 2001 Turing Award.

EC

Edgar F. Codd

1923–2003 · British · IBM San Jose

Relational model · 1970

In his 1970 paper “A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks” he proposed storing data as relations, that is, tables made of rows, and reaching data through values rather than by following pointers in the storage structure. Every table created and queried through JDBC is a relation in his sense. A SQL query says which rows are wanted, not how they are stored or how to find them; this idea rests on Codd's principle of data independence. 1981 Turing Award.

NW

Niklaus Wirth

1934–2024 · Swiss · ETH Zurich

Pascal · P-code

He designed Pascal (1970) to teach structured programming. The Pascal-P compilers of the 1970s produced P-code for a simple virtual stack machine, so porting Pascal to a new computer only required writing a small interpreter. Java's bytecode and the JVM follow the same path to run a single compiled program on many platforms. His book Algorithms + Data Structures = Programs (1976) sums up his view of programming. 1984 Turing Award.

Alan Kay

b. 1940 · American · Xerox PARC

Smalltalk · object-oriented programming

In the 1970s he led the group at Xerox PARC that developed Smalltalk. In Smalltalk everything is an object and all computation happens by sending messages to objects. The term “object-oriented programming” is credited to him. A method call in Java such as car.accelerate(10) reflects the same thought: a message is sent to the object, and the object decides how to respond using its own data and behavior. 2003 Turing Award.

David Parnas

b. 1941 · American-born Canadian · Carnegie Mellon, McMaster University

Information hiding · 1972

In his 1972 paper “On the Criteria To Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules” he argued that each module should hide a design decision that is likely to change and expose only an interface to the outside. In Java, private fields behind public methods apply this principle of information hiding: the internal representation of a class can be replaced with another one without changing the code that uses it.

Dennis Ritchie and Brian Kernighan

1941–2011 · b. 1942 · American · Canadian · Bell Labs

C · The C Programming Language, 1978

Ritchie created C at Bell Labs in the early 1970s; the book he wrote with Kernighan (1978) made printing “hello, world” as a first program famous. Per Brinch Hansen

1938–2007 · Danish · Copenhagen; Syracuse University, USA

Monitors · Concurrent Pascal

He proposed the monitor idea in 1972–73, gave it a class notation in his book Operating System Principles (1973), and built the idea into the language Concurrent Pascal (1975). In his article “Java's Insecure Parallelism” (SIGPLAN Notices, 1999) he argued that the synchronized keyword is a weak form of these ideas: nothing prevents unsynchronized methods or non-private fields from being used by several threads at the same time. Lost updates on a shared counter are exactly this kind of bug.

SEQUEL / SQL · 1974

At IBM they designed SEQUEL (1974), an English-like query language for Codd's relational model; a trademark conflict led to the language being renamed SQL, and SQL became an ANSI standard in 1986. Trygve Reenskaug

1930–2024 · Norwegian · Xerox PARC; University of Oslo

Model–View–Controller · 1979

In 1978–79, as a visiting researcher at Xerox PARC, he defined the Model–View–Controller (MVC) structure for Smalltalk: the model holds the data, the view displays it, and the controller handles the user's input. Swing uses a modified form of this separation; a JTextField is the view, while the Document obtained with getDocument() is the model that stores the text. MVC is also one of the standard architectural patterns.

Jim Gray

1944–2007 · American · IBM, Tandem, Microsoft Research

Database transactions · 1981

He worked out how database transactions are carried out with locking and logging; in his article “The Transaction Concept: Virtues and Limitations” (1981) he defined transactions as atomic, consistent, and durable. Bjarne Stroustrup

b. 1950 · Danish · Bell Labs; Texas A&M, Columbia University

C++ · 1983

In 1979 he began work at Bell Labs on “C with Classes,” which added Simula's classes to C; the language was renamed C++ in 1983. Java took most of C++'s syntax for classes, constructors, and the public, private, and protected access modifiers; it deliberately left out multiple inheritance of classes, operator overloading, and explicit pointers. BL

Barbara Liskov

b. 1939 · American · MIT

Abstract data types · substitution principle

In 1974, together with Stephen Zilles, she defined abstract data types, which are specified by their operations rather than their representation, and built them into the language CLU. In her OOPSLA 1987 keynote “Data Abstraction and Hierarchy” she stated the rule now called the Liskov substitution principle: an object of a subtype must be usable anywhere an object of its supertype is expected. She formalized the principle with Jeannette Wing in 1994. Both a correctly built is-a relationship and a polymorphic array that calls the same method on each of its elements rest on this rule. 2008 Turing Award.

BM

Bertrand Meyer

b. 1950 · French · Eiffel Software, ETH Zurich

Design by Contract · Eiffel

In the mid-1980s he built the Eiffel language on the idea of Design by Contract: each routine states a precondition that the caller must satisfy and a postcondition that the routine itself guarantees; at class level a class invariant is also defined. In his book Object-Oriented Software Construction (1988) he also stated the open–closed principle: modules should be open for extension but closed for modification. Because Java has no built-in contracts, the preconditions of public methods are usually checked by throwing IllegalArgumentException, and internal assumptions with assert.

Grady Booch

b. 1955 · American · Rational Software, IBM

Booch method · UML

His book Object-Oriented Analysis and Design with Applications (1991) introduced the Booch method and its drawing conventions for showing classes and the relationships between them. At Rational Software in 1994 he began merging his method with James Rumbaugh's, and in 1995 Ivar Jacobson joined them. The UML class box made of a name, attributes, and operations, and the arrow with a hollow triangle head that shows inheritance, are the usual way of drawing class hierarchies.

James Rumbaugh

b. 1947 · American · General Electric R&D, Rational Software

OMT · UML

At General Electric's research center he led the development of the Object Modeling Technique (OMT), described in the book Object-Oriented Modeling and Design (1991). The life cycle of a thread, which passes through states such as NEW, RUNNABLE, BLOCKED, and TERMINATED, is conventionally drawn with UML state diagrams.

TP

Ken Thompson and Rob Pike

b. 1943 · b. 1956 · American · Canadian · Bell Labs

UTF-8 · 1992

In September 1992 they designed the UTF-8 encoding of Unicode: in this encoding ASCII characters stay a single byte, while other characters take two to four bytes. A letter such as é takes two bytes in UTF-8; when a UTF-8 file is read with the wrong character set, such letters appear garbled. In Java a char is a 16-bit UTF-16 unit; that is why text read from a file is decoded and the charset can be specified explicitly. Thompson is also one of the creators of Unix; the two later designed the Go language together.

Erich Gamma, Richard Helm, Ralph Johnson, and John Vlissides

Vlissides 1961–2005 · Zurich · Sydney · Urbana-Champaign · IBM Research

Design Patterns · 1994

The book Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software (1994), whose authors are known as the “Gang of Four,” collected 23 design patterns in creational, structural, and behavioral groups. James Gosling

b. 1955 · Canadian · Sun Microsystems

Java · 1995

In 1991 Sun's Green project began work on a language called Oak, designed by Gosling for consumer devices; the language, renamed Java, was announced in May 1995, and JDK 1.0 came out in January 1996. This principle is summed up in the slogan “write once, run anywhere.” With Bill Joy and Guy Steele he wrote the book The Java Language Specification (1996).

GJ

Gilad Bracha, Martin Odersky, David Stoutamire, and Philip Wadler

living · Sun Microsystems · University of South Australia · Bell Labs

GJ (Generic Java) · 1998

The GJ design they presented at OOPSLA 1998 added type parameters to Java through erasure: the compiler checks a type such as List<String>, then erases the type arguments and inserts explicit casts where needed, so the result runs on existing JVMs. GJ was chosen as the basis of JSR 14, and generics entered the language with Java 5 (2004). Erasure is the reason new T[10] and List<int> cannot be written. Odersky's GJ compiler became the basis of javac starting with JDK 1.3; Odersky later created Scala, and Wadler is one of the designers of Haskell.

Koen Claessen and John Hughes

living · Swedish · British · Chalmers University of Technology

QuickCheck · 2000

The QuickCheck tool (ICFP 2000) introduced property-based testing for Haskell. Instead of listing sample inputs one by one, the programmer writes a property, for example that reversing a list twice gives back the original list; the tool then tests the property on hundreds of random inputs. Later versions shrink an input that breaks the test down to a small counterexample. The idea has been carried to many languages, including Java libraries such as jqwik and junit-quickcheck.

RH

D. Richard Hipp

b. 1961 · American · Charlotte, North Carolina

SQLite · 2000

In 2000 he started SQLite: a database engine that keeps the whole database in a single file, needs no server, and is released into the public domain. Its developers describe it as the most widely used database engine. Because no separate server is needed, a JDBC driver and a connection URL such as jdbc:sqlite:school.db are enough for a Java program to create tables and run SQL.

Tim Peters

living · Python core developer

TimSort · 2002

In 2002 he wrote TimSort for Python's list sorting. The algorithm finds the already sorted stretches in the data (runs), extends short runs with insertion sort, and merges the runs with merge sort. It is stable: the order of items that compare as equal is preserved. It takes O(n log n) in the worst case and about n comparisons on already sorted data. Java SE 7 switched the sorting of object arrays to TimSort; Arrays.sort(Object[]), as well as List.sort and Collections.sort, which sort lists based on it, use this algorithm. Arrays.sort(int[]), which sorts primitive arrays, uses the Dual-Pivot Quicksort of Vladimir Yaroslavskiy, Jon Bentley, and Joshua Bloch.
