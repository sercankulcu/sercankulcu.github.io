3.3.1PowerPoint and the workspace

Microsoft PowerPoint is the presentation program in the Microsoft 365 suite. Word produces a text document and Excel a spreadsheet; PowerPoint produces a file made of pages that appear on screen one after another. Each of these pages is called a slide. These notes are written for the English interface of Microsoft 365 PowerPoint on Windows. In the web, Mac, and mobile versions of PowerPoint the location of menus may differ a little, but the concepts are the same.

3.3.1.1What is presentation software for?

Two closely related terms are used with different meanings. The file itself is called a deck (or presentation file), and the act of delivering it in front of an audience is called a talk. For example, one says “the deck has 20 slides” but “the talk lasted 10 minutes”. This distinction is kept throughout the notes.

Definition 3.3.1

A slide is a page that fills the screen at one time; it can carry text, pictures, shapes, tables, charts, audio, and video. A presentation file (a deck) is a file made of slides that will be shown in a particular order. A talk is the delivery a speaker gives accompanied by these slides.

PowerPoint is used most of all to support a live talk. But its uses are not limited to that (Table 3.3.1).

Table 3.3.1: The main uses of PowerPoint.
UseRole of the deckExample
Lectures and talksSupports the speaker's delivery visuallyA lecture, a seminar talk
Project presentationStructures purpose, method, findings, and conclusion in a particular orderA graduation project defense
ReportingSummarizes data and findings with chartsA monthly energy-consumption report
Training materialOrganizes steps, examples, and exercisesTraining on how to use a lab instrument
Information screen (kiosk)Advances by itself with nobody touching it and returns to the startThe announcement screen at the faculty entrance
Recording and videoProduces an output that can be watched together with narrationA lecture video for distance education

What these uses have in common is this: a slide does not replace what is being said; it supports it. The slides of a good deck hold the key points, numbers, and visuals. The detail is in the speaker's words, in the speaker notes, or in a separate document.

3.3.1.2The workspace

When PowerPoint is opened and a new deck is created, five main regions share the screen (Figure 3.3.1). At the top is the ribbon, on the left the slide pane, in the middle the slide area, below it the notes pane, and at the bottom the status bar.

Definition 3.3.2

The ribbon is the tool area that shows commands divided into tabs such as File, Home, Insert, Design, Transitions, Animations, Slide Show, Review, and View. In each tab the commands are grouped by function. A contextual tab is a tab that appears only while a particular object is selected; for example, when a picture is selected the Picture Format tab appears, and when a table is selected the Table Design tab.

The roles of the regions are summarized in Table 3.3.2. Two of them are especially important. The slide pane is used to manage the deck as a whole, and the notes pane is for the speaker.

Table 3.3.2: The regions of the PowerPoint workspace.
RegionWhat it showsWhat it is used for
RibbonTabs and command groupsInsert, format, transition, animation, and show commands
Slide paneNumbered thumbnails of the slidesSelecting, reordering, duplicating, deleting, and hiding slides
Slide areaA large view of the selected slideTyping and editing the content
Notes paneThe speaker note for the selected slideWriting explanations that do not fit on the slide, the timing, and reminders
Status barSlide number, language, view buttons, zoomSeeing the position, switching quickly between views

In the dashed boxes in the slide area, texts such as “Click to add title” appear. These boxes are called placeholders; they do not appear in the show and only indicate what is to be written where. The type and position of each box are determined by the slide's layout.

Common mistake

Looking on the Home tab for the commands needed to edit a picture. Commands such as Crop, Compress, and Remove Background are on the Picture Format contextual tab, and this tab is visible only while the picture is selected. If the command being looked for is not on the ribbon, first check whether the relevant object is selected.

3.3.1.3Presentation views

The same deck can be shown in different ways for different jobs. PowerPoint calls these views. Views are chosen from the View tab or with the buttons in the status bar. Changing the view does not change the content of the file; it changes only how it is shown on screen.

Table 3.3.3: PowerPoint views and their purposes.
ViewWhat is on screenWhen it is used
NormalThe slide pane, the slide, and the notesWhen editing slide content; the default view
Outline ViewHeadings and bullets as textWhen building the content as text, before design
Slide SorterAll the slides in a gridTo see the flow as a whole, to reorder, to divide into sections
Notes PageThe slide on top, the notes below, in page layoutWhen preparing speaker notes in a form that will be printed
Reading ViewThe deck plays inside the windowTrying out the show without going full screen
Slide MasterThe master slide and the layouts tied to itManaging the common design that affects the whole deck
Slide ShowSlides full screen, one at a timeWhen presenting to the audience

Figure 3.3.2 shows the difference between two views. Normal view suits working on a single slide. Slide Sorter shows the deck as a whole: how many slides each section holds, which slides resemble one another, and where the flow breaks are easily noticed in this view.

Example 3.3.1

A student is working on a 35-slide graduation deck. Which view is the most suitable for each of the following jobs?

  1. The advisor said, “The method slides should come before the findings.” The position of several slides will change. All the slides need to be seen at the same time and moved by dragging: Slide Sorter.
  2. There are no slides yet; only the headings and bullets are known. The content will be entered quickly, as text: Outline View.
  3. The university logo will go in the bottom-right corner of all the slides: the common design changes instead of slide by slide, Slide Master view.
  4. The speaker notes will be printed on paper and held in the hand at rehearsal: to see the layout of the notes on the page, Notes Page.

None of the four jobs is impossible in Normal view; but each is done several times faster in the suitable view.

3.3.2Planning the talk

Starting to prepare a deck by opening PowerPoint is a common habit. Yet the most important part of a good talk is the plan made before the first slide is drawn. The plan answers three questions: What is this talk for? Who is it for? If the audience will remember a single sentence as they leave the room, which sentence is it?

3.3.2.1Purpose, audience, and main message

The elements of the plan, in order, are: purpose, audience, main message, evidence and examples, flow, and closing. The purpose says why the talk is given: to inform, to persuade, to get a decision made, or to teach. The audience determines how the same subject will be presented. For example, a solar-panel project is presented to faculty jury members with technical detail and to high-school students with examples from daily life.

Definition 3.3.3

The main message is the one-sentence conclusion that all the parts of the talk support. “Sensor-controlled lighting reduces the laboratory's electricity use by about a third” is a main message; “Lighting systems” is merely a topic heading.

The flow usually has three parts (Figure 3.3.3). The introduction builds an expectation in the audience by saying why the subject matters. The body develops, in order, a few ideas that support the main message; beside each idea is a piece of evidence, a number, or an example. The conclusion states the main message again, plainly. Three practical rules come out of this structure:

  • Every slide has a single, clear purpose. If a slide holds two separate ideas, two slides are usually needed.
  • Slides are not a transcript of the talk. If every sentence the speaker will say is written on the slide, the audience stops listening and starts reading.
  • The closing gives no new information; it gathers the main message. A “Thank you” slide alone is not a closing.
Common mistake

Mistaking the title of the talk for the main message. A title such as “Energy Use in the Laboratory” does not tell the audience what to think. A main message contains a judgment and can be supported by a number: “Half of the laboratory's night-time consumption comes from devices left on.”

3.3.2.2A slide plan based on time

Talks often have to fit a particular time. That is why the number of slides is calculated from the time before the content. There is a rule of thumb found through experience: a content slide carries on average 1–1.5 minutes of speech. Slides that are not talked over, such as a title slide or a sources slide, are not counted in this calculation.

Example 3.3.2

A 15-minute session is given for a project talk. Part of the session will be set aside for questions and answers. A slide plan is to be drawn up.

  1. The question-and-answer time is written into the plan first: a 15-minute session = 10 minutes of talk + 5 minutes of questions.
  2. The 10 minutes are shared among the parts: introduction and problem 2 min, method 3 min, findings 4 min, conclusion 1 min. The total is 2 + 3 + 4 + 1 = 10 minutes.
  3. Each part is given a number of slides that fits its time (Table 3.3.4). The total of the content slides is 2 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 9.
  4. It is compared with the rule of thumb: 10 ÷ 9 ≈ 1.1 minutes/slide. This value is within the range 1–1.5; the plan is realistic.
  5. The title and sources slides are added. The file has 11 slides, but 9 enter the time calculation.
Table 3.3.4: A time-based slide plan for a 10-minute talk.
PartTimeSlidesContent
Introduction and problem2 min2The topic and why it matters
Method3 min3One slide per step
Findings4 min3One finding and one chart per slide
Conclusion1 min1Repetition of the main message
Total10 min9+ title and sources slides

In the findings part, three slides fall to four minutes; because the charts are interpreted in the slides of this part, the time per slide is a little longer. The rule of thumb is not a strict rule but a warning threshold. Its real use is that it catches unrealistic plans at once. For example, if 30 slides have been prepared for 10 minutes, each slide gets 600 ÷ 30 = 20 seconds. In that time a chart can only be shown, not explained.

Some details may be needed if a question comes up: a long results table or the intermediate steps of a calculation. These are added to the end of the deck as hidden slides. Hidden slides are skipped in the normal show and opened when needed.

Common mistake

Estimating the time only by looking at the number of slides and not rehearsing at all. The rule of thumb is only a starting point. The real time is measured only by a spoken rehearsal; PowerPoint's Rehearse Timings tool records this measurement slide by slide.

3.3.2.3Outline View

The fastest way to pour the plan into slides is View → Outline View. In this view the deck looks like an indented list of text. Every line has a level. A line at the top level is the title of a slide; a line one level lower is a bullet of that slide.

Definition 3.3.4

The outline is the display of a deck's titles and bullets as text together with their levels. In the outline, Enter opens a new line at the same level; Tab lowers the level of the line by one (turns a title into a bullet), and Shift + Tab raises it by one (turns a bullet into a title).

The real power of the outline is this: as the levels change, the number of slides changes by itself. Figure 3.3.4 shows how a five-line outline takes shape in three steps. The operations are followed in order in Table 3.3.5.

Table 3.3.5: Level operations in Outline View and the slide count.
StepActionOutlineSlide count
1Five lines are typed, with Enter after eachProject Proposal / Purpose / Measure usage / Reduce cost / Method5
2Tab on the “Measure usage” line“Measure usage” becomes a bullet of Purpose4
3Tab on the “Reduce cost” line“Reduce cost” also becomes a bullet of Purpose3
4Enter at the end of the “Method” line, then TabThe new line becomes a bullet of Method; “Install sensors” is typed3

The fourth step needs care. When Enter is pressed at the end of a title line, the new line opens at the same level as the title; that is, a new, empty slide is created at that moment. When Tab is pressed, this empty slide turns into a bullet of the previous slide and the slide count falls to three again. In the outline, the only thing that determines the slide count is the number of top-level lines.

The outline applies the principle of content first, design second. While titles and bullets are being typed, font, color, and pictures are not thought about. After the flow has settled, a layout and a theme are applied to the slides. That way the effort spent on design does not go to slides that will be deleted later.

Common mistake

Forgetting to press Tab when meaning to type a bullet in the outline. Every new line opened with Enter is at title level; five lines typed thinking they were bullets create five separate slides. If the slide pane shows an unexpected number of empty-looking slides, the first place to look is the levels in the outline.

3.3.3File and slide management

When the plan is ready, it is time for the file and the slides. This section covers the format the deck will be saved in, the templates from which slides will be created, and how to keep slides in order in a long deck.

3.3.3.1New deck and file formats

A new deck is started empty or from a template with File → New. The file format chosen when the deck is saved determines how the file will open on another computer. The same content behaves very differently in different formats (Figure 3.3.5).

Table 3.3.6: The main file formats of PowerPoint.
ExtensionWhat kind of file?On double-clickAnimation
.pptxStandard, editable deckPowerPoint opens in Normal viewYes
.ppsxShow fileThe slide show starts directlyYes
.potxTemplateA new, untitled deck opens from the templateYes
.pdfFixed-layout documentThe pages appear in a PDF readerNone; the final state of the slide
.mp4VideoPlays in a video playerPlays according to the timings
Definition 3.3.5

A template is a file, used as the starting point for new decks, that carries a theme, layouts, and sample content. When a template is opened, what is edited is not the template itself but an untitled copy derived from it; the template is thus not damaged.

The difference between Save and Save As is also important. Save overwrites the open file. Save As creates a new file; the original stays unchanged and PowerPoint now works on the new file. Saving a deck as .ppsx or .pdf does not delete or convert the original .pptx file.

Example 3.3.3

A student has prepared a graduation deck (deck.pptx). The deck will go to three places. Which format is suitable for each?

  1. The advisor will read the deck and correct the slides: it must be editable, so .pptx is sent.
  2. A printed copy will be given to the jury members: the page layout must stay the same on every printer, so .pdf. A PDF has no animations; every slide is printed in its final state.
  3. It will loop as a promotion on the screen at the faculty entrance: the show must start directly when opened and nobody should see the editing view, so .ppsx. If the computer does not have PowerPoint, or can only play video, .mp4 is chosen.

All three outputs are produced from the same .pptx file with Save As or Export. If a correction is needed later, only the .pptx is corrected and the other formats are produced again.

Common mistake

Keeping the deck only as a PDF. Editing slides from a PDF is laborious, and animations and speaker notes are lost. Whatever the sharing format, the editable .pptx file is always kept separately.

3.3.3.2Slide layout and placeholders

A slide can be started from a blank page, but PowerPoint offers ready-made patterns instead. These patterns are called layouts. A layout determines which boxes a slide will have and where these boxes will sit.

Definition 3.3.6

A placeholder is a dashed-frame box on a slide reserved for a particular kind of content: title, text, content (text, table, chart, or picture), picture, and so on. A slide layout is the pattern that defines the type, number, and position of the placeholders.

Figure 3.3.6 shows the six most frequently used layouts. The Title Slide is for opening the deck. Title and Content is the ordinary slide carrying a title and a bullet list or a single chart. Two Content puts two things side by side; Comparison adds a heading to each of them. Title Only leaves an empty area beneath to fill freely. The Blank layout has no placeholders at all.

The layout of a slide can also be changed later with Home → Layout. In that case the text on the slide does not disappear; it moves into the suitable placeholders of the new layout. For example, if a slide in the Title and Content layout is turned into Two Content, the bullets move to the left box and the right box stays empty.

Using placeholders has three benefits. First, titles sit in the same place and in the same format on every slide; the audience's eye does not search for the title on each slide. Second, the text in a placeholder shows in Outline View; text in a hand-drawn text box does not. Third, screen readers read the slide title from the placeholder. A slide whose title is typed in a free text box looks “untitled” to a screen reader.

Common mistake

Opening every slide with the Blank layout and adding the title and bullets one by one with text boxes. Although the slides look tidy at first glance, the position of the titles drifts by a few pixels from slide to slide. In the outline these slides appear without titles, and when the theme changes the text boxes do not fit the new design.

3.3.3.3Adding, duplicating, moving, and hiding slides

The basic slide operations are done in the slide pane, by right-clicking the thumbnails, or from the Home tab (Table 3.3.7).

Treating a hidden slide as deleted. A hidden slide is inside the file: anyone who receives the file can open it and see it. Content that must not be shared is removed by deleting, not by hiding.

3.3.3.4Sections

In a deck of forty or fifty slides the slide pane becomes a long strip and navigating gets hard. Sections solve this problem. A section is a group of consecutive slides that has a name. Sections are only for editing and navigation; they are not visible in the show.

Definition 3.3.7

A section is a group of consecutive slides shown with a name in the slide pane and in Slide Sorter. A section starts at the selected slide and runs until the start of the next section. A section can be moved, collapsed, renamed, and deleted as a whole.

The greatest benefit of sections is moving in bulk. A group of slides left in the wrong place is put where it belongs without dragging the slides one by one. Figure 3.3.8 and Table 3.3.9 give an example step by step. In a seven-slide deck the method slides (Survey, Sample) have by mistake ended up after the findings.

Table 3.3.9: Adding and moving sections.
StepActionSections (slide numbers)Slide order
1StartNo sectionsTitle, Purpose, Finding 1, Finding 2, Finding 3, Survey, Sample
2Slide 1 selected, Add Section, name “Opening”Opening (1–7)Unchanged
3Slide 3 selected, Add Section, name “Findings”Opening (1–2), Findings (3–7)Unchanged
4Slide 6 selected, Add Section, name “Method”Opening (1–2), Findings (3–5), Method (6–7)Unchanged
5On the Method section, right-click → Move Section UpOpening (1–2), Method (3–4), Findings (5–7)Title, Purpose, Survey, Sample, Finding 1, Finding 2, Finding 3
6Right-click → Collapse AllOpening (2), Method (2), Findings (3)Unchanged

Between the second and fourth steps the slide order never changes; adding a section only groups. The real change is in the fifth step. The Method section moves up one place together with the two slides inside it, and the numbers are reassigned: the number of Survey falls from 6 to 3, and that of Finding 1 rises from 3 to 5. To do the same job without sections, the two slides would have to be dragged separately, dropping each in the right place every time.

Common mistake

Thinking that section names will show in the show as subheadings. A section name is visible only on the editing screen. If you want the audience to see the change of section, a separate title slide is put at the start of the section.

3.3.3.5Zoom and hyperlinks

A show normally runs linearly: 1, 2, 3, … Sometimes this order is not enough. In the question-and-answer period you may need to return quickly to a detail, or to choose one of the sections according to the audience's interest. PowerPoint has two tools for this: zoom and the hyperlink.

Definition 3.3.8

Summary Zoom is a contents slide made of clickable thumbnails of the chosen sections. Clicking a thumbnail goes to that section; when the section ends, the show returns to the summary slide. Section Zoom and Slide Zoom jump to a single section or slide. All are added with Insert → Zoom.

A hyperlink ties an object or text to another destination: a slide in the same deck, another file, or a web address. A hyperlink is added with Insert → Link. Action buttons (Insert → Shapes → Action Buttons) are shapes with ready-made targets such as “next”, “previous”, “first slide”, and “home”. In a kiosk show that does not advance by clicking, hyperlinks and action buttons are the only tools that let the viewer navigate.

Zoom is available in current PowerPoint versions. When the deck is exported to PDF, the zoom thumbnails remain static pictures and the movement is not seen. A nonlinear flow built with zoom therefore becomes linear by itself in a PDF.

Common mistake

Relying on a web hyperlink during a talk and not preparing an offline backup. If the hall has no internet connection, the link goes nowhere. A screenshot of the page the link would open is put on a backup slide.

3.3.4Theme, Slide Master, and design

The secret of a professional-looking deck is not decoration but consistency. All the titles sit in the same place, in the same font and the same color. The same colors mean the same thing on every slide. PowerPoint builds this consistency with two tools: the theme and the Slide Master.

3.3.4.1Theme and Slide Master

Definition 3.3.9

A theme is the design set that defines a deck's color palette, title and body fonts, effects, and background together; it is chosen from the Design tab. The Slide Master is the topmost slide that carries the rules common to all layouts: the position of placeholders, the font, color, logo, footer, and so on.

The way to understand the Slide Master is to see rules flowing downward in a hierarchy (Figure 3.3.10). At the top is the slide master. Beneath it are the layouts tied to it (Title Slide, Title and Content, Two Content, …). At the bottom are the real slides that use those layouts. A rule on the slide master passes to the layouts, and a rule on the layouts passes to the slides.

The Slide Master is entered with View → Slide Master. In this view, in the left pane, the slide master appears at the top with a large thumbnail and the smaller layout thumbnails beneath it. Items that must be on every slide, such as a logo, an institution name, or a slide number, are added here once. They also appear by themselves on slides added later.

The theme and the Slide Master work together. The theme chooses color and font; the Slide Master determines where and how these choices are applied on the slide. When the theme is changed, the slide master and all the layouts are redrawn according to the new theme.

Common mistake

Adding the logo to the first slide and duplicating that slide as many times as needed. Every logo created this way is a separate object. When the logo changes, it has to be changed on all the slides one by one; on slides added with a new layout, the logo does not appear at all. The place for an item that will be on every slide is the slide master.

3.3.4.2Changes in the Slide Master and manual formatting

The rules of the slide master do not always win. Formatting given directly, by hand, to an object on a slide takes precedence over the rule coming from the slide master. If this precedence is not known, it is not understood why a change made in the Slide Master does not show on some slides.

Definition 3.3.10

Manual formatting locks only the property that was changed; the other properties continue to come from the slide master. 3.3.4.4Visual hierarchy

When the audience first looks at a slide, the eye goes somewhere. This first look lasts a few seconds. The designer's job is to make the eye go first to the most important information.

Definition 3.3.11

Visual hierarchy is showing the order of importance of the elements on a slide through size, weight, color, position, and space. The most important element is the largest or most prominent; detail is written smaller and source information faintest.

Example 3.3.6

In the slide in Figure 3.3.12, 1,200 students enrolled in 2023 and 1,656 in 2024. Let us find the rate of increase and rearrange the slide.

  1. Amount of increase: 1,656 − 1,200 = 456 students.
  2. Rate of increase: 456 ÷ 1,200 = 0.38, that is, 38%.
  3. This rate is the main finding of the slide. The title states the result, not the topic: instead of “Enrollment results”, “Enrollments rose 38%”.
  4. 38% becomes the largest element on the slide. Below it, in small type, go 1,200 → 1,656 students and a short reason (new departments and scholarships).
  5. The source (Registrar's Office, 2024) is written smallest and faintest; it is read when needed but does not draw attention.

In the previous slide the five sentences have the same weight. The audience has to read all of them to find the most important piece of information. In the revised slide the eye goes first to 38%; the rest is read if needed.

Visual hierarchy has three tools. The title states the topic or the main result directly. Size and color show the order of importance; they are not used for decoration. Space and alignment also group the elements that belong together and draw a path for the eye. Elements that stand close together are perceived as related; space is left between unrelated ones.

Common mistake

Making everything bold and colored to emphasize the important information. If everything is emphasized, nothing is emphasized. On a slide usually a single element is brought forward.

3.3.4.5Text on a slide

A talk slide is not a document page. The audience cannot read a long text and listen to the speaker at the same time; they choose one of the two, and usually they choose reading. That is why the text on a slide is kept short (Figure 3.3.13).

There are six principles for slide text:

  • Short phrases: The slide holds the main point, not the whole speech. Key phrases are often enough instead of full sentences.
  • Readable size: Think of the audience member at the very back of the hall. Text that cannot be read from the back row might as well not be on the slide.
  • Consistent language: The items in a list are written with the same grammatical structure. If one reads “Collecting the data” and another “We analyzed”, the list looks disorderly.
  • Contrast: The text color is clearly separated from the background. A projector shows colors fainter than a computer screen.
  • Line length: A line is kept short enough for the eye to follow at a glance.
  • Speaker note: An explanation that does not fit on the slide is not deleted but moved to the notes pane.
Common mistake

Shrinking the font when the text does not fit. PowerPoint can shrink overflowing text by itself; but small text does not solve the problem, it only hides it. If the text does not fit, it is either shortened or split over two slides.

3.3.5Pictures, tables, and charts

The saying “a picture is worth a thousand words” is largely true for talks, but only for the right picture. A visual that does not explain the topic is nothing more than a decoration taking up space on the slide. This section explains when and how to use pictures, shapes, tables, and charts.

3.3.5.1Using pictures

A picture is added to a slide with Insert → Pictures. When a picture is selected, the Picture Format contextual tab opens; the crop, size, compress, and alt text commands are there. Six points are watched when using a picture:

  • Purpose: The visual explains the topic or proves a claim. A photo of the measurement setup explains the method; a random stock photo of “a person working at a computer” explains nothing.
  • Resolution: The picture has enough detail to look sharp on the presentation screen. A very small picture turns blurry when enlarged.
  • Cropping: Brings the important region forward. When cropping, the ratio is kept; the picture is not stretched by pulling in only one direction.
  • Alt text: Tells an audience member who uses a screen reader what the visual is about.
  • Source: The license and attribution terms of someone else's visual are checked.
  • Consistency: Similar visuals are used with the same ratio and placement.
Definition 3.3.12

The aspect ratio is the ratio of a picture's width to its height. A 4000 × 3000-pixel photo has the ratio 4000 ÷ 3000 = 4 : 3, and a 1920 × 1080-pixel screen has 16 : 9. If the ratio is not kept when a picture is resized, the image flattens or stretches.

Example 3.3.7

A 4000 × 3000-pixel (4 : 3) photo is to fill a 16 : 9 slide exactly. How is the photo brought to this ratio without stretching, and how much of the picture remains?

  1. The width is kept: 4000 pixels. At the ratio 16 : 9 the height must be 4000 × 9 ÷ 16 = 2250 pixels.
  2. 3000 − 2250 = 750 pixels are cropped from the height. Depending on the subject, these rows are cut from the top, from the bottom, or shared between the two.
  3. The remaining pixel count is 4000 × 2250 = 9,000,000. Of the original 4000 × 3000 = 12,000,000 pixels, 9,000,000 ÷ 12,000,000 = 0.75, that is, 75%, remains.

It is also possible to reach the same result by stretching, that is, by pulling the 4 : 3 picture horizontally by a factor of 16/9 ÷ 4/3 = 4/3 ≈ 1.33. But then every circle in the picture turns into an ellipse and every square into a rectangle (Figure 3.3.14).

Common mistake

Enlarging a picture by pulling the handle in the middle of an edge. A handle in the middle of an edge changes only one dimension and breaks the ratio. To keep the ratio, a corner handle is used or Shift is held down.

3.3.5.2Picture size and compression

Even phones now take photos above 12 megapixels. If these photos are added to a slide as they are, the deck file quickly reaches hundreds of megabytes. Yet the screen can show only a fraction of these pixels.

Example 3.3.8

A 4000 × 3000-pixel photo will be shown full screen on a 16 : 9 slide. The projector gives a 1920 × 1080-pixel image. If every pixel is stored uncompressed in 3 bytes (one byte each for red, green, and blue), how much of the photo is unnecessary?

  1. Raw size of the photo: 4000 × 3000 × 3 = 36,000,000 bytes, that is, 36 MB.
  2. Raw size of the image that fills the screen: 1920 × 1080 × 3 = 6,220,800 bytes, that is, about 6.2 MB.
  3. Ratio: 36,000,000 ÷ 6,220,800 ≈ 5.8. The photo carries about 5.8 times more data than the screen can show.

The extra pixels are not visible on screen; they only enlarge the file, slow down opening and saving, and make sending by e-mail harder. In a real file the pictures are stored compressed as JPEG or PNG, so the sizes are smaller. But the ratio is roughly preserved.

Definition 3.3.13

ppi (pixels per inch) shows how many pixels fall on a length of one inch (2.54 cm) of a picture. When the physical size of the picture on the slide is fixed, if ppi rises, the number of pixels, and therefore the file size, rises too.

The Picture Format → Compress Pictures command reduces the picture to a target ppi value. A value suitable for the intended use is chosen: for example 220 ppi for print, 150 ppi for web and screen, and 96 ppi for e-mail. In the same window there is also the option “Delete cropped areas of pictures”.

Example 3.3.9

A 16 : 9 slide measures 13.333 × 7.5 inches (33.867 × 19.05 cm). If a picture that completely fills the slide is compressed to different ppi values, how many pixels does it become?

If “delete cropped areas” is not chosen when compressing, the file size does not shrink. Thinking the file shrinks when a photo is cropped. The cropped area stays in the file until it is deleted during compression. After the cropped areas are deleted, the crop can no longer be undone; this step is done after the deck is finished.

3.3.5.4Tables

A table is the clearest way to compare numbers or attributes in rows and columns. But a table in a talk differs from a table in a report. The person reading a report can spend as much time as they like; the person watching a talk has a few seconds.

When preparing a table for a talk, the following are watched:

  • The header row explains the meaning and unit of each column.
  • Only the columns needed for the comparison are shown. If two of six columns carry the message, the other four are taken off the slide and, if needed, put on a backup slide.
  • The important cell is made prominent with a restrained color or weight.
  • Text is aligned left and numbers right. In right-aligned numbers the units, tens, and hundreds digits line up, and magnitudes can be compared at a glance.
  • The source and date of the data are added in small type below the table when needed.
  • If the table is still dense, a chart or a few key numbers are considered.
Common mistake

Pasting a table from Excel into a slide with all its rows and columns. The text size shrinks and the back rows cannot read a single number. A table on a slide is reduced to the few rows and columns that carry the message.

A chart turns the relationship between numbers into a shape. Which chart to choose depends not on the type of the data but on the message to be conveyed (Table 3.3.14).

Example 3.3.10

A student's monthly budget is divided into three items: rent 4,500 TL, food 3,000 TL, and transport 1,500 TL. For the message “Half of the budget goes to rent”, which chart is suitable, and what are the shares in the chart?

  1. The message is a part-to-whole relationship and there are only three categories: a pie chart is suitable.
  2. Total: 4,500 + 3,000 + 1,500 = 9,000 TL.
  3. Shares: rent 4,500 ÷ 9,000 = 50%, food 3,000 ÷ 9,000 ≈ 33.3%, transport 1,500 ÷ 9,000 ≈ 16.7%.
  4. The angles of the pie slices are the shares' equivalents in 360°: rent 0.5 × 360 = 180°, food 120°, transport 60°. The rent slice covers exactly half of the circle; the message is seen at first glance.
  5. The chart title states the message: “Half of the budget goes to rent”.

If the number of items rises to seven or eight, the slices get thin and look alike. In that case a percent bar or a sorted bar chart is more legible.

Three rules hold for every chart type. The chart title states the main result plainly. Axes and units are visible. Three-dimensional effects are not used, because perspective makes the front bar or slice look bigger than it is and spoils the comparison.

Common mistake

Showing change over time with a pie chart. A pie chart shows the shares at a single moment; when the pies of two years are put side by side, the slices cannot be compared. Data with a time axis is given with a line chart.

3.3.7Transitions and animation

There are two separate kinds of motion in PowerPoint, and the two are often confused. One determines how the screen changes when moving from one slide to another; the other determines how objects within a slide appear or disappear. Used well, motion directs attention and opens up a process step by step. Used badly, it draws more attention than the content.

3.3.7.1The difference between a transition and an animation

Definition 3.3.17

A transition is the way the screen changes when moving from one slide to the next, and it applies to the slide as a whole; it is given from the Transitions tab. An animation is the appearance, emphasis, disappearance, or movement of a single object within a slide (a title, a bullet, a picture, a chart); it is given from the Animations tab.

The best way to see the difference is to follow what each click does in the show. Figure 3.3.21 and Table 3.3.18 give a two-slide show click by click. Slide 2 has been given a Push transition. Putting each bullet on a separate slide and giving a transition to make the bullets come one at a time. This increases the slide count needlessly, and the whole screen changes at every click. Making the bullets on the same slide come in order is the job of the animation.

3.3.7.3Designer, Morph, and Copilot

In current versions of PowerPoint there are three tools that make design and motion easier. The presence and names of these tools may vary with the PowerPoint version, the license, and the update level; tab and button names may look slightly different in the version in use.

  • Designer: Offers ready-made layout suggestions by looking at the content on the slide; it is on the Design tab. Because the suggestions are generated online, it requires an internet connection and, in the desktop version, a Microsoft 365 subscription.
  • Morph: A transition type on the Transitions tab. If the same object stands at a different position, size, or color on two consecutive slides, PowerPoint generates the motion in between by itself.
  • Copilot: Offers AI assistance such as creating draft slides and summarizing the deck. It works with a monthly AI credit on a personal Microsoft 365 subscription and with a separate Copilot license on an organizational account.
Definition 3.3.18

Morph is a transition that animates the motion in between automatically by finding the differences in position, size, rotation, and color of the objects common to two consecutive slides. The transition is given to the second slide.

The easy way to Morph is this: the first slide is duplicated, the objects on the copy are moved or resized to their new positions, and the Morph transition is given to the second slide. Because the slide was duplicated, the objects on the two slides are the “same” objects as far as PowerPoint is concerned. If the objects were drawn separately, PowerPoint may not be able to match them; in that case one fades out and the other fades in.

Common mistake

Accepting a layout from the Designer pane without thinking. A suggestion may look good aesthetically but spoil the visual hierarchy: for example, it may shrink the main number and enlarge a decorative picture. A suggestion is used if it brings out the slide's single message.

3.3.7.5Start, duration, and delay

Every animation has three timing settings: when it will start, how long it will last, and how long it will wait before starting. These settings are in the Timing group of the Animations tab or in the Animation Pane.

Definition 3.3.19

An animation's start can be of three kinds. On Click: starts when the presenter clicks or presses a key. With Previous: starts at the same time as the previous effect in the list. After Previous: starts by itself when the previous effect finishes. The duration determines how long the effect lasts, and the delay how long the effect waits after the start moment.

From these settings the start and end moments of an effect are calculated with a simple rule:

  • Reference point: for On Click the moment of the click, for With Previous the start of the previous effect, for After Previous the moment the previous effect(s) finish.
  • Start = reference point + delay.
  • End = start + duration.
Example 3.3.13

Let us calculate the times of the three effects in Figure 3.3.25 by the rule. The presenter clicks at t = 0.

  1. Title (On Click, delay 0, duration 1 s): the reference point is the click moment, that is, 0. Start 0 + 0 = 0, end 0 + 1 = 1 s.
  2. Picture (With Previous, delay 0.5, duration 1.5 s): the reference point is the Title's start, that is, 0. Start 0 + 0.5 = 0.5 s, end 0.5 + 1.5 = 2 s.
  3. Bullet 1 (After Previous, delay 0, duration 0.5 s): the previous effect that finishes last is the Picture (2 s). Start 2 s, end 2 + 0.5 = 2.5 s.

The three effects are completed in 2.5 seconds with a single click. The Title and the Picture play at the same time between 0.5 and 1 s.

Animations are always rehearsed in show view. Effects that look independent of one another on the editing screen may overlap unexpectedly in the show. For accessibility, too, fast-blinking and needlessly repeating motion is avoided. Such motion causes discomfort and distraction in some viewers.

Common mistake

Taking the delay to mean “waiting after the previous effect finishes”. The delay of an effect that starts With Previous is counted from the start of the previous effect, not from its end. The Picture's 0.5 s delay elapses 0.5 s after the Title starts, not after it ends.

In animations spread over several clicks, the numbers in the Animation Pane gain importance. The number next to an effect in the pane shows with which click that effect starts. Effects that start With Previous and After Previous do not get their own number; they join the group of the click they depend on.

3.3.8Delivering the talk

After the deck is prepared the real work begins: speaking in front of the audience. At this stage PowerPoint does more for the speaker than show slides. It gathers the notes, the timer, and the next slide on a screen only the speaker sees, lets the show be run from the keyboard, and measures the rehearsal.

3.3.8.1Speaker notes

Definition 3.3.20

Speaker notes are text written in the notes pane, attached to a slide, that is only for the speaker. They are not visible to the audience in a normal show; they are shown to the speaker in Presenter View and can be printed as Notes Pages.

Four kinds of information are written in the notes:

  • Content: Detail of the delivery that is not on the slide. The explanation behind the “38% increase” text on the slide stays in the note.
  • Reminder: An example to give, a question to ask the audience, or a source.
  • Time: The time set aside for this part, for example “The Method part should end here: minute 5”.
  • Transition sentence: The sentence that connects to the next topic. Whether the flow breaks between two slides depends largely on this sentence.

Notes are also the safeguard for shortening the text on the slide. A sentence taken off the slide is not lost; it stays in front of the speaker's eyes. A paper copy of the notes can also be made with File → Print → Notes Pages.

Common mistake

Reading the notes sentence by sentence. A note is a reminder, not a script. A note written in short keywords is read at a glance; a long paragraph locks the speaker to the screen and breaks eye contact with the audience.

3.3.8.2Presenter View

When a laptop is connected to a projector or a second screen, two screens exist. When the show starts, PowerPoint sends different things to these two screens: only the current slide to the projector, and Presenter View to the laptop screen.

Definition 3.3.21

Presenter View is the show view that appears only on the speaker's screen. It shows the current slide, the next slide, the speaker notes, the elapsed time, the clock, and the show tools (pen, laser pointer, zoom, all slides) together.

The tools of Presenter View keep the talk flowing (Figure 3.3.29):

  • The timer shows the time elapsed since the show began; it can be paused and reset. Beside it is the real clock too.
  • The next-slide preview lets the speaker prepare the transition sentence to the next topic.
  • The pen and laser pointer are used to point at a particular area on the slide or to draw a short mark on it.
  • The All Slides button jumps to a slide chosen from the thumbnails. The audience does not see this search screen; the current slide stays on the projector.

Presenter View opens by itself when the show starts with a second screen connected. Alt + F5 starts the show directly with Presenter View. This shortcut can also be used when rehearsing on a single screen.

Common mistake

Projecting Presenter View onto the projector when the screens get mixed up. In that case the audience sees the speaker's notes and the next slide. The positions of the two screens are swapped from the display settings at the top of Presenter View. This setting is tried out in the hall before the talk.

3.3.8.4Slide size, footer, audio, and video

Some of the technical settings of a talk must be made before the deck is prepared. The first of these is the slide size.

Definition 3.3.22

The slide size is the aspect ratio and physical measure of the slide; it is chosen with Design → Slide Size. Widescreen (16 : 9) is 33.867 × 19.05 cm, and standard (4 : 3) is 25.4 × 19.05 cm. Most of today's screens and projectors are 16 : 9; 4 : 3 is for old projectors.

Example 3.3.17

A deck prepared as 16 : 9 is later converted to 4 : 3, and PowerPoint shrinks the content to fit. To how many points does a 32-point title fall?

  1. The two sizes have the same height (19.05 cm); the width falls from 33.867 cm to 25.4 cm.
  2. For the content to fit the new width, the scale ratio is 25.4 ÷ 33.867 ≈ 0.75.
  3. All the content shrinks by this ratio: 32 × 0.75 = 24 points. Pictures and shapes also shrink by a quarter; empty bands are left above and below the slide.

The other option is to place the content in the new size without shrinking it; in that case the objects on the right and left may spill off the slide. In both options the layout is spoiled and every slide has to be gone over. That is why the slide size is chosen before starting the deck.

The other technical settings are these:

  • Footer and slide number: With Insert → Header & Footer, the slide number, date, and footer text are added; with Apply to All they spread to all slides. The slide number lets an audience member point at a slide in question time, saying “the chart on slide 12”.
  • Audio and video: Added with Insert → Video or Insert → Audio. From the Playback tab that opens when the inserted media is selected, the way it starts (on click or automatically), trimming, and volume are set.
  • Font embedding: File → Options → Save → Embed fonts in the file. The fonts used in the deck are put inside the file; when the deck is opened on a computer where that font is not installed, the text does not get garbled. The license of some fonts does not allow embedding.
  • Duplicating: Ctrl + D copies the selected slide or object right next to itself.
  • Pen and laser: During the show Ctrl + P opens the pen and Ctrl + L the laser pointer.
Common mistake

Trusting a font that looks fine on the computer where the deck was prepared. If the computer in the hall does not have that font, PowerPoint puts another font in its place; lines get longer and the text spills out of the boxes. The font is embedded or tried out on the hall computer before the talk.

3.3.8.5Hidden slides and custom shows

The same deck can be presented to different audiences at different lengths. For example, a project is presented in 20 minutes to the jury and in 5 minutes on the department's open day. Instead of keeping a separate file for each, two tools are used.

Definition 3.3.23

A hidden slide is a slide that stays in the file but is skipped in the normal show. A custom show is a separate, named show sequence chosen from the slides of the same deck; it is created and played with Slide Show → Custom Slide Show.

Example 3.3.18

Let us compare the three shows for the deck in Figure 3.3.30.

  1. Normal show with F5: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6. The hidden slide 4 is skipped; five slides are shown.
  2. The “Short” custom show: 1, 3, 6. A custom show uses its own list; slides 2 and 5, which are not in the list, are not shown either.
  3. If a question comes: During the show 4 is typed and Enter is pressed. A hidden slide can be shown this way.

A hidden slide keeps backup detail ready for question time. A custom show produces talks of different lengths from the same file; when a slide is corrected, it is corrected in all the custom shows.

Common mistake

Hiding sensitive content and sharing the file. A hidden slide stays in the file; anyone who opens the file sees it in the slide pane. Sensitive content is deleted before sharing.

3.3.8.6Rehearsing timings and a self-running show

A show does not always advance by the speaker's click. On an information screen or in a narrated recording the slides must advance by themselves at set times. The easiest way to determine these times is to rehearse the deck once at real speed.

Definition 3.3.24

Rehearse Timings is the Slide Show command that starts the show and measures and records the time spent on each slide. The recorded times are written into each slide's transition settings. A show of the kiosk type opens full screen, does not advance by clicking, and stays in a loop until Esc is pressed.

Table 3.3.25: Steps of the timing rehearsal and the self-running show.
StepActionResult
1Slide Show → Rehearse TimingsThe show starts; the toolbar in the corner counts the slide time and the total time
2Slide 1 is narrated at real speed and advanced8 s is recorded for Slide 1
3The other slides are narrated12, 10, 10 s; total 8 + 12 + 10 + 10 = 40 s
4The rehearsal ends and the timings are savedEach slide's own time is written into the “After” box on its Transitions tab
5Set Up Slide Show → kiosk typeLoop continuously is checked automatically; the show does not advance on click
Example 3.3.19

A four-slide deck runs as a kiosk show (times 8, 12, 10, 10 s). What slide is on the screen t = 46 s and t = 75 s after the show starts?

  1. One lap lasts 8 + 12 + 10 + 10 = 40 s. The slides' intervals within a lap: Slide 1 0–8, Slide 2 8–20, Slide 3 20–30, Slide 4 30–40 s.
  2. t = 46: whole laps are discarded and the remainder is found: 46 − 40 = 6. The sixth second falls in Slide 1's interval (0–8). The screen shows Slide 1 of the second lap.
  3. t = 75: 75 − 40 = 35. The 35th second falls in Slide 4's interval (30–40). The screen shows Slide 4; 35 − 30 = 5 s have passed in this slide.

The general rule: the remainder of t divided by the lap time is found and it is seen which slide's interval it falls in. For example in 10 minutes (600 s) the show makes 600 ÷ 40 = 15 laps.

Because clicking does nothing in a kiosk show, timing is essential. A kiosk show without timings gets stuck on the first slide. If the viewer is to navigate by touching the screen, only hyperlinks and action buttons work.

Common mistake

Forgetting the timings recorded during rehearsal in a live talk. The recorded times advance the slides without waiting for the speaker's click; if the speaker talks longer, the slide changes without waiting for them. Before a live talk, in Set Up Slide Show the option to advance manually is chosen.

3.3.8.7Presenting effectively

Even the best-prepared deck is strengthened or weakened by the speaker's manner. There are six principles for the talk itself:

  • The talk is rehearsed beforehand with the real screen and connections. The projector's resolution, its colors, and the connecting cable are tried before the day of the talk.
  • The slide is not read out; eye contact and communication with the audience are kept. If the speaker turns to the slide and reads, the audience reads too and stops listening to the speaker.
  • Every part is tied clearly to the main message. At the end of a part a linking sentence such as “this result tells us the following about our main question” is said.
  • The pointer and animation are used only to direct attention. Drawing circles on the slide with the laser pointer distracts.
  • The time limit is respected; room is left for questions and answers. If time is running over, the last slides are not rushed; a detail decided in advance is skipped.
  • Alternatives such as a PDF backup, a screen adapter, and an offline copy are kept ready. If the computer, the software, or the internet connection causes trouble, the talk is continued from the PDF.
Common mistake

Rushing through slides when time runs short and saying “there's no time for these”. The audience takes the skipped slides to be important and thinks they are missing something. If time is not enough, the speaker jumps to the closing slide before reaching the end and states the main message.

3.3.9Quality and sharing

When the deck is finished, the job is not considered done. Before sharing, it is checked that the deck can be read by everyone, carries no unwanted information, and works correctly on another computer.

3.3.9.1An accessible deck

Definition 3.3.25

An accessible deck is one from which audience members with different vision, hearing, or color perception, and those using screen readers, can take in the content fully. Alt text is a short description that tells a screen reader what a visual is about.

Six points are checked in an accessible deck:

  • Reading order: A screen reader reads objects in the order they were added; this order may differ from the visual order (Figure 3.3.32). The order is corrected in the Reading Order pane.
  • Alt text: Every meaningful visual is briefly described. Not “Picture” but “A column chart showing that enrollments rose 38% from 2023 to 2024”. Visuals that are purely decorative are marked “decorative”.
  • Title: Every slide has a unique and descriptive title. An audience member who uses a screen reader navigates among slides by looking at the titles.
  • Contrast: There is enough separation between text and background.
  • Color: Information is not given by color difference alone; a label or symbol accompanies the color.
  • Captions: Captions are used for video, audio, or live narration when appropriate.

Contrast is a measurable quantity. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) recommend that the ratio between text color and background color be at least 4.5 : 1 for normal-size text. The ratio is calculated from the relative luminance of the two colors: (luminance of the lighter color + 0.05) ÷ (luminance of the darker color + 0.05). Luminance is 1 for white and 0 for black.

Example 3.3.20

Two text colors are tried on a white background: black and a medium gray with RGB values (119, 119, 119). Are the contrast ratios sufficient?

  1. White on black: (1 + 0.05) ÷ (0 + 0.05) = 1.05 ÷ 0.05 = 21. The ratio is 21 : 1, the highest possible value.
  2. For the gray, each channel is first brought to the 0–1 range: 119 ÷ 255 ≈ 0.467. Because screen colors are not linear, this value is converted to a luminance of ((0.467 + 0.055) ÷ 1.055)2.4 ≈ 0.184. Since the three channels are equal, the gray's relative luminance is also 0.184.
  3. Ratio: (1 + 0.05) ÷ (0.184 + 0.05) ≈ 1.05 ÷ 0.234 ≈ 4.48. The ratio falls just below 4.5; according to the guideline this gray is insufficient for normal text.

A shade darker gray (118, 118, 118) gives about 4.54 : 1 and passes the limit. Because projectors show colors fainter than a screen, colors clearly darker than the borderline ones are chosen for a talk.

Common mistake

Showing the successful group in green and the unsuccessful group in red in a chart and making no other distinction. An audience member with red-green color blindness cannot tell the two groups apart, and a screen reader does not read color. A label (“successful”, “unsuccessful”) or a different pattern is added to the color.

3.3.9.4Recording and export

  • PDF: Gives a fixed-layout share or print; it contains no animations or transitions.
  • Handouts: Several slides are printed on one page; they can be distributed to the audience.
  • Packaging: When the deck is carried to another computer, linked media and fonts are also checked.
  • File size: The file is made smaller by compressing images and videos appropriately.
Common mistake

Adding a video as a link and copying the deck file alone to another computer. A linked video is not inside the deck file; it cannot be found on the new computer and does not play in the show. The video is embedded in the deck or packaged together with the deck's media.

3.3.10Summary

  • A deck is the file, a talk is the delivery. A slide supports the talk and does not replace it. The workspace consists of the ribbon, the slide pane, the slide area, the notes pane, and the status bar; contextual tabs appear while an object is selected.
  • Views do not change the content: Normal is for editing, Outline View for building text, Slide Sorter for seeing and ordering the flow, and Slide Master for the common design.
  • The plan starts with purpose, audience, and the main message. A content slide carries about 1–1.5 minutes; the question-and-answer time is written into the plan from the start.
  • In the outline every top-level line is a slide; Tab lowers the level and Shift + Tab raises it.
  • .pptx is editable, .ppsx is a direct show, .potx a template, .pdf fixed and animation-free, .mp4 a video. Save As creates a new file.
  • The layout determines the type and position of placeholders. A hidden slide stays in the file and is skipped in the show. Sections group slides and move them group by group; they are not visible in the show.
  • The theme chooses color and font, and the Slide Master carries their placement to all layouts and slides. Manual formatting locks only the property that was changed and takes precedence over the slide master; Reset removes this lock.
  • In visual hierarchy the most important element is the largest and the source the faintest; the title states the result. Slide text is kept short and detail is moved to the notes.
  • The ratio is kept when a picture is cropped. Pixels beyond what the screen can show only enlarge the file; Compress Pictures reduces to the target ppi and can delete cropped areas.
  • The chart is chosen by the message: columns for comparison, line for time, pie for part-to-whole with few categories, scatter for relationships. An embedded chart travels with the file, a linked chart is updated from Excel.
  • Align brings edges to a common line; Distribute leaves the two outer objects in place and equalizes the space: gap = (total span − sum of widths) ÷ (number of objects − 1).
  • A transition works between slides, an animation on objects within a slide. The animation types are entrance, emphasis, exit, and motion path.
  • Start = reference point + delay, end = start + duration. On Click takes the click, With Previous the start of the previous effect, and After Previous the end of the previous effects as the reference point. The numbers in the pane give the click count.
  • Speaker notes are not visible to the audience; Presenter View shows the speaker the notes, the next slide, and the timer. F5 starts from the beginning, Shift + F5 from the current slide; B/W blacken or whiten the screen.
  • The slide size is chosen at the start; in going from 16 : 9 to 4 : 3 the content shrinks by about 0.75. Fonts are embedded in the file.
  • Rehearse Timings records slide times; a kiosk show stays in a loop, and the slide at moment t is found from the remainder of dividing by the lap time.
  • Accessibility needs a title, reading order, alt text, enough contrast (at least 4.5 : 1 for normal text), and a cue other than color. The checkers find the problem; the user does the fixing; Inspect Document is run on a copy.
  • In printing, the page count is the slide count divided by slides per page, rounded up; in double-sided printing the sheet count is half of that, rounded up.

3.3.12Test Yourself

  1. In Outline View the following six lines are typed in order: Budget, Income, Scholarship, Rent, Expense, Conclusion. Then Tab is pressed on the “Scholarship” and “Rent” lines, and afterwards Shift + Tab on the “Rent” line. How many slides are there at each stage, and what are the bullets of the “Income” slide in the final state?

    Answer

    All six lines are at title level: 6 slides. When Tab is pressed on Scholarship and Rent, both become bullets of Income: Budget, Income, Expense, Conclusion, that is, 4 slides. Shift + Tab on Rent raises the level again and Rent becomes a separate slide: Budget, Income, Rent, Expense, Conclusion, that is, 5 slides. The only bullet of the Income slide is Scholarship.

  2. 6 minutes of a 20-minute session are set aside for questions. If a content slide carries 1–1.5 minutes, how many content slides should be planned? How many seconds per slide does a student get who prepares 35 slides for the same time?

    Answer

    14 minutes remain for the talk: 20 − 6. 14 ÷ 1.5 ≈ 9.3 and 14 ÷ 1 = 14, so about 10–14 content slides are suitable. With 35 slides each slide gets 14 × 60 ÷ 35 = 24 seconds; that is not enough time to explain a chart.

  3. On the slide master the title size is 40 pt and the color navy. Slide 2's title had earlier been given only a manual size of 28 pt. On the slide master the title size is now changed to 44 pt and the color to maroon. After Close Master View, how do the titles of Slide 1 and Slide 2 look? What happens if Reset is applied to Slide 2?

    Answer

    Slide 1: 44 pt, maroon; both rules come from the slide master. Slide 2: the size stays 28 pt because it was given by hand, and the color becomes maroon because it was not changed by hand. Reset deletes the manually given size; Slide 2's title also becomes 44 pt and maroon.

  4. A 6000 × 4000-pixel (3 : 2) photo will be shown full screen on a 16 : 9 slide on a 1920 × 1080-pixel projector. Counting 3 bytes per pixel, how many times the amount the screen can display is the photo's raw size? If it is cropped to 16 : 9 keeping the width, what percentage of the pixels remains?

    Answer

    The photo is 6000 × 4000 × 3 = 72,000,000 bytes (72 MB), and the screen image 1920 × 1080 × 3 = 6,220,800 bytes. The ratio is 72,000,000 ÷ 6,220,800 ≈ 11.6. On cropping, the height becomes 6000 × 9 ÷ 16 = 3375 pixels; 4000 − 3375 = 625 rows are discarded. The remaining ratio is 3375 ÷ 4000 ≈ 84.4%.

  5. On a slide extending horizontally from 0 to 960 there are three shapes: A (x = 40, width 120), B (x = 300, width 100), and C (x = 700, width 200). All three are selected and Distribute Horizontally is applied; then they are grouped and centered horizontally. What are the shapes' x values after each step?

    Answer

    The outer edges are 40 and 700 + 200 = 900; the span is 860. The sum of the widths is 120 + 100 + 200 = 420, the empty space 860 − 420 = 440, each gap 220. After Distribute: A = 40, B = 40 + 120 + 220 = 380, C = 700. The group is 860 wide; when centered its left edge becomes (960 − 860) ÷ 2 = 50, that is, the group shifts 10 units to the right: A = 50, B = 390, C = 710.

  6. In the Animation Pane there are these effects in order: Title (On Click, duration 1 s), Subtitle (With Previous, delay 0.5 s, duration 1 s), Bullet (After Previous, duration 0.5 s), Logo (After Previous, delay 1 s, duration 2 s), Chart (On Click, duration 1.5 s). The presenter clicks at t = 0 and t = 8. When does each effect play? Which numbers appear in the pane?

    Answer

    Title 0–1 s. Subtitle 0.5 s after the Title's start: 0.5–1.5 s. Bullet starts at the latest end of the previous effects (1.5): 1.5–2 s. Logo, 1 s after the end of Bullet: 3–5 s. Chart with the second click: 8–9.5 s. In the pane only 1 appears next to Title and 2 next to Chart; two clicks are enough for the slide's animations.

  7. Slide 1 has no animation. Slide 2 has been given a Fade transition. Bullet 1 and Bullet 3 start On Click and Bullet 2 With Previous; Bullet 3 has also been given an emphasis that starts After Previous. In a show starting from Slide 1, how many clicks are needed to see Slide 3?

    Answer

    Four clicks. Click 1 moves to Slide 2 with Fade. Click 2 brings Bullet 1 and Bullet 2 together. Click 3 brings Bullet 3; the emphasis then plays by itself. Click 4 moves to Slide 3.

  8. In a three-slide kiosk show the timings are 15, 20, and 25 s. What slide is on the screen 100 s and 200 s after the show starts, and how many seconds have passed in that slide?

    Answer

    The lap time is 15 + 20 + 25 = 60 s; the intervals are Slide 1 0–15, Slide 2 15–35, Slide 3 35–60. t = 100: 100 − 60 = 40, Slide 3's interval; 40 − 35 = 5 s have passed in the slide. t = 200: 200 − 3 × 60 = 20, Slide 2's interval; 20 − 15 = 5 s have passed in the slide.

  9. A 45-slide deck will be printed double-sided as a 6-slide handout. How many pages and how many sheets are needed? What is the page count in the 4-slide layout?

    Answer

    45 ÷ 6 = 7.5 → 8 pages; in double-sided printing 8 ÷ 2 = 4 sheets. In the 4-slide layout 45 ÷ 4 = 11.25 → 12 pages.

  10. In an eight-slide deck slide 7 is hidden. A custom show named “Jury” consists of slides 1, 2, 5, and 8. How many slides are shown with F5? Which slides does the “Jury” show present? Is it safe to share the file because of the hidden slide?

    Answer

    With F5 seven slides are shown in the order 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8; slide 7 is skipped. The “Jury” show presents only 1, 2, 5, and 8. Because the hidden slide stays in the file, anyone who receives the file can see it; if there is sensitive content, it is deleted before sharing.
