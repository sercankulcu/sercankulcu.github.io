Computer Fundamentals and Introduction to Programming · People

Important People in Computing and Programming

Short profiles of the 74 mathematicians, engineers and programmers behind the ideas used in the course, collected on 58 cards. The list runs from Euclid's algorithm and Boolean logic to Turing machines, the transistor, the Internet, Unix, spreadsheets and Java. Each card links to the chapters of the course in which the person's work appears; the details open with the button at the bottom of the card.

EU

Euclid of Alexandria

c. 300 BCEGreek · Alexandria

Euclid's algorithm (GCD) · Elements, Book VII

In his Elements he described how to find the greatest common divisor (GCD) of two numbers. The method is one of the oldest known algorithms and is still taught today.

greatest common divisorremaindertermination
ChaptersChapter 9 · MethodsChapter 12 · Recursion

Why it matters: The rule gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b) stops when b = 0. In the course it is the model example for both the loop version and the recursive version of a method.

Euclid's original method uses repeated subtraction; the remainder form is its modern equivalent. The numbers shrink at every step, which is the standard argument that the algorithm always stops.

ER

Eratosthenes of Cyrene

c. 276 – c. 194 BCEGreek · Library of Alexandria

Sieve of Eratosthenes · prime numbers

He was the chief librarian of the Library of Alexandria. The method of finding primes by repeatedly marking off the multiples of each prime is named after him.

prime numberssieve of Eratosthenesboolean array
ChaptersChapter 8 · LoopsChapter 10 · Arrays

Why it matters: The sieve keeps track of whether each number has been crossed out in a boolean array. Once a prime is found, all of its multiples are marked in a single pass. The outer loop can stop when i · i > n, because every composite number up to n has a prime divisor no larger than √n. This is much faster than testing each number by trial division.

He calculated the circumference of the Earth by comparing the noon shadows at Alexandria and Syene (modern Aswan) at the summer solstice. Some historians think Eratosthenes did not invent the method and only gave it the name "sieve".

AK

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi

c. 780 – c. 850Persian · House of Wisdom, Baghdad

Origin of the words "algorithm" and "algebra"

He wrote influential books on arithmetic with Hindu-Arabic numerals and on solving equations. The Latin form of his name, Algoritmi, became the word "algorithm".

step-by-step proceduresdecimal numeralsalgebra
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational ThinkingChapter 4.1 · Algorithms

Why it matters: The central idea of the course carries his name: a finite, precise sequence of steps that solves a problem.

The word algebra comes from al-jabr in the title of his book on equations (c. 820). Latin translations of his arithmetic book spread the positional decimal notation to medieval Europe.

LF

Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci)

c. 1170 – c. 1250Italian · Pisa

Liber Abaci · 1202

His Liber Abaci (1202) popularised Hindu-Arabic numerals in Europe. The rabbit problem in the book produces the sequence 0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, …

Fibonacci sequencerecurrence relationiteration and recursion
ChaptersChapter 4.1 · AlgorithmsChapter 8 · LoopsChapter 12 · Recursion

Why it matters: F(n) = F(n−1) + F(n−2) is the classic loop exercise. The sequence also shows why naive recursion repeats the same work again and again.

The sequence was already known in Indian mathematics. The name "Fibonacci sequence" was popularised by Édouard Lucas in the 19th century. The terms grow so fast that even Java's long type overflows after the 92nd term.

GL

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

1646–1716German · polymath, Hanover

Binary arithmetic · 1703 · stepped reckoner

He published binary arithmetic, which uses only 0 and 1, in 1703. He also built a mechanical calculator called the stepped reckoner.

binary numbersmechanical calculationsymbolic reasoning
ChaptersChapter 1.1 · Basic ConceptsChapter 1.3 · Computers

Why it matters: Every number, character and instruction in a computer is stored in the binary system Leibniz described.

His essay "Explication de l'arithmétique binaire" appeared in the yearbook of the Paris Academy of Sciences. Leibniz also dreamed of a formal language in which reasoning could be carried out by calculation.

JJ

Joseph Marie Jacquard

1752–1834French · weaver and inventor, Lyon

Punched-card loom · 1804

He built a loom mechanism that wove patterns automatically (first demonstrated around 1804). A chain of punched cards controlled the mechanism.

punched cardsstored instructionsautomation
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational Thinking

Why it matters: When the cards change, the machine stays the same and the pattern changes. This is an early form of keeping the program separate from the hardware.

Babbage planned to feed the Analytical Engine with Jacquard-style cards. Punched cards went on to carry data and programs until the 1970s.

CG

Carl Friedrich Gauss

1777–1855German · University of Göttingen

The sum 1 + 2 + … + n = n(n + 1)/2

According to a well-known (perhaps embellished) story, as a schoolboy he instantly found the sum of the numbers from 1 to 100. He did it by pairing terms from the beginning and the end.

closed-form formulaefficiencyloop vs. formula
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational Thinking

Why it matters: In the course, a summing loop (n additions) is compared with Gauss's formula (one multiplication and one division). This shows that algorithms can have very different costs.

Gauss is one of the most important mathematicians in history. He worked on number theory, astronomy, geodesy and magnetism. Gaussian elimination and the Gaussian (normal) distribution carry his name.

CB

Charles Babbage

1791–1871English · University of Cambridge (Lucasian Professor)

Difference Engine · Analytical Engine (1837)

He designed the Difference Engine for computing tables. In 1837 he then designed the Analytical Engine, a general-purpose mechanical computer that was never completed.

store and millpunched-card inputgeneral-purpose machine
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational Thinking

Why it matters: The Analytical Engine separated memory (the "store") from processing (the "mill"). Today's computers use the same arrangement.

The design included conditional branching and loops controlled by punched cards. In 1991 the Science Museum in London completed a working Difference Engine No. 2 from his drawings.

AM

Augustus De Morgan

1806–1871English · University College London

De Morgan's laws · Formal Logic, 1847

He stated as rules that the negation of an AND expression equals the OR of the negations of its terms, and the reverse. The laws appeared in a paper of 1846 and were published again in his book Formal Logic in 1847.

De Morgan's lawslogical operatorsnegating a condition
ChaptersChapter 7 · Conditionals

Why it matters: In Java, !(a && b) always has the same value as !a || !b, and !(a || b) the same as !a && !b. When you negate a condition, the ! is distributed into the parentheses and && and || swap places: the condition !(s >= 15 && s <= 30) becomes s < 15 || s > 30.

He was born in Madurai, India, and was a professor of mathematics at University College London for many years. In 1840–1842 he taught Ada Lovelace mathematics by correspondence. He became the first president of the London Mathematical Society, founded in 1865.

GB

George Boole

1815–1864English · Queen's College Cork, Ireland

Boolean algebra · 1847, 1854

He treated logic as an algebra of true/false values. He developed the idea in his books The Mathematical Analysis of Logic (1847) and The Laws of Thought (1854).

AND / OR / NOTtruth tablesboolean type
ChaptersChapter 1.3 · ComputersChapter 6 · Variables, Data Types and OperatorsChapter 7 · Conditionals

Why it matters: The logic gates in a processor and the conditions in if statements and loops are all Boolean expressions. Java's boolean type carries his name.

Boole was largely self-taught and became the first professor of mathematics at Queen's College Cork. Claude Shannon later showed that Boolean algebra describes switching circuits.

AL

Ada Lovelace

1815–1852English · mathematician, London

Notes on the Analytical Engine · 1843

She translated an article about Babbage's Analytical Engine and in 1843 added long notes to it. The notes included a step-by-step method for calculating Bernoulli numbers.

first algorithm published for a machineNote Gsymbol manipulation
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational ThinkingChapter 4.3 · Introduction to Programming

Why it matters: Note G is often regarded as the first computer program. Lovelace realised that such a machine could process not only numbers but also symbols.

She was the daughter of the poet Lord Byron. The Ada programming language (1980) carries her name. Ada Lovelace Day celebrates women in science and technology.

ÉL

Édouard Lucas

1842–1891French · mathematician, Paris

Tower of Hanoi puzzle · 1883

He invented the Tower of Hanoi puzzle and put it on the market in 1883 under the pseudonym "N. Claus de Siam".

Tower of Hanoi2ⁿ − 1 movesrecursive thinking
ChaptersChapter 12 · Recursion

Why it matters: Moving n disks reduces to moving n − 1 disks twice. It is the textbook example of a problem whose recursive solution is far simpler than its loop solution.

Lucas also studied the Fibonacci numbers and gave them that name. The Lucas numbers and Lucas primality tests are also named after him.

HH

Herman Hollerith

1860–1929American · Tabulating Machine Company

Punched-card tabulator · 1890 US census

He built electromechanical machines that counted data recorded on punched cards. These machines processed the data of the 1890 US census.

data processingpunched cardstabulating machines
ChaptersChapter 1.1 · Basic Concepts

Why it matters: Tabulating machines turned data processing into an industry: input, processing and output were now done by machines.

The Tabulating Machine Company he founded (1896) merged into the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company in 1911. That company was renamed International Business Machines (IBM) in 1924.

AC

Alonzo Church

1903–1995American · Princeton University, UCLA

Lambda calculus · Church–Turing thesis

He developed the lambda calculus. In 1936 he used it to show that the decision problem of logic (the Entscheidungsproblem) has no general solution.

lambda calculuscomputabilityundecidability
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational Thinking

Why it matters: According to the Church–Turing thesis, anything that can be computed by an effective method can also be computed by a Turing machine. The lambda calculus has the same computing power as the Turing machine.

Church reached the result shortly before Turing and by a different route. He supervised Turing's doctorate at Princeton (1938). The lambda calculus is the foundation of functional programming and of lambda expressions in today's languages.

JvN

John von Neumann

1903–1957Hungarian-born American · Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton

Stored-program architecture · 1945 · merge sort

He described the stored-program computer, in which program and data sit in the same memory. The description is in his 1945 First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC.

von Neumann architecturefetch–decode–executemerge sort
ChaptersChapter 1.3 · ComputersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational ThinkingChapter 4.1 · AlgorithmsChapter 4.2 · FlowchartsChapter 12 · Recursion

Why it matters: The processor–memory arrangement discussed in the hardware chapters, and the "von Neumann bottleneck", carry his name.

Merge sort (1945) is attributed to him. With Herman Goldstine he introduced flowcharts for planning programs (1947). He also made major contributions to game theory, quantum mechanics and numerical analysis.

TF

Tommy Flowers

1905–1998English · Post Office Research Station, Dollis Hill

Colossus · 1944

He designed and built Colossus, an electronic machine that ran on vacuum tubes. From early 1944, Colossus helped break German Lorenz ciphers at Bletchley Park.

vacuum tubeselectronic computationcodebreaking
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational Thinking

Why it matters: Colossus showed that large numbers of tubes could work reliably in digital computation. It was programmed with switches and plugs but did not store its program in memory.

Colossus Mark 1 had about 1,600 tubes and Mark 2 about 2,400. The machines were kept secret for decades, so Flowers's work was little known until the 1970s.

GH

Grace Hopper

1906–1992American · US Navy (rear admiral), Remington Rand

A-0 compiler · FLOW-MATIC · COBOL

In 1952 she developed the A-0 system, one of the first compilers. She also created the English-like language FLOW-MATIC, which strongly influenced COBOL (1959).

compilersCOBOLEnglish-like syntax
ChaptersChapter 4.3 · Introduction to Programming

Why it matters: She argued that programs should be written in languages people can read and translated automatically. This idea lies behind every compiler in the course.

During the Second World War she programmed the Harvard Mark I. The moth her team taped into the logbook in 1947 is a famous story about computer "bugs"; the word had in fact been used earlier. In 2016 she was posthumously awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

ME

John Mauchly & J. Presper Eckert

1907–1980 · 1919–1995American · Moore School, University of Pennsylvania

ENIAC · 1945–46 · UNIVAC I

They led the design of ENIAC, a general-purpose electronic computer. ENIAC was completed in 1945 and unveiled in February 1946.

ENIACvacuum tubesUNIVAC
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational Thinking

Why it matters: ENIAC showed that large-scale electronic computation was possible. The difficulty of reprogramming it with cables led directly to the stored-program design.

ENIAC had about 18,000 vacuum tubes. The machine was programmed by setting switches and cables. The company they founded built the UNIVAC I; the first machine was delivered to the US Census Bureau in 1951.

BBS

John Bardeen, Walter Brattain & William Shockley

1908–1991 · 1902–1987 · 1910–1989American · Bell Labs

Transistor · 1947

Bardeen and Brattain built the first point-contact transistor at Bell Labs in December 1947. Shortly afterwards Shockley designed the junction transistor.

Awards: 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics (all three together); Bardeen won a second Nobel Prize in Physics in 1972

transistorsemiconductorsswitches
ChaptersChapter 1.1 · Basic ConceptsChapter 1.3 · Computers

Why it matters: A transistor is a tiny electronic switch. Logic gates and processors are built from millions, even billions, of transistors.

Transistors replaced vacuum tubes because they were smaller, faster, cooler and more reliable. The company Shockley later founded contributed to the birth of Silicon Valley. Some engineers who left that company founded Fairchild Semiconductor.

KZ

Konrad Zuse

1910–1995German · civil engineer, Berlin

Z3 · 1941 · Plankalkül

In 1941 he completed the Z3, a programmable, fully automatic digital computer built from relays. He also designed Plankalkül, one of the first high-level programming languages.

binary floating pointrelaysPlankalkül
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational ThinkingChapter 4.3 · Introduction to Programming

Why it matters: The Z3 used binary arithmetic and floating-point numbers. These ideas are central to data representation and to Java's double type.

The Z3 read its program from punched film and was destroyed in an air raid in 1943. Plankalkül was designed in 1942–1945 but was published only in 1972.

AT

Alan Turing

1912–1954English · University of Cambridge, Bletchley Park, University of Manchester

Turing machine · 1936

He defined the Turing machine in his paper "On Computable Numbers" (1936). He proved that some problems cannot be solved by any algorithm.

Turing machineuniversal machinecomputability
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational Thinking

Why it matters: The universal Turing machine reads the rules of another machine from its own tape. This is the theoretical form of the stored-program idea.

In the Second World War he led the codebreaking work on the Enigma cipher at Bletchley Park. He designed the ACE computer (1945) and proposed the Turing test (1950). The ACM Turing Award, first given in 1966, carries his name.

HG

Herman Goldstine

1913–2004American · ENIAC project, Institute for Advanced Study

Flow diagrams · 1947

With von Neumann he wrote the reports "Planning and Coding of Problems for an Electronic Computing Instrument" (1947–48). These reports introduced flowcharts for program design.

flowchartsprogram planningIAS machine
ChaptersChapter 4.3 · Introduction to ProgrammingChapter 4.2 · Flowcharts

Why it matters: The boxes and arrows of the flowcharts used in the algorithms chapters come from these reports.

As an army officer he was the liaison to the ENIAC project, and in 1944 he brought von Neumann into it. He later directed the IAS computer project. He also wrote a history of computing, The Computer from Pascal to von Neumann (1972).

CS

Claude Shannon

1916–2001American · MIT, Bell Labs

Switching circuits · 1937 · information theory · 1948

In his 1937 master's thesis he showed that Boolean algebra can describe and simplify relay switching circuits. In 1948 he founded information theory.

logic circuitsbitinformation theory
ChaptersChapter 1.1 · Basic ConceptsChapter 1.3 · Computers

Why it matters: His thesis connects Boolean logic to the logic gates in processors. His 1948 paper made the bit the unit of information.

The word "bit" (binary digit) appears in the 1948 paper; Shannon credits it there to John Tukey. Data compression and reliable communication over networks rest on information theory.

BB

Bob Bemer

1920–2004American · IBM and other companies

ASCII · escape sequence · backslash

He played a leading role in creating ASCII, first published in 1963. The escape sequence and the backslash (\) character are also among his contributions.

ASCIIcharacter codesescape sequences
ChaptersChapter 1.1 · Basic ConceptsChapter 1.3 · Computers

Why it matters: ASCII's 7-bit codes (A = 65) are the first 128 code points of Unicode. The character arithmetic used in the course is based on these codes.

ASCII was a committee effort, but Bemer is often called its father. He proposed the backslash in 1961. He was also one of the first to draw attention to the year 2000 problem.

BJ

Corrado Böhm & Giuseppe Jacopini

1923–2017 (Böhm)Italian · Rome (IAC-CNR); Jacopini was Böhm's student

Structured program theorem · 1966

They proved that any program written as a flowchart can be rewritten using only sequence, selection and repetition (Communications of the ACM, 1966).

sequenceselectionrepetition
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational ThinkingChapter 4.2 · FlowchartsChapter 7 · ConditionalsChapter 8 · Loops

Why it matters: The three control structures (sequence, selection and loops) are enough for every algorithm; goto-style jumps are not needed.

This result formed the theoretical basis of structured programming, which Dijkstra championed from 1968 on. Böhm later also did important work on the lambda calculus.

KN

Jack Kilby & Robert Noyce

1923–2005 · 1927–1990American · Texas Instruments (Kilby); Fairchild Semiconductor, Intel (Noyce)

Integrated circuit · 1958–59

They invented the integrated circuit independently of each other. Kilby demonstrated the first example at Texas Instruments in 1958. In 1959 Noyce developed the practical planar silicon form at Fairchild.

Awards: Kilby: 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics (Noyce had died in 1990)

integrated circuitchipsminiaturisation
ChaptersChapter 1.2 · Computer HardwareChapter 1.3 · Computers

Why it matters: Fitting many transistors on a single chip made processors, memory and today's computers possible.

Noyce co-founded Intel in 1968 with Gordon Moore. Kilby's team also developed the first handheld electronic calculator (1967).

JB

John Backus

1924–2007American · IBM

FORTRAN · 1957 · Backus–Naur Form

He led the team at IBM that developed FORTRAN (1957). With its optimising compiler, FORTRAN was the first widely used high-level language.

Awards: 1977 Turing Award

FORTRANcompilersBNF grammar
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational ThinkingChapter 4.3 · Introduction to Programming

Why it matters: FORTRAN showed that compiled code can run at nearly the speed of hand-written machine code. This made high-level languages practical.

He proposed a notation for writing the syntax of languages. Peter Naur used it in the ALGOL 60 report. The notation, known as Backus–Naur Form (BNF), is still used today to define language grammars.

DE

Douglas Engelbart

1925–2013American · Stanford Research Institute (SRI)

Computer mouse · "Mother of All Demos", 1968

He invented the computer mouse; he built the first one with Bill English in the mid-1960s. In 1968 he demonstrated windows, hypertext and real-time collaborative document editing live.

Awards: 1997 Turing Award

mousehypertextinteractive computing
ChaptersChapter 1.2 · Computer HardwareChapter 2.1 · Software and Operating SystemsChapter 2.2 · The Windows Operating System

Why it matters: The pointing and window ideas of graphical desktops such as Windows are based on his work.

His demonstration in San Francisco in December 1968 later became known as the "Mother of All Demos". His goal was to use computers to augment human intellect.

BD

Paul Baran & Donald Davies

1926–2011 · 1924–2000Polish-born American · RAND (Baran); Welsh · National Physical Laboratory (Davies)

Packet switching · 1960s

They developed packet switching independently of each other. In this method a message is split into small blocks. The blocks travel through the network separately and are reassembled at the destination.

packet switchingdistributed networksrouting
ChaptersChapter 1.4 · The Internet

Why it matters: Every message on the Internet is carried as independent packets.

Baran described distributed networks in a series of RAND reports in 1964. Davies coined the term "packet" and built a packet-switched network at the NPL in Britain. The work of both influenced the design of ARPANET (1969).

KK

John Kemeny & Thomas Kurtz

1926–1992 · 1928–2024Hungarian-born American; American · Dartmouth College

BASIC · 1964

They developed the BASIC language and the Dartmouth Time-Sharing System. The first BASIC program ran on this system on 1 May 1964.

BASICtime sharingprogramming for everyone
ChaptersChapter 4.3 · Introduction to Programming

Why it matters: BASIC was designed so that students outside the sciences could also learn programming. It became the first language of many home-computer users.

Microsoft's first product was a BASIC interpreter (1975). Kemeny was president of Dartmouth from 1970 to 1981. Together with Kurtz he later founded the company True BASIC.

JM

John McCarthy

1927–2011American · MIT, Stanford University

Lisp · 1958 · "artificial intelligence"

He designed Lisp, a language built on recursive functions (1958). He coined the term "artificial intelligence" in the 1955 Dartmouth proposal.

Awards: 1971 Turing Award

recursionconditional expressionsLisp
ChaptersChapter 4.3 · Introduction to ProgrammingChapter 7 · ConditionalsChapter 12 · Recursion

Why it matters: Lisp made recursion an ordinary programming tool. The language also introduced the conditional expression, the ancestor of the if–else construct.

He described Lisp in the paper "Recursive Functions of Symbolic Expressions and Their Computation by Machine" (1960). He also invented garbage collection and proposed time sharing.

SP

Seymour Papert

1928–2016South African-born · MIT

Logo · Mindstorms (1980)

He co-developed the Logo language for children (1967). He used the phrase "computational thinking" in his book Mindstorms (1980).

Logolearning by programmingcomputational thinking
ChaptersChapter 1.5 · Computation and Computational Thinking

Why it matters: He argued that programming is a way of thinking about problems. Jeannette Wing later developed this idea.

In Logo's turtle graphics the student gives commands step by step and sees the result immediately. The LEGO Mindstorms robot kits take their name from his book.

GM

Gordon Moore

1929–2023American · Fairchild Semiconductor, Intel

Moore's law · 1965

In 1965 he observed that the number of components on a chip was doubling every year. In 1975 he revised the rate to about once every two years.

Moore's lawtransistor countsemiconductors
ChaptersChapter 1.1 · Basic ConceptsChapter 1.2 · Computer Hardware

Why it matters: Moore's law explains the steady growth in processor power and memory capacity discussed in the hardware presentation.

He co-founded Intel in 1968 with Robert Noyce. The trend lasted for decades. As transistors approached physical limits, the growth slowed.

ED

Edsger W. Dijkstra

1930–2002Dutch · Mathematisch Centrum, Eindhoven University of Technology, University of Texas at Austin

Structured programming · "Go To Statement Considered Harmful", 1968

In 1968 he argued that unrestricted goto jumps make programs incomprehensible. This article started the structured programming movement.

Awards: 1972 Turing Award

structured programmingshortest pathssemaphores
ChaptersChapter 2.1 · Software and Operating SystemsChapter 4.3 · Introduction to ProgrammingChapter 4.2 · FlowchartsChapter 8 · Loops

Why it matters: Writing programs with a clear sequence, selection and loops, as in Java, follows his ideas.

He published his shortest-path algorithm in 1959. He introduced semaphores to coordinate processes. The "dining philosophers" problem illustrates deadlock, one of the topics of the operating systems presentation.

WK

William Kahan

b. 1933Canadian · University of California, Berkeley

IEEE 754 floating point · 1985

He was the chief architect of the IEEE 754 standard (1985) for floating-point arithmetic.

Awards: 1989 Turing Award

floating pointroundingdouble
ChaptersChapter 1.3 · ComputersChapter 6 · Variables, Data Types and Operators

Why it matters: Java's float and double types follow IEEE 754. This is why 0.1 + 0.2 prints 0.30000000000000004.

The standard defines formats made of sign, exponent and fraction bits, the rounding rules, and special values such as infinity and NaN. As a result, the same program gives the same result on different machines.

TH

Tony Hoare

1934–2026English · Elliott Brothers, Queen's University Belfast, University of Oxford, Microsoft Research

Quicksort · 1959–61

He devised quicksort around 1959–60 and published it in 1961. The algorithm partitions the array into two parts around a pivot and then sorts the parts recursively.

Awards: 1980 Turing Award

quicksortdivide and conquerHoare logic
ChaptersChapter 4.1 · AlgorithmsChapter 12 · Recursion

Why it matters: Like merge sort, quicksort is a divide-and-conquer algorithm. Its average running time is O(n log n); on large arrays it is much faster than bubble sort.

He developed Hoare logic (1969) for proving programs correct and CSP for concurrency. He added the null reference to ALGOL W (1965), which he later called his "billion-dollar mistake". He died on 5 March 2026.

NW

Niklaus Wirth

1934–2024Swiss · ETH Zurich

Pascal · 1970 · Algorithms + Data Structures = Programs

He designed Pascal (1970) to teach structured programming. He then developed the languages Modula-2 and Oberon.

Awards: 1984 Turing Award

Pascalstructured programmingstepwise refinement
ChaptersChapter 4.3 · Introduction to Programming

Why it matters: Pascal made structured, strongly typed programming the standard of introductory courses for twenty years.

The title of his book Algorithms + Data Structures = Programs (1976) sums up one of the main ideas of this course. He also advocated stepwise refinement: a problem is solved by dividing it into smaller steps.

EC

Edward G. Coffman Jr.

b. 1934American · Pennsylvania State University, Bell Laboratories, Columbia University

The four conditions for deadlock · 1971

In the paper "System Deadlocks" (Computing Surveys, 1971), written with M. J. Elphick and Arie Shoshani, he defined the four conditions that must hold at the same time for a deadlock to occur. These are known today as the Coffman conditions.

deadlockCoffman conditionsresource management
ChaptersChapter 2.1 · Software and Operating Systems

Why it matters: A deadlock cannot occur unless mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption and circular wait all hold together; breaking any one is enough. A process that holds the printer and waits for the file, and another that holds the file and waits for the printer, is the simplest example of circular wait. If all processes request resources in the same order, this cycle cannot form.

In 1973 he wrote the book Operating Systems Theory with Peter J. Denning. He worked on scheduling, bin packing and queueing theory, and is among the founders of the ACM Symposium on Operating Systems Principles.

HFS

Ted Hoff, Federico Faggin & Masatoshi Shima

b. 1937 · b. 1941 · b. 1943American; Italian-born American; Japanese · Intel and Busicom

Intel 4004 microprocessor · 1971

Together with Stanley Mazor, they developed the Intel 4004 (1971), the first commercial single-chip microprocessor.

microprocessorCPU on a chip4-bit
ChaptersChapter 1.2 · Computer HardwareChapter 1.3 · Computers

Why it matters: Fitting the entire central processing unit (CPU) on one chip opened the way to personal computers, phones and today's processors.

Hoff proposed the architecture; Shima of Busicom worked on the logic design; Faggin led the silicon design. The 4004 had about 2,300 transistors. Faggin and Shima later designed the Zilog Z80.

DK

Donald Knuth

b. 1938American · Stanford University

The Art of Computer Programming (since 1968)

He turned the analysis of algorithms into a systematic field in the series The Art of Computer Programming. The first volume appeared in 1968.

Awards: 1974 Turing Award

algorithm analysisbig Osorting and searching
ChaptersChapter 4.3 · Introduction to ProgrammingChapter 4.1 · Algorithms

Why it matters: Comparing linear and binary search, or bubble sort and merge sort, by counting steps is the method he made standard.

Volume 3 of the series treats sorting and searching in detail. He also created the TeX typesetting system (1978) and advocated "literate programming".

AK

Alan Kay

b. 1940American · Xerox PARC

Smalltalk · overlapping windows · Dynabook

In the 1970s at Xerox PARC he led the team that developed the Smalltalk language and the graphical interface with overlapping windows.

Awards: 2003 Turing Award

graphical user interfaceobject-oriented programmingSmalltalk
ChaptersChapter 2.1 · Software and Operating SystemsChapter 2.2 · The Windows Operating System

Why it matters: The desktop with windows, icons and menus, later popularised by Apple and Microsoft, grew out of this work.

The term "object-oriented" is attributed to him for the approach used in Smalltalk; Smalltalk also influenced Java. The Dynabook concept (1972) envisioned a portable, tablet-like computer for children.

RT

Dennis Ritchie & Ken Thompson

1941–2011 · b. 1943American · Bell Labs

Unix · 1969 · C language · 1972

They developed the Unix operating system at Bell Labs starting in 1969. Ritchie developed the C language around 1972, and Unix was rewritten in it.

Awards: 1983 Turing Award (shared)

UnixC languageportability
ChaptersChapter 1.3 · ComputersChapter 2.1 · Software and Operating SystemsChapter 4.3 · Introduction to Programming

Why it matters: Linux, macOS and Android follow Unix ideas. Java's syntax (curly braces, if, for, while) also comes from C.

Thompson also created the B language and, with Rob Pike, designed the UTF-8 encoding (1992). At Google he became one of the designers of the Go language (2009).

RT

Ray Tomlinson

1941–2016American · BBN (Bolt Beranek and Newman)

Network email · 1971 · @ sign

In 1971 he sent the first email between computers on ARPANET. He chose the @ sign to separate the user name from the computer name.

email@ signARPANET
