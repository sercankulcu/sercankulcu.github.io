Suppose the universe contains no written instruction:

**This is what your life is for.**

What follows?

One possibility is despair.

Another is freedom.

If purpose is not discovered as a cosmic fact, perhaps it can be **created**.

## Purpose vs Meaning

Purpose concerns:

what a life is directed toward.

Meaning concerns:

why that life matters or feels significant.

They overlap.

They are not identical.

## Given Purpose

A given purpose comes from an external source:

- deity,
- society,
- family,
- institution.

This can provide structure.

But it can also conflict with personal agency.

## Created Purpose

A created purpose emerges from:

- commitments,
- projects,
- relationships.

It is not discovered like a planet.

It is built.

## Does Created Mean Arbitrary?

No.

A bridge is created by humans.

That does not make it imaginary.

Created structures can be:

- real,
- binding,
- valuable.

Purpose can be constructed without being meaningless.

## Existentialism

Existentialist thinkers emphasize that humans are not born with a fully specified essence.

We must choose what to become.

This gives freedom a heavy burden.

## Sartre

Jean-Paul Sartre famously argued that:

existence precedes essence.

Humans first exist.

Then define themselves through action.

## Radical Freedom

Sartre sometimes described human freedom in radical terms.

Even refusing to choose is itself a choice.

This captures responsibility for self-definition.

## Limits on Freedom

But real humans are not infinitely free.

We inherit:

- bodies,
- history,
- social conditions.

Existential freedom operates within constraints.

## Facticity

Sartre used **facticity** for the given conditions of existence.

Examples:

- birthplace,
- past,
- body.

Freedom does not erase facticity.

It responds to it.

## Transcendence

Humans can project beyond current conditions.

We imagine:

what might be.

Purpose links facticity to possibility.

## Bad Faith

Sartre's **bad faith** involves denying one's freedom or pretending one is merely a fixed role.

Example:

"I had no choice; this is simply what I am."

The concept challenges self-deception.

## But Constraints Are Real

Bad faith should not be used to blame people for structural limits.

Freedom is situated.

Agency varies with real opportunity.

## Camus

Albert Camus focused on the **absurd**:

humans seek meaning,

while the universe offers no clear answer.

His response was not surrender.

It was revolt.

## The Myth of Sisyphus

Sisyphus endlessly pushes a stone uphill.

Camus transforms the image.

Meaning can arise through conscious engagement even without cosmic resolution.

## Absurdity

The absurd is not:

life is worthless.

It is the tension between:

our demand for ultimate meaning

and:

a silent universe.

## Nietzsche

Nietzsche challenged inherited value systems and emphasized value creation.

The death of traditional certainties creates both:

- danger,
- opportunity.

## Value Creation

To create values is not merely to pick randomly.

It involves:

- self-overcoming,
- discipline,
- interpretation.

Created values can be demanding.

## Frankl

Viktor Frankl emphasized meaning through:

- work,
- love,
- attitude toward unavoidable suffering.

His approach differs from Sartre but also rejects passive nihilism.

## Meaning Is Relational

A life can matter because of relations to:

- people,
- communities,
- projects.

Meaning need not be cosmic.

## Love

Relationships create purposes not reducible to individual preference.

Caring for another person generates:

- obligations,
- commitments.

Purpose can be interpersonal.

## Parenthood

A parent may organize life around:

- protecting,
- educating,
- supporting

a child.

This purpose is created through relationship but not arbitrarily chosen moment to moment.

## Work

Meaningful work can provide:

- contribution,
- mastery,
- structure.

But work imposed under exploitation may undermine purpose.

Context matters.

## Creation

Artists and researchers often experience purpose through:

- making,
- discovering.

The project becomes larger than immediate pleasure.

## Service

Helping others can generate durable meaning.

Purpose often extends beyond the self.

This is a recurring empirical and philosophical theme.

## Mastery

Long-term skill development gives direction.

Examples:

- music,
- mathematics,
- craft.

Progress creates temporal structure.

## Understanding

For some people, understanding reality is itself a purpose.

Science can be a form of existential commitment.

Knowledge need not be merely instrumental.

## Purpose and Pleasure

A meaningful activity may be difficult.

Therefore:

purpose ≠ pleasure.

Training,
parenting,
research

can involve frustration yet remain meaningful.

## Purpose and Happiness

Purpose can contribute to well-being.

But happiness and meaning are not identical.

A difficult sacrifice can feel meaningful without making someone happy.

## Hedonic vs Eudaimonic

Psychology often distinguishes:

- hedonic well-being:
  pleasure and comfort,
- eudaimonic well-being:
  flourishing, purpose, development.

The distinction echoes ancient philosophy.

## Aristotle

Aristotle's concept of **eudaimonia** concerns flourishing through excellent activity.

Purpose is connected to:

- virtue,
- function,
- community.

This differs from existentialist self-creation.

## Stoicism

Stoic philosophy emphasizes living according to:

- reason,
- nature.

Purpose comes from aligning action with what is under our control.

This offers another naturalistic framework.

## Buddhism

Buddhist traditions often question attachment to a fixed self.

Purpose may center on:

- reducing suffering,
- cultivating wisdom.

This differs from individualistic self-authorship.

## Religious Purpose

For religious people, purpose may be discovered rather than created.

The existentialist model is not the only possibility.

Pluralism matters.

## Can Purposes Conflict?

Yes.

Career,
family,
truth,
loyalty

can pull in different directions.

Purpose is often plural.

## No Single Life Goal

A healthy life need not have one grand mission.

Purposes can exist at multiple scales:

- daily,
- relational,
- lifelong.

This reduces pressure for one totalizing answer.

## Local Purpose

Today:

help a student.

This year:

finish a book.

A lifetime:

contribute to knowledge.

Local purposes can nest.

## Hierarchical Purpose

Purposes can form a hierarchy.

Small actions gain meaning because they serve larger commitments.

This mirrors goal hierarchies in agency.

## Purpose Can Change

People change.

A purpose appropriate at age twenty may no longer fit at fifty.

Revision is not failure.

It can be maturity.

## Identity and Purpose

Long-term purposes help stabilize identity.

We become partly the projects we repeatedly choose.

This links purpose to self-formation.

## Narrative Meaning

Humans interpret lives as stories.

Events gain meaning through:

- context,
- direction.

Narrative can organize purpose.

## Narrative Danger

A rigid life story can trap someone.

Reality does not owe us a clean plot.

Good purpose allows revision.

## Socially Supplied Purpose

Societies provide roles:

- teacher,
- citizen,
- parent.

These roles can support meaning.

They can also become oppressive if imposed without choice.

## Tradition

Inherited traditions provide ready-made frameworks.

Rejecting all inherited meaning is unnecessary.

Created purpose can include consciously chosen tradition.

## Authenticity

A purpose feels more authentic when it survives reflection.

Ask:

Would I endorse this commitment if I understood its sources?

This connects purpose to autonomy.

## Manipulated Purpose

If advertising or coercion installs a goal, is it truly yours?

Agency requires capacity to:

- examine,
- revise.

Purpose should not merely be implanted preference.

## Reflection

Reflection helps distinguish:

what I happen to want

from:

what I want to stand for.

This is a Frankfurt-like hierarchy of values.

## Second-Order Commitment

You may not only want:

to succeed.

You may want:

to be the kind of person who values truth over success.

Purpose operates at higher-order levels.

## Free Will Connection

Even if compatibilism is true, purpose can be self-created through reasons-responsive reflection.

Metaphysical indeterminism is not required.

Self-authorship can be gradual and causal.

## Hard Incompatibilist Connection

Even if ultimate free will is impossible, people can still have:

- goals,
- relationships,
- projects.

Meaning does not require causa sui status.

## Meaning Under Determinism

A deterministic poem can still be beautiful.

A deterministic friendship can still matter.

Causal determination does not erase value.

## Nihilism

**Nihilism** can mean that no objective values or meanings exist.

Even if cosmic objective meaning is absent, practical and relational meaning may remain.

The inference from:

no cosmic meaning

to:

nothing matters

is invalid.

## Subjectivism

Pure subjectivism says:

whatever I choose becomes meaningful.

This seems too weak.

Some choices are shallow or self-destructive.

Purpose may require more than preference.

## Objective List Theories

Some philosophers argue certain goods contribute to meaningful life objectively.

Possible examples:

- knowledge,
- achievement,
- relationships.

Purpose may therefore be partly created and partly constrained by real human goods.

## Susan Wolf

Susan Wolf has argued that meaning arises when:

subjective attraction

meets:

objective attractiveness.

One is actively engaged in projects of worth.

This bridges subjectivism and objectivism.

## Worthy Projects

A project can matter beyond merely occupying attention.

Examples:

- curing disease,
- making art,
- caring for others.

Meaning connects self to value outside self.

## Criticism of Objective Worth

Who decides which projects are worthy?

Cultural power can distort judgments.

Any objective account needs humility and pluralism.

## Interpersonal Recognition

Meaning often depends on being recognized by others.

We are social creatures.

Purpose is partly relational and communal.

## Isolation

A project pursued entirely alone can still matter.

But prolonged social isolation often undermines well-being.

Human purpose evolved in social worlds.

## Mortality

Death gives purpose urgency.

Time is limited.

Choices exclude alternatives.

Finitude creates weight.

## Would Immortality Destroy Meaning?

Bernard Williams argued that endless life could become intolerably boring under some conditions.

Others disagree.

The thought experiment shows mortality and purpose are connected.

## Scarcity of Time

Because we cannot do everything, commitment matters.

A purpose says:

this rather than every possible thing.

Limitation creates structure.

## Legacy

Some people seek purpose through what remains after death:

- ideas,
- children,
- institutions.

Legacy extends projects beyond one lifespan.

## Cosmic Insignificance

The universe is enormous.

Does our small scale make our lives insignificant?

Size is not a measure of value.

A bacterium is smaller than a mountain.

That does not make the mountain morally important.

## Temporal Insignificance

Human lives are brief cosmically.

But duration also does not determine meaning.

A short experience can matter deeply.

## The View from Nowhere

From a cosmic perspective, one life may seem negligible.

From within human relationships, it may be central.

Both perspectives can be true.

## Scale Relativity

Meaning is often scale-relative.

A sentence matters inside a story.

A neuron matters inside a brain.

A human purpose can be real inside human life.

## Self-Created Does Not Mean Solitary

Purpose is created with materials we did not invent:

- language,
- culture,
- relationships.

Self-creation is always socially scaffolded.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Individuals can create purpose without creating themselves from nothing.

Purpose emerges through:

- commitment,
- reflection,
- relationship,
- action.

A universe without assigned purpose can still contain lives that matter.

## The End of Part XVII

Part XVII began with free will.

It ends with purpose.

The arc is clear:

Even if humans are fully part of nature, we can still be:

- agents,
- reason-responsive systems,
- creators of projects.

We do not need to stand outside nature to live meaningfully.

## The Next Question

Purpose depends on what we take to be true and valuable.

That leads naturally to belief.

Why do we believe what we believe?

When does belief become knowledge?

Part XVIII begins with:

**What Is Belief?**
