Why do we blame one person and excuse another?

Because moral responsibility depends on more than:

what happened.

It depends on:

- intention,
- knowledge,
- control.

Free-will debates matter because they challenge the foundations of praise and blame.

## Moral Responsibility

To be morally responsible is to be appropriately answerable for an action.

This can include:

- praise,
- blame,
- obligation to repair.

But different theories disagree about why these responses are justified.

## Responsibility vs Causation

To say:

Alice caused the window to break

is not yet to say:

Alice is morally responsible.

A gust of wind can cause damage.

Moral responsibility requires agency.

## Intent

If Alice intentionally breaks the window, responsibility is stronger than if:

she trips accidentally.

This is why intent matters.

## Knowledge

Suppose Alice presses a button believing it turns on a light.

Unknown to her, it destroys a machine.

Her responsibility may be reduced because she lacked relevant knowledge.

## Control

Suppose someone physically forces Alice's hand.

The causal chain passes through her body.

But her control is absent.

Responsibility falls.

## Three Core Conditions

Many theories therefore emphasize:

- epistemic condition:
  did the person know?
- control condition:
  could they govern the action?
- ownership condition:
  did the action issue from their agency?

## Mens Rea

Criminal law often uses the concept of **mens rea**:

the guilty mind.

Legal categories distinguish states such as:

- intention,
- knowledge,
- recklessness,
- negligence.

These are practical theories of responsibility.

## Actus Reus

Law also requires an action or prohibited conduct:

**actus reus**.

Responsibility usually involves both:

- mental state,
- action.

## Intention

Intentional wrongdoing is usually judged more harshly than accidental harm.

Why?

Because intention reveals stronger alignment between:

- agent,
- outcome.

## Recklessness

A reckless person consciously disregards substantial risk.

They may not desire the harmful result.

But they knowingly expose others to danger.

## Negligence

Negligence involves failure to exercise reasonable care.

The person may not have consciously considered the risk.

Responsibility can therefore extend beyond explicit intention.

## Ignorance

Ignorance can excuse.

But not always.

If ignorance itself was negligent, responsibility may remain.

This creates recursive responsibility.

## Voluntary Ignorance

A person may deliberately avoid learning inconvenient facts.

This is sometimes called **willful blindness**.

Ignorance then does not fully excuse.

## Coercion

Threats can reduce responsibility.

But how strong must the threat be?

Moral and legal systems assess:

- severity,
- alternatives.

Responsibility often comes in degrees.

## Duress

Under **duress**, a person acts because of a serious threat.

The action may remain intentional but less voluntary.

This shows:

intent ≠ full responsibility.

## Compulsion

Internal compulsion can also reduce control.

Severe addiction or psychiatric disorder may affect responsibility.

The challenge is distinguishing:

difficulty

from:

incapacity.

## Capacity

Responsibility depends partly on capacities such as:

- understanding,
- self-control,
- reasons responsiveness.

This aligns naturally with compatibilism.

## Children

Young children are agents in some sense.

But responsibility is limited because relevant capacities are still developing.

This supports graded responsibility.

## Insanity Defense

Legal systems sometimes excuse or reduce responsibility when severe mental disorder undermines:

- understanding,
- control.

The exact standards vary.

The underlying idea is capacity-sensitive responsibility.

## Compatibilist Responsibility

Compatibilists argue moral responsibility can survive determinism.

If a person acts through:

- their own reasons-responsive mechanism,
- without coercion,

the action can be attributable to them.

## Incompatibilist Challenge

If the person's character and reasons were themselves determined by causes before birth, can they truly deserve blame?

This is the hard incompatibilist objection.

## Basic Desert

The strongest responsibility notion is **basic desert**.

The person deserves blame or punishment simply because they did wrong.

No future benefit is needed.

## Retributivism

Retributive theories of punishment emphasize desert.

Wrongdoers should suffer proportionately because:

they deserve it.

This view is most threatened by free-will skepticism.

## Consequentialism

A consequentialist justifies punishment by its effects.

Possible goals include:

- deterrence,
- incapacitation,
- rehabilitation.

Ultimate metaphysical freedom is less important.

## Deterrence

Punishment can alter behavior by changing incentives.

This makes perfect sense in a causally determined world.

Indeed, determinism makes causal influence central.

## Incapacitation

A dangerous person can be restrained to protect others.

This does not require believing they deserve suffering.

Protection and retribution are distinct.

## Rehabilitation

Intervention can aim to change:

- habits,
- skills,
- opportunities.

This treats harmful behavior as causally modifiable.

## Restorative Justice

Restorative approaches focus on:

- repairing harm,
- acknowledging responsibility,
- rebuilding relationships.

The goal is not merely inflicting pain.

## Quarantine Analogy

Hard incompatibilists sometimes compare justified restraint to quarantine.

A dangerous individual may be restricted without moral hatred.

The analogy supports forward-looking accountability.

## Objection to Quarantine

Critics worry this model treats persons merely as dangerous objects.

Respect for autonomy may require more than risk management.

Responsibility practices also express moral relationships.

## P. F. Strawson

P. F. Strawson emphasized **reactive attitudes**:

- resentment,
- gratitude,
- forgiveness.

These are natural features of interpersonal life.

Responsibility is embedded in relationships.

## Resentment

Resentment communicates:

you wronged me.

It presupposes that the other person was a participant in a moral relationship.

This is different from merely controlling dangerous behavior.

## Forgiveness

Forgiveness can modify blame while preserving acknowledgment of wrongdoing.

It makes sense only within a responsibility framework.

## Excuses

We suspend reactive attitudes when behavior results from:

- accident,
- coercion,
- incapacity.

This reflects practical judgments about agency.

## Exemptions

Sometimes we treat a person as not fully participating in ordinary responsibility practices.

Examples may include:

- severe cognitive incapacity.

This is Strawson's objective stance.

## Moral Luck

Responsibility is complicated by luck.

We do not control:

- outcomes,
- circumstances,
- traits.

Yet moral judgment often depends on them.

## Resultant Luck

Two equally reckless drivers behave the same.

Only one kills someone.

Do they deserve different blame?

Our intuitions often say yes, even though outcome difference is luck.

## Circumstantial Luck

One person faces an opportunity for wrongdoing.

Another never does.

Their moral records differ partly because of circumstances.

## Constitutive Luck

Character depends on:

- genes,
- upbringing,
- culture.

These are largely unchosen.

This threatens ultimate desert.

## Causal Luck

Every decision emerges from a causal history outside complete control.

Hard incompatibilists see this as the deepest problem.

## Moral Responsibility Without Ultimate Desert

A middle position can reject ultimate desert while preserving:

- accountability,
- repair,
- protection.

Responsibility can be practical rather than metaphysically absolute.

## Answerability

One concept is **answerability**.

A responsible agent can be asked:

Why did you do that?

They can explain and evaluate reasons.

This links responsibility to rational agency.

## Attributability

Another concept is **attributability**.

An action reflects the person's:

- values,
- judgments.

It is appropriately attributable to them.

## Accountability

A third concept is **accountability**.

Others may legitimately demand:

- apology,
- repair,
- changed behavior.

These three notions need not rise and fall together.

## Responsibility as Social Practice

Moral responsibility may be partly constructed by:

- norms,
- institutions.

But this does not make it arbitrary.

Practices can respond to real capacities.

## Forward-Looking Responsibility

A person may be responsible because holding them answerable improves:

- coordination,
- trust,
- future behavior.

This is compatible with causal naturalism.

## Backward-Looking Responsibility

We also care about what actually happened.

Victims may reasonably demand recognition of wrong.

A purely forward-looking model can feel incomplete.

## The Importance of Proportionality

Even if punishment is justified, it should be proportionate.

Responsibility is not a license for unlimited suffering.

This is an ethical constraint independent of metaphysics.

## Free Will Skepticism and Compassion

Skepticism can encourage more attention to:

- causes,
- rehabilitation,
- prevention.

But compassion does not require denying agency.

A nuanced view can hold both.

## Agency and Social Conditions

Responsibility should consider:

- poverty,
- coercive environments,
- education.

These factors shape available options.

Individual agency is real but situated.

## Structural Causes

Some harms arise partly from:

- institutions,
- incentives,
- organizational culture.

Blaming one individual may miss distributed causation.

## Collective Responsibility

Groups can produce outcomes no single member intended.

Examples include:

- corporations,
- governments.

Responsibility can become distributed.

## Machine Responsibility Preview

Artificial agents may soon make increasingly consequential decisions.

Can a machine be morally responsible?

Or does responsibility remain with:

- designers,
- users,
- institutions?

This is the next question.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Moral responsibility does not depend on one simple idea of free will.

It involves:

- knowledge,
- control,
- ownership,
- social relationships.

Ultimate self-creation may be impossible.

But practical accountability can remain meaningful.

## The Next Question

If artificial systems become autonomous agents, the same framework must be extended.

Can a machine:

- own an action,
- understand a norm,
- deserve blame?
