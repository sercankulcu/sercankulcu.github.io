Emotion is often treated as the opposite of reason.

That picture is misleading.

Emotions help determine:

- what matters,
- what deserves attention,
- what should be remembered,
- what action is urgent.

A mind without emotion would not necessarily be more rational.

It might be directionless.

## What Is an Emotion?

An emotion is not one thing.

It usually involves some combination of:

- bodily change,
- appraisal,
- feeling,
- action tendency,
- expression.

Different theories emphasize different components.

## Feeling and Emotion

A useful distinction is between:

- emotion as a broader organism-level process,
- feeling as the conscious subjective aspect.

One can have physiological and behavioral components without fully articulated conscious feeling.

## Affect

**Affect** is a broad term for states related to:

- valence,
- arousal,
- mood,
- emotion.

Valence refers roughly to:

pleasant vs unpleasant.

Arousal refers to:

activation intensity.

## Emotions Organize Action

Fear prepares avoidance.

Anger may prepare confrontation.

Disgust promotes rejection.

These are not rigid reflexes.

But emotions bias action toward adaptive possibilities.

## Evolutionary Perspective

Many emotional systems likely evolved because they helped organisms handle recurring problems.

Examples:

- threat,
- loss,
- social conflict,
- attachment.

This does not mean every modern emotional response is adaptive.

## Basic Emotion Theories

Some theories propose a set of basic emotions such as:

- fear,
- anger,
- disgust,
- sadness,
- happiness,
- surprise.

Paul Ekman's work strongly influenced this tradition.

The exact universality and boundaries remain debated.

## Constructed Emotion

Other theories argue emotions are constructed from more general ingredients such as:

- bodily state,
- concepts,
- context.

On this view, emotion categories are not fixed biological packages.

The debate remains active.

## James–Lange Theory

William James and Carl Lange proposed that bodily changes are central to emotional experience.

A simplified version says:

we feel afraid because we perceive our body responding.

The historical theory was more nuanced than the slogan.

## Cannon–Bard Critique

Walter Cannon and Philip Bard argued that bodily changes alone could not explain emotional experience.

They emphasized central nervous system processes.

Emotion theory became increasingly brain-centered.

## Schachter–Singer

The two-factor theory proposed that emotion depends on:

- physiological arousal,
- cognitive interpretation.

Similar bodily arousal can be experienced differently depending on context.

## Appraisal Theories

Appraisal theories emphasize evaluation.

An event becomes emotionally significant because the organism appraises it relative to:

- goals,
- expectations,
- control.

Emotion is relational.

## Same Event, Different Emotion

Suppose two people face the same exam.

One interprets it as:

challenge.

Another as:

threat.

The external situation is similar.

The appraisal differs.

So does emotion.

## Amygdala

The amygdala is strongly involved in processing biologically relevant stimuli, especially threat-related learning.

But it is not simply:

the fear center.

It participates in broader salience and learning processes.

## Fear Circuits

Fear-related behavior involves networks including:

- amygdala,
- hypothalamus,
- brainstem,
- cortex.

Complex emotions are network phenomena.

## Insula

The insula participates in:

- interoception,
- pain,
- salience,
- emotional awareness.

Its function cannot be reduced to one emotion.

## Prefrontal Cortex

Prefrontal systems contribute to:

- regulation,
- reappraisal,
- decision making.

Emotion and cognition interact through distributed networks.

## Interoception

**Interoception** is perception of internal bodily state.

Signals include:

- heartbeat,
- breathing,
- gut activity.

These signals contribute to emotional experience.

## Body and Emotion

Emotion is deeply embodied.

The nervous system continually monitors:

- internal state,
- external threat,
- social context.

Feeling emerges from integration.

## Facial Expression

Emotions can influence facial expression.

Expressions also communicate social information.

But mapping emotion to expression is not perfectly one-to-one.

Context matters.

## Culture

Cultures shape:

- expression rules,
- emotion concepts,
- interpretation.

Biology and culture interact.

Neither alone explains the full emotional repertoire.

## Mood

Mood differs from a brief emotion.

It is usually:

- longer-lasting,
- less tied to one specific trigger.

Mood changes the background against which events are interpreted.

## Emotion and Attention

Threat captures attention.

Reward-related cues attract attention.

Emotion helps allocate limited processing resources.

It changes what becomes figure.

## Emotion and Memory

Emotion can strengthen memory for salient events.

But it can also distort detail.

High confidence does not guarantee accuracy.

## Emotion and Decision

Antonio Damasio's work emphasized that decision making can deteriorate when emotion-related neural systems are damaged.

Pure abstract reasoning is not enough.

Values must guide choice.

## Somatic Marker Hypothesis

Damasio proposed that bodily-associated signals help mark options as:

good,
bad,
dangerous,
promising.

These **somatic markers** may guide decisions when explicit calculation is costly.

The theory is influential and debated.

## Expected Utility Is Not Enough

A perfectly rational decision model needs preferences.

Without value, there is nothing to optimize.

Emotion contributes to valuation.

Reason cannot select goals from logic alone.

## Fear and Risk

Fear can make low-probability dangers feel more important.

This can be adaptive under uncertainty.

It can also produce distorted risk estimation.

Emotion influences probability judgment.

## Anger and Confidence

Anger can sometimes increase:

- certainty,
- approach behavior.

Different emotions alter cognition in distinct ways.

They are not one generic noise signal.

## Sadness

Sadness is often associated with:

- loss,
- withdrawal,
- changed attention.

Its functions and forms vary.

Persistent clinical conditions should not be reduced to ordinary sadness.

## Disgust

Disgust likely has roots in contamination avoidance.

Humans also extend disgust into:

- moral,
- social judgments.

Biological systems become cultural metaphors.

## Shame and Guilt

These emotions depend strongly on social norms and self-evaluation.

They require models of:

- self,
- others,
- expected standards.

Emotion can be cognitively sophisticated.

## Pride

Pride connects success to self-model.

It can reinforce socially valued behavior.

It can also distort self-assessment.

## Empathy

Empathy involves responsiveness to another person's state.

Researchers distinguish aspects such as:

- affective sharing,
- cognitive perspective-taking.

Empathy is not one single capacity.

## Compassion

Compassion involves concern for another's suffering and motivation to help.

It is related to empathy but not identical.

A person can understand distress without sharing it directly.

## Emotional Regulation

Humans can regulate emotion through strategies such as:

- reappraisal,
- attention shift,
- suppression.

Regulation does not mean eliminating emotion.

It changes the emotional process.

## Reappraisal

Reappraisal changes interpretation.

A stressful event may be reframed as:

temporary,
manageable,
informative.

Changing the model changes the emotion.

## Suppression

Suppression attempts to inhibit expression after emotion has arisen.

It may reduce visible behavior without eliminating internal response.

Different regulation strategies have different consequences.

## Emotion and Rationality

Emotion can interfere with reasoning.

But emotion can also make reasoning possible by assigning:

- relevance,
- urgency,
- value.

The opposition:

emotion vs reason

is too simple.

## Affective Forecasting

People are often poor at predicting how future events will affect their feelings.

We may overestimate:

- intensity,
- duration.

Motivation is based partly on imperfect emotional forecasts.

## Hedonic Adaptation

Emotional responses to major changes often diminish over time.

People adapt.

This complicates naive assumptions about what will permanently make us happy or miserable.

## Reward and Emotion

Reward-learning systems contribute to motivation.

But reward, pleasure, desire, and emotion are not identical.

Multiple systems interact.

## Emotion in Animals

Many animals display behavior and physiology consistent with affective states.

The exact nature of subjective experience is difficult to infer.

Continuity across species is scientifically plausible without requiring identical emotion concepts.

## Machine Emotion

A machine can simulate emotional expression.

It can use variables resembling:

- urgency,
- confidence,
- reward.

Does that constitute emotion?

A functionalist may be open to the possibility.

A biological view may require more.

## Artificial Affect

AI systems can use affective computing to:

- detect emotion,
- adapt interaction.

This is not evidence that the system itself feels.

Recognition and experience are different.

## Emotion and Consciousness

Some emotional processing occurs unconsciously.

But conscious feeling is central to many emotions as humans experience them.

The relation between affect and consciousness will return later.

## Emotion and Self

Emotions help construct identity.

We learn:

what we care about,
what threatens us,
what we value.

A self without affect would be radically different.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Emotion is not a primitive enemy of rationality.

It is a system for:

- valuation,
- salience,
- bodily regulation,
- action preparation.

Emotion tells cognition what matters.

Reason then works inside a landscape already structured by value.

## The Next Question

If emotion assigns importance, reasoning tries to connect beliefs and evidence.

But human reasoning is neither perfectly logical nor purely intuitive.

How does reasoning actually work?

That is the next topic:

**Reasoning.**
