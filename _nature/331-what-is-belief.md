You believe:

the Earth orbits the Sun.

You believe:

your keys are on the table.

You may believe:

a friend will keep a promise.

What exactly is a **belief**?

A belief is not merely a sentence spoken aloud.

It is a state that guides how an agent interprets and acts in the world.

## Belief as Attitude

Philosophers often describe belief as a **propositional attitude**.

A person takes a certain attitude toward a proposition.

Example:

Proposition:

it will rain tomorrow.

Possible attitudes:

- believe it,
- doubt it,
- deny it.

## Proposition

A proposition is roughly:

the content that can be true or false.

Different sentences can express the same proposition.

"Snow is white."

and a translation in another language may express the same content.

## Belief and Truth

Belief is psychological.

Truth is not.

You can believe something false.

This is one of the most important distinctions in epistemology.

## Belief as Representation

A cognitive system may represent the world as being a certain way.

To believe:

the door is locked

is to maintain a model in which:

door = locked.

This representation can guide action.

## Belief and Action

If you believe the door is locked, you may:

- look for a key,
- choose another exit.

Beliefs influence behavior through their content.

## Dispositional Belief

Not every belief is consciously active.

You believe:

Paris is in France

even when you are not thinking about Paris.

This is a **dispositional belief**.

## Occurrent Belief

An **occurrent belief** is actively present in thought.

Example:

you suddenly think:

"The meeting is at three."

The distinction matters because minds contain more beliefs than are consciously accessible at once.

## Explicit and Implicit Belief

Some beliefs are explicit:

you can state them directly.

Others are implicit:

they influence behavior without clear verbal endorsement.

## Implicit Bias

A person may sincerely endorse equality while displaying automatic biased associations.

Does that association count as a belief?

The answer depends on theory.

Belief may come in layers.

## Belief vs Acceptance

Sometimes we **accept** a claim for practical purposes without fully believing it.

A scientist may say:

"Assume the ideal gas law."

This is acceptance within a model.

Not necessarily belief about literal reality.

## Belief vs Guess

A guess is a weak commitment.

You may guess:

the coin will land heads.

But you may not genuinely believe it.

Confidence matters.

## Belief and Confidence

Beliefs are often treated as binary:

believe / do not believe.

But real cognition is graded.

You may be:

- 55% confident,
- 95% confident.

This suggests probabilistic belief.

## Credence

A **credence** is a degree of belief.

Example:

\[
P(\text{rain tomorrow}) = 0.7
\]

Bayesian epistemology often models beliefs this way.

## Binary vs Graded Belief

Do ordinary beliefs reduce to thresholds on credence?

Perhaps:

believe P if confidence > 0.8.

But thresholds create problems.

The relationship is not straightforward.

## Lottery Paradox

Suppose a lottery has one million tickets.

For each ticket, you believe:

this ticket will lose.

Yet you also believe:

one ticket will win.

Your highly probable beliefs cannot all be jointly true.

This is the **lottery paradox**.

## Belief Consistency

Ideally, beliefs should be mutually consistent.

Humans often hold contradictions.

Belief systems are not perfectly logical databases.

## Cognitive Dissonance

When beliefs or actions conflict, people may experience **cognitive dissonance**.

They sometimes revise beliefs to reduce tension.

Belief change is psychologically motivated, not purely logical.

## Confirmation Bias

People tend to seek and interpret evidence in ways that support existing beliefs.

This makes beliefs self-reinforcing.

We will examine this more deeply later.

## Belief Perseverance

A belief can survive even after its original evidence is discredited.

This is **belief perseverance**.

Once integrated into a worldview, beliefs can become resistant to correction.

## Motivated Reasoning

Desire can shape belief formation.

People may accept evidence more easily when it supports:

- identity,
- goals,
- group membership.

Belief is not always neutral inference.

## Wishful Thinking

You want:

the test result to be good.

You begin to believe:

it probably is.

This is wishful thinking.

Desire contaminates evidence assessment.

## Belief and Desire

Belief represents:

how the world is.

Desire represents:

how the world should be.

This is a classic distinction.

## Direction of Fit

Beliefs have a **mind-to-world direction of fit**.

If belief conflicts with reality:

change belief.

Desires have a **world-to-mind direction of fit**.

If reality conflicts with desire:

try to change reality.

## Intentions

Intentions are different again.

They commit the agent to action.

Belief:

the door is closed.

Desire:

I want it open.

Intention:

I will open it.

## Belief and Evidence

A rational belief should respond to evidence.

If evidence changes, belief should change.

This is an epistemic norm.

Humans often violate it.

## Evidence Is Not Self-Interpreting

The same evidence can support different conclusions depending on:

- background assumptions,
- models.

Belief formation always occurs within context.

## Background Beliefs

To interpret one claim, we rely on many others.

Example:

You believe the thermometer is reliable.

That background belief affects whether you trust the reading.

## Web of Belief

W. V. O. Quine and others emphasized that beliefs form a network.

Evidence usually confronts not one isolated statement but a larger **web of belief**.

Revision can occur at different points in the web.

## Central and Peripheral Beliefs

Some beliefs are easy to revise.

Others are deeply connected to identity or theory.

Central beliefs are more resistant to change.

## Belief Revision

Formal belief-revision theory studies how rational agents should update inconsistent belief sets.

One influential framework is AGM theory.

## AGM Intuition

When new information arrives, an agent may need to:

- expand beliefs,
- contract beliefs,
- revise beliefs.

The goal is minimal coherent change.

## Belief and Memory

Many beliefs depend on memory.

But memory is reconstructive.

A remembered event can be:

- distorted,
- incomplete.

Belief inherits memory's imperfections.

## Testimony

Much human belief comes from other people.

You believe facts about:

- distant countries,
- history,
- science

without direct observation.

Testimony is essential.

## Epistemic Dependence

No individual verifies everything.

Knowledge is social.

Belief systems depend on:

- experts,
- institutions,
- records.

This makes trust epistemically central.

## Authority

Authority can be a legitimate source when the authority is:

- competent,
- reliable.

But authority can also be abused.

Belief should not become blind obedience.

## Belief and Identity

Some beliefs express group membership.

They function socially as signals.

This can make factual correction difficult.

Changing belief may feel like changing identity.

## Ideological Beliefs

Beliefs can organize whole worldviews.

They may determine:

- what counts as evidence,
- whom to trust.

Epistemology therefore intersects sociology.

## Delusion

A **delusion** is a strongly held belief resistant to counterevidence, often associated with psychiatric conditions.

Clinical definitions are complex.

The key point is that confidence alone does not guarantee truth.

## Hallucination vs Delusion

A hallucination is typically a perceptual experience without corresponding external stimulus.

A delusion is a belief.

Perception and belief can dissociate.

## Seeing Is Not Believing

You can experience an illusion while believing:

"I know these lines are equal."

Belief can override perceptual appearance.

## Belief and Imagination

You can imagine:

a dragon

without believing dragons exist.

Representation does not imply belief.

This is crucial for understanding fiction.

## Fiction

While reading a novel, you may emotionally respond to characters you know are fictional.

Imagination can guide emotion without literal belief.

## Pretense

Children can pretend:

this box is a spaceship.

They represent one world while believing another.

Cognition can maintain multiple models.

## Belief in Machines

Can machines have beliefs?

A simple system can store:

door_locked = true.

Is that a belief?

Functionalists may say yes in a minimal sense.

Others require richer semantic commitment.

## Database vs Belief

A database contains information.

But it does not necessarily:

- act,
- update autonomously,
- care about truth.

Belief may require embedding representation inside agency.

## Agent Belief

In AI, agents often maintain **belief states** about uncertain environments.

A belief state can be a probability distribution over possible world states.

This is operationally precise.

## Bayesian Belief State

Example:

\[
P(s_1)=0.6,\quad P(s_2)=0.4
\]

The agent acts based on this uncertainty.

Belief becomes mathematically actionable.

## False Belief in Machines

A robot can maintain an incorrect map.

Then it behaves according to a false representation.

This resembles false belief functionally.

## Belief and Understanding

A system can store a proposition without understanding it deeply.

Belief and understanding are distinct.

Humans also hold many shallow beliefs.

## Belief and Commitment

Belief involves some degree of commitment to truth.

If challenged, the agent should defend or revise it.

This distinguishes belief from temporary token storage.

## Contradictory Beliefs

Humans can hold:

P

and effectively:

not-P

in different contexts.

This suggests beliefs may be fragmented rather than globally consistent.

## Contextual Activation

Different situations activate different representations.

A person may reason differently at:

work,
home.

Belief may be context-sensitive.

## Fragmentation

Some philosophers propose **fragmented belief** models.

The mind contains partially isolated belief clusters.

Contradictions can persist because they are rarely activated together.

## Rational Ideal vs Human Reality

Logic gives ideals:

- consistency,
- evidence responsiveness.

Human cognition is bounded.

We operate under:

- limited memory,
- limited time.

Belief rationality is therefore approximate.

## Bounded Rationality

Herbert Simon emphasized **bounded rationality**.

Agents satisfice rather than optimize perfectly.

Belief formation occurs under cognitive constraints.

## Belief as Prediction

One modern view says beliefs are parts of predictive models.

The brain predicts:

- sensory input,
- consequences.

Belief becomes probabilistic world-modeling.

## Predictive Processing

Under predictive-processing frameworks, perception itself involves hypotheses about causes.

The boundary between:

perception

and:

belief

becomes less sharp.

## Belief and Action Loop

Beliefs guide actions.

Actions generate new evidence.

Evidence updates beliefs.

This forms a loop:

belief → action → observation → belief.

## Epistemic Agency

A good agent does not merely hold beliefs.

It manages them.

It asks:

- what evidence do I have?
- how confident should I be?
- what would change my mind?

This is epistemic agency.

## Belief Is Cheap

Anyone can believe anything.

The hard question is:

When does belief become justified?

And when does justified belief become knowledge?

## The Beginning of Part XVIII

Part XVIII examines:

- belief,
- knowledge,
- skepticism,
- bias,
- uncertainty.

The central question is:

How should a finite mind decide what to believe?

## The Philosophical Lesson

Belief is a commitment to a representation of the world as true.

It can be:

- explicit,
- implicit,
- graded,
- false.

Belief guides action.

But belief alone is not enough for knowledge.

## The Next Question

You can sincerely believe something and still be wrong.

So what must be added before belief becomes knowledge?

First we must make one distinction absolutely clear:

**Belief Is Not Knowledge.**
