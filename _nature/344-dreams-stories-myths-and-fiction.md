Sherlock Holmes did not live at 221B Baker Street as a historical detective.

Yet millions of people know:

- his habits,
- his methods,
- his companion.

A dream can contain a city that never existed.

A myth can shape a civilization.

How can something that is not literally real have real effects?

## Different Senses of Reality

We need distinctions.

A fictional character is not:

physically instantiated as the described person.

But the character exists as:

- text,
- representation,
- cultural object.

Reality has levels.

## Fictional Truth

Within a story, some statements are true.

Example:

Sherlock Holmes is a detective.

Within Conan Doyle's fiction:

true.

Sherlock Holmes is a professional astronaut:

false.

Fiction has internal structure.

## Truth in Fiction

Philosophers study how statements can be true **in** fictional worlds.

This does not require the fictional entities to exist physically.

The truth is relative to a representational framework.

## Possible-World Semantics

One approach treats fiction through sets of possible worlds compatible with the story.

A fictional statement is true if it holds in the relevant story worlds.

This is useful but technically complex.

## Fictionalism

**Fictionalism** is a broader philosophical strategy.

We can use discourse that behaves as if entities exist without committing to literal ontology.

This approach appears in debates about:

- mathematics,
- morality.

## Stories Are Physical Too

A novel has physical realization:

- ink,
- pixels,
- neural memories.

The story is not physically nonexistent.

Its **referent** may be fictional.

## Multiple Realization

The same story can exist as:

- book,
- film,
- memory.

Narrative content is not identical to one physical token.

It is a repeatable informational pattern.

## Narrative as Information

A story encodes:

- agents,
- events,
- causal relations.

It creates a structured possibility space.

Humans simulate that space mentally.

## Simulation

When reading, the mind predicts:

- motives,
- consequences.

Fiction can function as social simulation.

## Emotional Response

We cry over fictional deaths.

Why?

Because emotional systems respond to represented situations.

Literal belief is not necessary for every emotion.

## Paradox of Fiction

The **paradox of fiction** asks:

How can we feel genuine emotion toward entities we know do not exist?

Different theories answer through:

- imagination,
- quasi-emotion,
- make-believe.

## Belief Is Not Required

You need not believe:

the monster is real

to feel fear during a film.

Representation can activate emotional systems.

## Suspension of Disbelief

The phrase suggests we temporarily stop disbelieving.

But this is probably too strong.

We usually retain knowledge that the story is fictional.

We become imaginatively engaged.

## Imagination

Imagination allows representation without belief.

You can entertain:

a dragon is behind the door

while believing:

there is no dragon.

This cognitive separation is fundamental.

## Counterfactual Thought

Fiction and imagination support:

What if?

This is essential for:

- planning,
- science,
- ethics.

Imagined worlds are tools for reasoning.

## Thought Experiments

Philosophy uses fiction-like scenarios:

- trolley problem,
- zombies,
- brain in a vat.

Their value comes from testing concepts.

## Scientific Models

Science also uses idealized fictions:

- frictionless plane,
- ideal gas.

These entities are not literally real.

Yet they reveal structure.

## Models as Controlled Fiction

A scientific model can be viewed as a disciplined fictional world constrained by:

- mathematics,
- data.

The difference from ordinary fiction is epistemic purpose.

## Dreams

Dreams are internally generated experiences during sleep.

They can include:

- places,
- people,
- events

with little correspondence to current external reality.

## Dream Reality

A dream event is not real as an external event.

But the experience is real as:

a brain and conscious event.

Again, ontology depends on level.

## Lucid Dreams

In lucid dreaming, a person realizes:

I am dreaming

while the dream continues.

Belief about reality can change inside experience.

This is philosophically striking.

## Dream Logic

Dreams can violate:

- continuity,
- identity.

Yet while dreaming, strange events may feel normal.

Reality testing is altered.

## Memory and Dreams

Dream memory is unstable.

Narrative coherence may be reconstructed after waking.

We should be cautious about treating remembered dreams as exact recordings.

## Myths

A **myth** is not simply a false story.

In anthropology and religious studies, myths are culturally important narratives about:

- origins,
- values,
- social order.

Their function exceeds factual reporting.

## Myth and Explanation

Historically, myths explained:

- seasons,
- death,
- cosmos.

Science later replaced many causal explanations.

But myths can continue to carry symbolic meaning.

## Mythic Truth

A myth can be factually false yet express:

- moral,
- psychological,
- social truths.

This is a different sense of truth.

We should keep the senses distinct.

## Literal vs Symbolic

Trouble begins when symbolic meaning is treated as empirical fact without evidence.

Interpretive and scientific claims need separate standards.

## Origin Myths

Cultures create stories explaining:

where people came from.

These narratives organize identity.

Their historical accuracy varies.

## National Myths

Nations also create simplified narratives about:

- founders,
- heroic events.

These stories can unify groups.

They can also distort history.

## Myth-Making Is Ongoing

Modern societies are not post-mythic.

Brands,
politics,
celebrities

generate narratives with mythic structure.

## Hero Stories

Complex historical processes are often compressed into:

one heroic individual.

Narrative simplifies causation.

This can hide institutions and collective action.

## Villain Stories

The reverse also happens.

One villain is made responsible for a complex failure.

Narrative prefers intentional agents.

## Narrative Bias

Stories prefer:

- clear causes,
- arcs,
- endings.

Reality often contains:

- ambiguity,
- randomness.

Narrative coherence can exceed factual coherence.

## Memory as Narrative

Autobiographical memory constructs life stories.

We organize:

events

into:

identity.

The self partly exists narratively.

## False Coherence

Looking backward, life can appear inevitable.

We connect events into a path.

This is related to hindsight bias.

## Narrative Identity

Philosophers such as Paul Ricoeur and psychologists have explored the idea that identity is partly narrative.

We understand ourselves through stories.

## Story as Compression

Narrative compresses massive detail into causal structure.

This makes experience manageable.

But compression removes alternatives.

## Fiction and Empathy

Fiction can expose readers to:

- perspectives,
- lives.

It may expand social imagination.

The magnitude and conditions of such effects are empirically debated.

## Moral Laboratory

Stories let us explore consequences without real-world cost.

We can test:

loyalty,
betrayal,
sacrifice.

Fiction becomes an ethical simulation space.

## Propaganda

The same narrative power can manipulate.

A vivid story can overpower statistics.

Fictional technique is ethically neutral.

Its use is not.

## Deepfakes

Modern generative systems blur fiction and documentation.

Synthetic video can appear evidential.

The epistemic boundary between representation and record becomes harder to police.

## Documentary Appearance

A photograph feels like direct evidence.

But images can be:

- staged,
- cropped,
- altered.

Representational realism should not be confused with truth.

## Virtual Reality

VR can create convincing environments.

The virtual mountain is not a geological mountain.

But the user's:

- fear,
- movement,
- memory

are real.

## Digital Objects

Objects in games have:

- rules,
- persistence.

A virtual sword can be real **within a game system** while not being a metal sword.

## Institutional Fiction

Law contains entities that exist partly through collective representation.

A corporation is a legal construct.

Yet its contracts have consequences.

Social reality often begins as shared fiction-like representation.

## "As If"

Humans coordinate by acting **as if** certain abstractions are real.

Money,
borders,
roles

become causally effective through collective acceptance.

## Searle's Institutional Reality

John Searle emphasized constitutive rules:

X counts as Y in context C.

A piece of paper counts as money.

Social reality is rule-created.

## Collective Intentionality

Institutional facts depend on shared recognition.

If everyone stops treating the currency as valid, its social function collapses.

## Fiction vs Lie

Fiction is normally presented as fiction.

A lie presents falsehood as fact.

The epistemic ethics differ.

## Myth vs Deception

A myth can be symbolic without intending empirical deception.

Categories must be handled carefully.

## Fiction and Possibility

Fiction explores worlds that could exist.

It expands our model space.

Science fiction has often influenced technological imagination.

## But Fiction Does Not Predict Automatically

A novelist imagining a technology does not provide scientific evidence that it is possible.

Creativity and feasibility are different.

## Counterfactual Intelligence

The capacity to simulate unreal possibilities may be central to intelligence.

We plan by imagining futures that do not yet exist.

## Reality Requires Contrast With Possibility

To understand what **is**, we often compare it with:

what could have been.

Fiction and modal thought illuminate reality indirectly.

## Dreams and Skepticism

Dreams show:

experience can occur without ordinary external correspondence.

This fuels skepticism.

But waking reality is distinguished by stronger causal stability.

## Shared Reality

A dream is private.

Waking experience supports:

- shared measurement,
- coordination.

Intersubjective constraint helps distinguish reality from imagination.

## A Story Can Change the World

A fictional work can influence:

- law,
- fashion,
- scientific ambition.

The represented world may be fictional.

The causal consequences are not.

## Ontological Layering

We should therefore ask:

real in what sense?

A fictional character can be:

- unreal as historical person,
- real as cultural representation.

This avoids false binaries.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Dreams, stories, myths, and fiction show that representation has its own reality.

A representation need not correspond literally to an external object to:

- carry meaning,
- cause emotion,
- shape society.

Reality is richer than simple physical instantiation.

## The Next Question

One domain pushes this issue even further.

Numbers do not appear to be physical objects.

Yet mathematics describes nature with extraordinary precision.

Are mathematical entities invented symbols?

Or are they discovered realities?

The next essay asks:

**Is Mathematics Real?**
