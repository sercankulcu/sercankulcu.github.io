What is real?

A rock seems real.

A dream seems less real.

A number is real in a different sense.

A corporation can:

- own property,
- sign contracts

without being a physical object like a chair.

Reality may not be one simple category.

## Ontology

**Ontology** asks:

what exists?

It is one of the oldest branches of metaphysics.

The question is not merely:

What can we observe?

It is:

What kinds of things must we say are real?

## Ontological Commitment

When a theory says:

electrons exist,

it commits to electrons.

When mathematics quantifies over:

numbers,
sets,

does it commit us to those too?

This is the problem of **ontological commitment**.

## Quine

W. V. O. Quine famously linked ontology to quantification.

Roughly:

to be is to be the value of a bound variable.

If our best theory says:

there exists an x such that...

we take x seriously.

## Reality and Observation

We observe:

instrument readings,
tracks,
effects.

We rarely observe fundamental entities directly.

Reality therefore extends beyond immediate perception.

## Electrons

No one sees an electron with naked eyes.

We infer electrons from:

- repeatable experiments,
- successful theory.

Indirect evidence can support realism.

## Scientific Realism

Scientific realism says successful scientific theories aim to describe a mind-independent world.

Unobservable entities such as:

- electrons,
- fields

can be genuinely real.

## Instrumentalism

Instrumentalism is more cautious.

A theory may be a useful predictive instrument without literally describing hidden reality.

The equations work.

Ontology may be secondary.

## No-Miracles Argument

Realists ask:

Why are mature scientific theories so predictively successful?

One answer:

because they approximately describe real structure.

Otherwise success would seem miraculous.

## Pessimistic Meta-Induction

Critics point out that many past successful theories contained entities later rejected.

Examples include:

- phlogiston,
- caloric.

Success does not guarantee ontology.

## Structural Realism

A compromise is **structural realism**.

Perhaps science reliably captures:

relations,
structure

even when its picture of underlying entities changes.

## Entity Realism

Another view says we should be realist about entities we can manipulate reliably.

If electrons can be used to produce controlled effects, that supports their reality.

## Mind-Independent Reality

A central realist intuition is:

the world does not disappear when nobody looks.

Mountains existed before humans.

Reality is not created by ordinary observation.

## Observer Dependence

Yet some properties depend on observers.

Color depends partly on:

- visual systems,
- illumination.

Does that make color unreal?

Not necessarily.

## Relational Reality

A property can be real as a relation.

Velocity is relative to reference frame.

That does not make velocity imaginary.

Observer-dependence and unreality are different.

## Social Reality

Money has value because people collectively treat it as valuable.

Its social status is constructed.

But bank balances have real effects.

Social construction does not mean fiction.

## Institutional Facts

John Searle distinguished brute facts from **institutional facts**.

A piece of paper is physically paper.

Within institutions it can be:

currency.

The second fact depends on collective rules.

## Corporations

A corporation is not one physical object.

It is a legally and socially organized entity.

It can:

- persist,
- own assets,
- incur obligations.

This is a real higher-level pattern.

## Nations

Nations depend on:

- people,
- institutions,
- borders,
- narratives.

They are socially constructed.

Yet wars and laws show their causal reality.

## Emergent Reality

Earlier we saw that higher-level properties can be real without being fundamental.

Temperature is real.

It emerges from molecular dynamics.

Reality can be layered.

## Reduction Does Not Eliminate

If temperature reduces to molecular statistics, it does not become unreal.

Likewise:

mind,
life,
economy

may be real at higher levels.

## Dennett's Real Patterns

Daniel Dennett proposed that a pattern is real when it supports:

- compression,
- prediction.

A chess strategy is not a particle.

But recognizing it explains behavior.

## Pattern Realism

This suggests reality includes stable higher-level patterns.

Not every real thing must be fundamental.

## Fictional Entities

Sherlock Holmes does not exist as a historical detective.

Yet statements about Holmes can be:

- true within the fiction.

Fiction has structure without literal external existence.

## Possible Worlds

Modal logic talks about:

possible worlds.

Are these real?

David Lewis famously defended modal realism:

possible worlds are as real as ours.

Most philosophers find this ontologically expensive.

## Abstract Objects

What about:

- numbers,
- sets,
- propositions?

Platonists say abstract objects are real.

Nominalists reject them as independent entities.

## Mathematical Platonism

On Platonism:

2

exists independently of minds.

Mathematics discovers abstract reality.

## Nominalism

Nominalists try to explain mathematics without committing to abstract entities.

Numbers may be:

- symbols,
- linguistic devices.

The debate remains open.

## Fictionalism About Mathematics

Mathematical fictionalists treat mathematical discourse as useful without literal commitment to abstract objects.

The mathematics can work even if numbers are not entities.

## Properties

Does:

redness

exist?

Or only red things?

Realists about universals say properties are real.

Nominalists say categories are constructed from particulars.

## Universals

A **universal** is a repeatable property:

redness,
triangularity.

How can the same property occur in many objects?

This is the problem of universals.

## Tropes

Trope theory says each object has its own particularized property instance.

This apple has:

this redness.

Another apple has:

another redness.

Similarity links them.

## Events

Are events real?

A storm is not an object in the same way as a rock.

It is a temporally extended process.

Ontology may need:

- objects,
- events.

## Processes

Process philosophy emphasizes change rather than static substances.

Maybe the fundamental reality consists of:

events,
relations.

Objects become relatively stable processes.

## Fields

Modern physics describes fields extending through space.

Particles may be excitations of fields.

Everyday object ontology may be far from fundamental physics.

## Quantum Fields

In quantum field theory, particle number can change.

Fields appear more fundamental than classical particles.

But interpreting the ontology of QFT remains philosophically difficult.

## Wavefunction

Is the quantum wavefunction real?

Some interpretations treat it as physical.

Others treat it as:

- information,
- knowledge.

Quantum ontology is contested.

## Spacetime

General relativity treats spacetime geometrically.

Is spacetime a substance?

Or merely relations among events?

This revives the old substantivalism–relationalism debate.

## Relationalism

Relationalists say space is constituted by relations among physical things.

No independent container is needed.

## Substantivalism

Substantivalists treat spacetime points or structure as real in their own right.

Modern physics does not automatically settle the metaphysics.

## Consciousness

Is subjective experience real?

Even illusionists must explain:

- reports,
- self-models.

Phenomenal realists say experience is among the most undeniable realities.

## The Self

Is the self real?

It may not be a simple indivisible substance.

But a constructed self-model can still be a real causal pattern.

## Illusion ≠ Unreal Process

If the self is an illusion, the mechanisms generating that illusion are real.

The word "illusion" often hides ontological ambiguity.

## Levels of Reality

We can distinguish:

- fundamental,
- biological,
- psychological,
- social.

Reality may be plural across levels.

## Dependence

A social fact depends on physical systems.

This does not make it nonexistent.

Dependence is not unreality.

## Grounding

Metaphysicians use **grounding** for relations of metaphysical dependence.

Example:

the fact that a table exists may be grounded in arrangements of particles.

Grounding is not ordinary causation.

## Grounding vs Causation

Particles do not cause the table to exist in the same temporal sense that fire causes smoke.

They constitute or ground it.

This mirrors earlier brain–consciousness distinctions.

## Fundamentality

Some entities may be more fundamental than others.

But:

less fundamental

does not mean:

less real.

A hurricane is real despite depending on molecules.

## Ontological Parity

Some philosophers resist ranking reality.

If an entity participates in true explanations, perhaps it is simply real.

Fundamentality is one property among others.

## Realism About Everyday Objects

Physics tells us tables are mostly empty space.

Does that mean tables are not real?

No.

"Solid table" is a higher-level description of electromagnetic and quantum structure.

## Manifest Image

Wilfrid Sellars distinguished the **manifest image**:

the world of:

- people,
- objects,
- colors.

from the **scientific image**:

theoretical scientific ontology.

## Scientific Image

Science describes:

- fields,
- particles,
- spacetime.

The challenge is integrating both images.

Neither can simply be ignored.

## Eliminativism

Sometimes science removes an entity.

Phlogiston was abandoned.

So some higher-level concepts genuinely fail.

Reality should not be granted merely because a word exists.

## Criteria for Reality

Possible criteria include:

- causal power,
- explanatory necessity,
- predictive value,
- independence from belief.

No single criterion works perfectly for every domain.

## Causal Criterion

If something has causal effects, it seems real.

But abstract numbers appear causally inert.

Are they therefore unreal?

The criterion has limits.

## Explanatory Criterion

If an entity appears indispensably in our best explanations, perhaps we should accept it.

This supports scientific realism.

## Indispensability Argument

Quine and Putnam argued that because mathematics is indispensable to science, we should accept mathematical entities along with scientific ones.

The argument is controversial.

## Fictional Use

Critics reply that a concept can be useful without literal ontological commitment.

Models often idealize nonexistent objects.

Utility alone is insufficient.

## Mind Dependence

Some things exist only because minds exist.

Examples:

- laws,
- money,
- games.

They are still real within social systems.

## Objective vs Subjective

Reality is not divided cleanly into:

objective = real,
subjective = unreal.

Pain is subjective and real.

Currency is intersubjective and real.

## Intersubjective Reality

An intersubjective fact exists because many agents coordinate around it.

Institutions belong here.

This is a distinct mode of reality.

## Virtual Objects

A digital object can have:

- persistence,
- ownership,
- causal effects.

Does a file exist?

Certainly in an ordinary sense.

It is realized through physical states.

## Software

Software is not identical to one physical device.

The same program can run on different hardware.

It is a real abstract-functional pattern.

## Multiple Realizability Again

A real entity may be realized in many physical forms.

This supports higher-level ontology.

## Reality and Models

A model is not the world.

But models may successfully refer to real structures.

We need not choose between:

perfect representation

and:

pure fiction.

## Approximate Truth

Scientific realism often accepts **approximate truth**.

A model can be wrong in detail while capturing important structure.

Newtonian mechanics is the classic example.

## Reality Beyond Access

A mind-independent world may contain structures humans cannot directly perceive or conceptualize.

Epistemic limits do not imply ontological limits.

## Humility

We should separate:

what exists

from:

what we can currently know exists.

Ontology exceeds epistemology.

## The Beginning of Part XIX

The final section returns to our opening questions.

After:

- physics,
- information,
- mind,
- belief,

we ask what all these descriptions are descriptions **of**.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Reality is not one homogeneous category.

It may include:

- fundamental entities,
- emergent patterns,
- subjective experiences,
- social institutions.

The hard task is deciding which ontological commitments our best explanations genuinely require.

## The Next Question

Even if reality exists independently of us, we never encounter it without mediation.

We encounter:

appearances.

How should we distinguish what seems to be from what is?

The next essay is:

**Reality and Appearance.**
