Grammar is often taught as a list of rules about correct writing.

Do not split this.

Do not say that.

Use this form.

But in linguistics, grammar means something deeper.

It is the structured system that allows speakers to:

- form expressions,
- recognize patterns,
- interpret relations.

Grammar is not merely etiquette.

It is architecture.

## Descriptive Grammar

A **descriptive grammar** asks:

How does a language actually work?

It studies the patterns speakers reliably use.

If millions of native speakers use a construction consistently, it belongs to the linguistic system whether or not a style guide approves.

## Prescriptive Grammar

A **prescriptive grammar** asks:

How should people write or speak under a chosen standard?

Prescriptive rules matter in:

- education,
- publishing,
- professional communication.

They are social norms, not the whole science of grammar.

## Grammar Is Mostly Unconscious

Native speakers know enormous amounts of grammar without being able to state the rules.

An English speaker immediately senses that:

"The child opened the door."

is natural,

while:

"Child the door the opened."

is not.

Implicit knowledge exceeds explicit explanation.

## Word Order

Languages use different word orders.

English commonly follows:

Subject–Verb–Object.

Turkish commonly allows Subject–Object–Verb patterns.

Word order is one grammatical resource.

It is not universal in one fixed form.

## Morphology

Languages also encode grammar inside words.

A word can carry information about:

- tense,
- number,
- case,
- person.

Some languages rely heavily on morphology.

Others rely more on word order.

Grammar distributes information differently.

## Agreement

Words can change form to match grammatical features.

English:

"She runs."

"They run."

Other languages have much richer agreement systems.

Grammar encodes relationships across parts of the sentence.

## Case

Case marking can indicate roles such as:

- subject,
- object,
- possession,
- location.

Languages with rich case systems can often allow freer word order because morphology identifies grammatical roles.

Structure can be signaled in multiple ways.

## Constituents

Sentences are organized into **constituents**.

Consider:

"The small dog chased the cat."

"The small dog" behaves as a unit.

"The cat" behaves as another.

Grammar groups words into hierarchical chunks.

## Constituency Tests

Linguists use tests such as:

- substitution,
- movement,
- coordination

to identify constituents.

If a group of words behaves together under multiple operations, that supports treating it as a structural unit.

## Phrase Structure

A simplified phrase structure might be:

Sentence
→ Noun Phrase + Verb Phrase.

The verb phrase may contain:

Verb + Noun Phrase.

This produces a tree.

Grammar is hierarchical.

## Syntax Trees

A syntax tree makes relationships visible.

Words at the bottom combine into phrases.

Phrases combine into larger phrases.

The tree represents structure that linear word order alone cannot show.

## Ambiguity

Consider:

"I saw the man with the telescope."

Two structures are possible.

The observer may use the telescope.

Or the man may possess it.

Same words.

Same order.

Different tree.

Different meaning.

## Structure Determines Interpretation

This demonstrates a major principle:

meaning depends not only on which words appear, but how they are grouped.

Syntax guides semantics.

Representation is hierarchical.

## Dependencies

Grammar also contains dependencies across distance.

"Which book did she say that the student bought?"

The word "which" is interpreted as the object of "bought" even though many words intervene.

Language tracks nonlocal relations.

## Recursion

Grammar can embed structures inside structures.

A noun phrase can contain a relative clause.

That clause contains another sentence-like structure.

This supports open-ended generativity.

## Categories

Grammar groups words into categories such as:

- noun,
- verb,
- adjective,
- determiner.

These categories are not defined only by meaning.

They are also defined by how words behave structurally.

## Distribution

The word "quickly" is recognized as an adverb partly because of where it can occur.

Linguistics studies **distribution**:

which environments accept which forms.

Structure can reveal category.

## Grammar Is More Than Syntax

Grammar broadly includes:

- morphology,
- syntax,
- phonology,
- sometimes aspects of semantics.

Different linguistic traditions draw boundaries differently.

The key point is that language is organized at several levels.

## Phonological Structure

Sound systems have grammar-like organization too.

Languages restrict which sound combinations are allowed.

English permits:

`str-`

at the beginning of a word.

Other combinations are impossible or rare.

Speakers internalize these constraints.

## Morphological Structure

Words can be decomposed into morphemes.

"unhappiness"

contains roughly:

un + happy + ness.

But not every affix can attach everywhere.

Morphology has combinatorial rules.

## Grammaticality

A sentence can be grammatically well-formed but semantically odd.

"A square circle sings politely."

The syntax may be acceptable.

The meaning is strange.

Grammar and world knowledge are separate.

## Acceptability

Linguists distinguish **grammaticality** from **acceptability**.

A sentence may be structurally possible yet hard to process.

Long center embeddings are a classic example.

Human judgments reflect both grammar and cognitive limitations.

## Usage

Modern linguistics also studies how frequency shapes grammar.

Common patterns become entrenched.

Rare forms may disappear.

Usage and structure interact.

Grammar is not necessarily a fixed timeless rulebook.

## Statistical Structure

Speakers are sensitive to probabilities.

Some grammatical forms are possible but uncommon.

Language competence includes graded expectations, not only binary legal/illegal judgments.

This has become increasingly important in computational linguistics.

## Construction Grammar

Some theories treat grammar as a network of learned form-meaning pairings called **constructions**.

A construction can range from:

individual word

to

abstract sentence pattern.

This contrasts with theories emphasizing a small set of universal syntactic principles.

## Generative Grammar

Generative theories emphasize formal rules or principles capable of producing the structures of a language.

Their goal is to explain:

- productivity,
- hierarchy,
- acquisition.

There are many generative frameworks.

No single version defines all modern linguistics.

## Universal Grammar

Chomskyan traditions have proposed that humans possess innate constraints on possible grammar.

The precise content of **Universal Grammar** has changed across theories.

The idea remains debated.

## Usage-Based Views

Usage-based theories emphasize:

- exposure,
- frequency,
- analogy,
- social interaction.

Children learn patterns from actual language use.

These approaches often see grammar as emergent from learned constructions.

## Statistical Learning

Children can detect statistical regularities in sequences.

This ability may contribute to:

- word segmentation,
- pattern learning,
- category formation.

Language acquisition likely combines multiple learning mechanisms.

## Grammar and Meaning

Grammar helps determine:

who did what to whom.

Compare:

"Dog bites man."

"Man bites dog."

The vocabulary is identical.

Structure reverses roles.

Grammar is a machine for relational meaning.

## Grammar and Thought

Because grammar organizes relations, it may influence how thoughts are externalized and manipulated.

But grammatical structure should not automatically be equated with the architecture of all thought.

Language is one representational system among several.

## Grammar as Compression

A grammar compresses linguistic knowledge.

Instead of memorizing every sentence, speakers internalize reusable patterns.

Finite structure generates open-ended expression.

This is another example of compact rules producing vast possibility.

## The Next Question

Grammar contains several interacting levels.

Words themselves have internal structure.

Phrases combine into syntax.

Sentences carry semantics.

Context adds pragmatics.

Sounds or signs are organized phonologically.
