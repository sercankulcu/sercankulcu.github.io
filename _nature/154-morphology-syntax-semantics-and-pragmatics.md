Language works through several interacting layers.

A word has internal structure.

Words combine into phrases.

Phrases express propositions.

Speakers use those propositions in situations.

Four major concepts help separate these layers:

- morphology,
- syntax,
- semantics,
- pragmatics.

They overlap.

They are not interchangeable.

## Morphology

**Morphology** studies the internal structure of words.

Consider:

`unhappiness`

It can be analyzed as:

un + happy + ness.

Each part contributes structure or meaning.

These minimal meaningful units are called **morphemes**.

## Free and Bound Morphemes

Some morphemes can stand alone.

`book`

`run`

Others cannot.

`-ed`

`un-`

`-s`

These are bound morphemes.

Languages build words by combining both kinds.

## Inflection

**Inflection** changes grammatical form without creating a fundamentally new lexical item.

English examples include:

walk → walked

cat → cats

The core word remains recognizable.

Grammar adds information such as tense or number.

## Derivation

**Derivation** often creates a new word or category.

happy → happiness

teach → teacher

legal → illegal

Morphology can therefore generate vocabulary, not just grammatical variants.

## Morphological Diversity

Languages differ greatly.

English uses relatively modest inflection.

Turkish can build long words by adding multiple suffixes.

Other languages organize grammatical information differently.

There is no single universal word architecture.

## Syntax

**Syntax** studies how words and phrases combine.

Compare:

"The dog chased the cat."

"The cat chased the dog."

Same words.

Different structure.

Different meaning.

Syntax organizes relationships.

## Hierarchy

Syntax is not just word order.

Sentences contain nested phrases.

A prepositional phrase may attach to one constituent rather than another.

A relative clause may modify one noun phrase.

Hierarchical structure determines interpretation.

## Semantics

**Semantics** studies conventional meaning.

Questions include:

- What does a word denote?
- What proposition does a sentence express?
- How do smaller meanings combine?

Semantics connects linguistic form to interpreted content.

## Lexical Semantics

Words have meanings and relationships.

Examples:

dog / animal

hot / cold

buy / sell

Lexical semantics studies:

- synonymy,
- antonymy,
- category relations,
- polysemy.

Words live in semantic networks.

## Compositional Semantics

The meaning of a sentence depends partly on:

- word meanings,
- syntactic structure.

This is the principle of **compositionality**.

"The cat chased the dog"

and

"The dog chased the cat"

contain the same lexical items.

Structure changes the proposition.

## Pragmatics

**Pragmatics** studies meaning in context and use.

The sentence:

"Can you open the window?"

literally asks about ability.

In ordinary conversation, it usually functions as a request.

Pragmatics explains the additional inference.

## Literal and Intended Meaning

A speaker can mean more than the literal sentence states.

"There's a draft in here."

may function as:

"Please close the window."

The listener infers intention from context.

Human communication is inferential.

## Speech Acts

Language performs actions.

A speaker can:

- promise,
- apologize,
- order,
- warn,
- declare.

These are **speech acts**.

Words can alter social relations when spoken under appropriate conditions.

## Context

Pragmatic interpretation depends on:

- who is speaking,
- where,
- when,
- shared knowledge.

The same sentence can perform different acts in different contexts.

## Phonology

Although not in the title, another major layer is **phonology**.

Phonology studies how a language organizes sound or sign contrasts.

It determines which distinctions matter linguistically.

Language is layered from physical form upward.

## A Layered Example

Take:

"She walked home."

### Morphology
walk + -ed

### Syntax
subject + verb + complement

### Semantics
a past event involving a female referent moving homeward

### Pragmatics
perhaps an answer to "How did she get home?"

The same utterance supports several analyses.

## Layers Interact

The layers are conceptually separable.

In real language, they influence each other.

Morphology can resolve syntax.

Syntax can resolve semantic ambiguity.

Pragmatic expectations can affect how syntax is interpreted.

Language is an interacting system.

## Morphology and Syntax

Some grammatical information can be expressed morphologically in one language and syntactically in another.

Languages distribute structure differently.

This shows why linguistic categories are relational rather than tied to one surface form.

## Syntax and Semantics

Syntax constrains meaning.

But meaning also influences parsing.

"The astronomer saw the star with the telescope."

World knowledge helps decide who has the telescope.

Grammar alone may permit multiple structures.

## Semantics and Pragmatics

Literal meaning can remain constant while pragmatic interpretation changes.

"You're early."

It can be:

- praise,
- surprise,
- complaint,
- irony.

Context supplies communicative force.

## Ambiguity at Every Layer

Ambiguity can occur in:

- word meaning,
- syntax,
- reference,
- pragmatic intention.

Language remains usable because humans combine multiple information sources.

Understanding is constraint satisfaction.

## Why the Distinctions Matter

Without these distinctions, we may ask the wrong questions.

A computer can parse syntax correctly while missing pragmatics.

A child may know word meaning while still learning morphology.

A sentence can be grammatical but nonsensical.

Each layer has its own problems.

## Language Models

Modern language models learn statistical patterns across all these layers to varying degrees.

They can often reproduce:

- morphology,
- syntax,
- semantic associations,
- pragmatic patterns.

But performance does not automatically settle whether they genuinely understand.

That debate requires a theory of meaning.

## The Next Question

One distinction deserves special attention.

A sentence can be perfectly well-formed yet fail to convey coherent meaning.

Formal systems can manipulate symbols without knowing what they refer to.

So the next question is:

**Why is syntax not the same as meaning?**
