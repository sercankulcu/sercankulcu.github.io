There is a difference between:

a camera detecting red

and:

red appearing to someone.

There is a difference between:

a sensor registering damage

and:

pain hurting.

This difference is the domain of **subjective experience**.

## What Makes Experience Subjective?

An experience is subjective because it occurs:

for a subject.

There is:

a point of view.

The experience is not merely an event in the world.

It is an event as lived from somewhere.

## First-Person Perspective

Science usually describes reality from a third-person perspective.

It asks:

What can observers measure?

Subjective experience adds another perspective:

What is it like from within the system?

## Perspective Is Not Opinion

"Subjective" can mean:

mere opinion.

That is not the relevant sense.

Pain can be objectively real even though only one person feels that particular pain directly.

Subjective does not mean unreal.

## The Subject

What is the subject of experience?

Possibilities include:

- organism,
- brain process,
- self-model,
- stream of consciousness.

The answer is not obvious.

## Experience Without a Strong Self

A system may have experience without possessing a sophisticated autobiographical self.

Infants and many animals may be conscious without reflecting:

"I am a continuing person."

Subjectivity does not require philosophical self-awareness.

## Minimal Subjectivity

At minimum, experience may involve:

- a centered perspective,
- distinction between self-related and world-related information.

This is sometimes called a minimal self.

## Phenomenology

**Phenomenology** studies the structure of experience as experienced.

Questions include:

- what appears,
- how it appears,
- how attention changes it.

This philosophical tradition predates modern neuroscience.

## Husserl

Edmund Husserl developed phenomenology as a systematic study of conscious experience.

He emphasized intentionality:

consciousness is typically consciousness **of** something.

## Intentionality

A visual experience is about:

an object.

A thought is about:

a proposition.

A fear is about:

a threat.

Subjective experience often has directedness.

## Not Every Experience Is Clearly Object-Directed

Some states are diffuse.

Examples include:

- anxiety,
- bodily discomfort,
- mood.

They may not have one obvious object.

Subjective life includes both focused and background experience.

## Background Experience

At any moment, consciousness may include:

- bodily posture,
- ambient sound,
- vague mood.

These are not always in focal attention.

Experience has a field-like structure.

## Figure and Ground

Some content occupies the foreground.

Other content remains background.

Attention can shift the boundary.

This connects consciousness to earlier ideas about figure and ground.

## Temporal Structure

Experience is not a series of isolated points.

The present contains traces of:

- immediate past,
- anticipated future.

Subjective time has thickness.

## Specious Present

Philosophers and psychologists have used the term **specious present** for the short interval experienced as now.

A melody illustrates this.

You must retain earlier notes to hear the phrase.

## Music

A single note is not a melody.

Consciousness integrates sequences.

The present experience of music contains:

memory of what just happened

and:

expectation of what comes next.

## Stream of Consciousness

William James emphasized that consciousness flows.

Thoughts transition.

Percepts fade into memories.

Experience is continuous even when content changes.

## Discontinuity

Yet neural processing is not literally smooth in every respect.

Attention and perception may update in bursts.

The relationship between neural discreteness and phenomenal continuity remains debated.

## Privacy

Your experience is privately accessible.

Another person can observe:

- your brain,
- your behavior.

They cannot literally occupy your first-person perspective.

## Privacy Is Epistemic

This privacy may concern access, not substance.

A password-protected file is inaccessible to outsiders without being nonphysical.

Likewise, first-person privilege does not prove dualism.

## First-Person Authority

If you say:

"I have a headache,"

your report has special authority.

Others usually cannot override it based solely on external observation.

But first-person authority is not absolute.

## Misidentifying Experience

People can confuse:

- anxiety with excitement,
- memory with imagination.

They can misinterpret causes.

Subjective access is immediate, but interpretation can be wrong.

## Experience vs Judgment

A person may experience:

a visual illusion

while knowing:

the illusion is false.

The percept and the judgment can diverge.

This shows subjective appearance is not belief.

## Müller-Lyer Illusion

Two lines may look different in length even after you know they are equal.

Knowledge does not erase the perceptual experience.

Experience has partial autonomy.

## Transparency

When we look at a red apple, we seem aware of:

the apple,

not:

our neural representation.

Experience is often transparent toward the world.

## Transparency and Construction

The percept may still be constructed.

We experience:

a stable external object.

The brain hides the computational process producing that appearance.

## The Brain Does Not Show Its Work

We are usually conscious of:

results,

not:

intermediate processing.

Visual computation involving thousands of neural operations appears simply as:

"there is a cup."

## Introspection Is Selective

We cannot inspect:

- synaptic weights,
- early visual features,
- grammatical parsing

directly.

Subjective access covers only a fraction of mental processing.

## Experience and Report

A report is an action caused by many systems.

The experience itself may precede or exceed what can be reported.

Words compress phenomenology.

## Richness Debate

Some philosophers argue conscious experience is richer than what attention and working memory can access.

Others argue apparent richness is an illusion.

This is an active debate.

## Sperling Experiment

George Sperling's iconic-memory experiments showed people briefly have access to more visual information than they can report all at once.

Whether this demonstrates richly conscious content or short-lived preconscious representation is debated.

## Change Blindness

People can fail to notice large visual changes.

This suggests our sense of a detailed visual world may exceed actual maintained representation.

Subjective confidence can mislead.

## Inattentional Blindness

People focused on one task can miss highly visible events.

Attention strongly shapes conscious access.

Seeing is not merely opening the eyes.

## Experience and Attention

Attention can:

- enhance,
- select,
- stabilize

content.

But some theories argue consciousness can occur without focal attention.

The relationship is not settled.

## Experience and Memory

A conscious state can vanish without being stored.

If you cannot remember an experience, did it occur?

This creates methodological difficulty.

## Retrospective Report

Most consciousness experiments ask after the fact:

What did you see?

Memory becomes part of measurement.

The experience itself has already passed.

## Experience and Language

Language lets us classify and communicate experience.

But experience likely predates language evolutionarily and developmentally.

Language is not required for all subjectivity.

## Ineffability Again

Some aspects of experience resist precise description.

That may reflect:

- limited vocabulary,
- high-dimensional phenomenology.

Ineffability need not imply supernatural properties.

## High-Dimensional Experience

A smell can contain subtle dimensions difficult to name.

Experts can learn richer labels.

Language compresses a complex perceptual space.

## Qualitative Similarity

We can compare experiences:

orange is more like red than blue.

These similarity judgments make phenomenology scientifically tractable.

Subjective experience has structure.

## Psychophysics

Psychophysics links:

physical stimulus

to:

reported experience.

It maps lawful relationships across the first-person/third-person boundary.

## Neural Decoding

Neuroscientists can sometimes predict what a person sees from brain activity.

This reduces privacy at the informational level.

It does not give the scientist the person's actual experience.

## Decoding Is Not Sharing

Knowing:

you see red

is different from:

seeing red as you do.

Information about experience is not identical to possession of that experience.

## Empathy

Humans approximate others' subjective states through:

- imagination,
- similarity,
- social learning.

Empathy bridges perspectives imperfectly.

It does not literally merge them.

## Other Minds

We assume other humans have experiences because of:

- behavioral similarity,
- biological similarity,
- shared development.

This is an inference.

It is extraordinarily strong in ordinary life.

## Animal Subjectivity

Animals may have subjective worlds unlike ours.

A bat's echolocation is not simply:

human hearing plus extra data.

Its perceptual world may be organized differently.

## Machine Subjectivity

If an artificial system were conscious, its perspective might be radically unfamiliar.

It might organize experience around:

- text,
- high-dimensional vectors,
- nonhuman sensors.

Human phenomenology need not be the template.

## Subjectivity and Self-Model

A system may model:

this body,
this location,
these goals.

Such a self-model could create a centered perspective.

But a model of self does not automatically imply subjective experience.

## Subjectivity and Embodiment

Bodies provide a natural center.

Sensory signals arrive relative to:

- this eye,
- this skin,
- this body.

Embodiment may help organize first-person structure.

## Interoception

Internal bodily signals contribute to subjective presence.

Heartbeat,
breathing,
gut state

shape experience.

The self is not only visually located.

## Predictive Processing

Predictive theories treat perception as inference from a generative model.

Subjective experience may correspond to the system's best current model of:

world,
body.

This explains structure.

The hard problem remains.

## Subjective Certainty

Descartes used the apparent certainty of thought as a philosophical foundation.

Even if the world is doubted, experience itself seems difficult to deny.

Something appears.

## Can Experience Be Illusory?

A visual content can be illusory.

But can the fact:

"there is an experience"

itself be an illusion?

Illusionism says some properties attributed to experience are mistaken.

Critics argue the occurrence of appearance cannot itself disappear.

## Appearance and Reality

Consciousness is peculiar because appearance is part of the reality being studied.

If something seems painful, the seeming itself is a real mental event.

This gives phenomenology unusual epistemic status.

## The Explanatory Target

A theory of consciousness must not only explain:

- reports,
- discrimination.

It must identify what makes subjective perspective possible.

Otherwise the original puzzle remains.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Subjective experience is not merely private data.

It is the fact that information processing appears from a point of view.

This first-person organization is central to consciousness.

The challenge is explaining how a physical system acquires:

an inside.

## The Next Question

Not every conscious creature needs to know:

"I am conscious."

This suggests an important distinction.

A being may be capable of feeling without possessing a reflective self-concept.

The next essay separates:

**Sentience and Self-Awareness.**
