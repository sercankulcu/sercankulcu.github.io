You believe you are reading this page.

But how do you know?

Maybe you are:

- dreaming,
- hallucinating,
- inside a simulation.

Skepticism asks whether our apparent knowledge can survive such possibilities.

## Skepticism

**Skepticism** is not simply:

doubting everything.

Philosophical skepticism asks whether our beliefs satisfy the standards required for knowledge.

## Local Skepticism

You can be skeptical about one domain.

Examples:

- astrology,
- memory testimony,
- one experiment.

This is ordinary critical thinking.

## Global Skepticism

Global skepticism is much stronger.

It asks whether we can know:

anything about the external world.

This is the classical challenge.

## Ancient Skepticism

Ancient Greek skeptics explored suspension of judgment.

Pyrrhonian skepticism emphasized:

**epoché**,

withholding assent when arguments balance.

The aim was partly tranquility.

## Academic Skepticism

Other ancient skeptics argued that knowledge may be impossible.

Different traditions used skepticism differently.

## Descartes

René Descartes used radical doubt methodologically.

He wanted to find a foundation that could survive every skeptical challenge.

## Methodological Doubt

Descartes did not doubt merely for entertainment.

He asked:

What cannot possibly be doubted?

Everything uncertain would be temporarily set aside.

## Sensory Error

Our senses sometimes deceive us.

Objects look smaller at a distance.

Straight sticks appear bent in water.

Therefore sensory belief is not automatically certain.

## Dream Argument

Sometimes dreams feel realistic.

If I can dream that I am sitting by a fire, how can I prove right now that I am awake?

This threatens ordinary perceptual knowledge.

## Dreaming vs Waking

We usually distinguish waking from dreaming using:

- coherence,
- memory,
- stable interaction.

But the skeptic can imagine a perfectly convincing dream.

The challenge targets logical possibility.

## Evil Demon

Descartes imagines a powerful deceiver manipulating all experience.

Even mathematical reasoning might be corrupted.

This pushes doubt to its extreme.

## Cogito

Descartes reaches:

**I think, therefore I am.**

Even if deceived, there must be thinking occurring.

The act of doubt seems to establish at least existence of thought.

## What Does the Cogito Prove?

It may establish:

there is thinking now.

Whether it establishes a substantial enduring self is more controversial.

The skeptical foundation is thinner than ordinary identity.

## Brain in a Vat

A modern version imagines:

a brain maintained in a vat,

connected to a computer generating all sensory input.

Your experiences would seem normal.

Could you know you are not such a brain?

## Simulation Scenario

A simulation hypothesis creates a similar structure.

If all observations are generated artificially, perceptual evidence may not distinguish:

base reality

from:

simulation.

## Closure Principle

A skeptical argument often uses epistemic closure:

If you know P,

and know that P implies Q,

then you should know Q.

If you know:

I have hands,

then you should know:

I am not a handless brain in a vat.

## Skeptical Modus Tollens

The skeptic says:

1. If I know I have hands, I know I am not a brain in a vat.
2. I do not know I am not a brain in a vat.
3. Therefore I do not know I have hands.

This is powerful because each step feels plausible.

## Moore's Response

G. E. Moore famously reversed the argument.

He held up his hands and effectively argued:

1. Here is one hand.
2. Here is another.
3. Therefore an external world exists.

## Moorean Shift

Moore's strategy says:

ordinary knowledge may be more certain than the skeptical premise.

If a philosophical argument implies:

I do not know I have hands,

perhaps the argument is less credible than common-sense knowledge.

## Is That Question-Begging?

Critics say Moore simply assumes what skepticism questions.

Defenders say rational argument always begins from some comparatively secure commitments.

The dispute is about epistemic priority.

## Contextualism

**Contextualism** says the standards for "knows" vary with conversational context.

In ordinary life:

"I know the car is outside"

can be true.

When skeptical possibilities become salient, standards rise.

## Why Contextualism Helps

Ordinary knowledge survives in low-standard contexts.

Skepticism reveals that we may not meet extraordinary standards.

Both intuitions are preserved.

## Invariantism

Invariantists reject shifting standards.

Knowledge means the same thing across contexts.

They seek another response to skepticism.

## Relevant Alternatives

Another idea says knowledge requires ruling out only **relevant alternatives**.

You need not eliminate every bizarre logical possibility.

For ordinary knowledge, brain-in-a-vat scenarios may be irrelevant.

## What Makes an Alternative Relevant?

This is difficult.

Relevant alternatives may depend on:

- context,
- evidence,
- practical stakes.

The theory needs principled boundaries.

## Safety

Safety theories say:

If you know P, then in nearby possible worlds where you form the belief similarly, P remains true.

Brain-in-a-vat worlds may be too distant.

Ordinary knowledge can survive.

## Sensitivity

Sensitivity asks:

If P were false, would you still believe P?

For skeptical hypotheses, this can generate trouble.

If you were a brain in a vat, you would still believe:

I am not one.

## Nozick's Counterintuitive Result

Tracking theories may say:

you know you have hands

but do not know:

you are not a brain in a vat.

This violates closure.

Some philosophers accept that cost.

## Externalism

An externalist can say:

knowledge depends on reliable connection to reality.

If you are in fact in the normal world and perception is reliable, you can know.

You need not prove reliability from inside.

## Skeptic's Reply

But how do you know your process is reliable?

The externalist answer is:

knowledge need not require knowing that you know.

This rejects a strong reflective requirement.

## Internalism and the Regress

If every justified belief requires another justified reason, we get a regress.

How does justification end?

This is the **regress problem**.

## Foundationalism

Foundationalists say some beliefs are basic.

They are justified without inference from other beliefs.

Perceptual beliefs are common candidates.

## Coherentism

Coherentists say justification comes from fit within a mutually supporting system of beliefs.

No single belief needs absolute foundational status.

## Infinitism

Infinitism allows an endless chain of reasons.

This is a less common but serious position.

The regress need not terminate.

## Agrippa's Trilemma

Skeptical traditions highlight three unsatisfying options:

- infinite regress,
- circular support,
- arbitrary stopping point.

This is sometimes called Agrippa's trilemma.

## Münchhausen Trilemma

A modern version makes the same point.

Every justification seems to end in:

- regress,
- circle,
- dogma.

This challenges ultimate foundations.

## Fallibilist Response

Perhaps justification does not require ultimate certainty.

Beliefs can be justified by:

- reliable evidence,
- mutual support

without infallible foundations.

This is the dominant practical response.

## Scientific Knowledge Under Skepticism

Science cannot prove:

we are not in a perfect simulation.

But it can construct extremely reliable models of experienced regularities.

This may be enough for empirical knowledge.

## Instrumental Success

Scientific theories:

- predict,
- control,
- unify.

Even a skeptic must explain why these methods work so consistently within experience.

## Skepticism and Action

Complete practical skepticism is hard to live.

You still avoid:

- cliffs,
- fire.

Behavior reveals deep reliance on ordinary beliefs.

## Hume's Response

David Hume argued radical skepticism is psychologically unstable.

Nature forces us back into ordinary belief.

We cannot sustain universal doubt continuously.

## Skepticism as Tool

Skepticism is valuable when used diagnostically.

Ask:

- What supports this belief?
- Could I be mistaken?
- What alternative explanation exists?

This improves reasoning.

## Skepticism as Paralysis

If every possibility of error prevents belief, no action is possible.

Epistemology must work for finite agents.

Absolute doubt is often too demanding.

## Radical Skepticism and Probability

Even if certainty is impossible, probabilities can differ dramatically.

Maybe:

brain in vat = logically possible.

That does not make it equally plausible with:

ordinary external world.

Possibility is not probability.

## Inference to Best Explanation

The external world explains:

- stable perception,
- intersubjective agreement,
- successful prediction.

A skeptical scenario can reproduce these too, but often at greater complexity.

Ockham's razor favors ordinary realism.

## Underdetermination

Still, if two hypotheses generate exactly the same possible observations, evidence cannot distinguish them.

This is genuine underdetermination.

Some metaphysical questions may remain empirically undecidable.

## Pragmatic Vindication

Even if ultimate proof is unavailable, trusting perception works.

It enables:

- survival,
- science,
- coordination.

Pragmatic success does not prove metaphysics, but it supports practice.

## Skepticism and AI

A machine also operates under partial observability.

It cannot directly know hidden world state.

It maintains beliefs and updates from evidence.

Epistemic uncertainty is not uniquely human.

## POMDP Analogy

In a partially observable environment, an agent maintains a probability distribution over states.

It acts despite uncertainty.

This is a useful model of finite epistemic agency.

## Knowing Without Certainty

The lesson is not:

nothing can be known.

It is:

knowledge need not require ruling out every conceivable skeptical scenario.

Fallible knowledge can still be robust.

## Intellectual Humility

Skepticism teaches humility.

Our beliefs are:

- revisable,
- perspective-bound.

But humility should not collapse into:

all claims are equal.

Evidence still matters.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Skepticism exposes a gap between:

certainty

and:

ordinary knowledge.

We may be unable to prove that deception is impossible.

But this does not force us to abandon well-supported belief.

The practical task is to distinguish:

reasonable doubt

from:

unlimited doubt.

## The Next Question

If humans can be skeptical, why do we so often believe claims with little evidence?

Why are:

- conspiracies,
- superstitions,
- pseudoscientific stories

so psychologically attractive?

**Why Humans Believe Strange Things.**
