You look outside and believe:

it is raining.

Maybe you are right.

Maybe a sprinkler is hitting the window.

Belief can be:

- true,
- false,
- justified,
- unjustified.

Knowledge is more demanding.

## The Fundamental Distinction

To believe P is:

to take P to be true.

To know P is:

to stand in a stronger epistemic relation to P.

Exactly what stronger relation is required is the central problem of epistemology.

## False Belief

You can believe:

the capital of Australia is Sydney.

That belief is false.

Therefore it is not knowledge.

Truth is necessary.

## True Belief

Suppose you guess:

the coin will land heads.

It does.

Your belief was true.

Did you know?

No.

True belief alone is not enough.

## Lucky Truth

A belief can be true by accident.

Knowledge seems incompatible with excessive epistemic luck.

This becomes central in the Gettier problem.

## Classical Analysis

A traditional account says knowledge is:

**justified true belief.**

Formally:

S knows P if:

1. P is true,
2. S believes P,
3. S is justified in believing P.

This is the JTB model.

## Why Belief Is Required

Can you know something you do not believe?

Suppose evidence overwhelmingly supports P, but you insist:

"I do not think P is true."

It seems strange to say you know P.

Knowledge normally includes belief.

## Why Truth Is Required

You cannot know a false proposition.

You may think you know it.

But if P is false:

knowledge fails.

This is a factive property.

## Factivity

Knowledge is **factive**.

If:

S knows P,

then:

P.

Belief is not factive.

## Why Justification Is Required

A lucky guess should not count as knowledge.

Justification connects belief to:

- evidence,
- reasons,
- reliable methods.

It distinguishes knowing from accidental correctness.

## Example

You check a reliable thermometer.

It reads:

20°C.

You believe:

the room is 20°C.

If the device works normally, the belief appears justified.

## But How Much Justification?

Do you need certainty?

Probably not.

Ordinary knowledge is fallible.

You can know something even though error remains possible.

## Fallibilism

**Fallibilism** says knowledge does not require absolute certainty.

A belief can be well justified and still, in principle, mistaken.

This reflects scientific practice.

## Infallibilism

An infallibilist requires stronger certainty.

This creates skepticism about ordinary knowledge.

Few everyday beliefs meet such a standard.

## Knowledge vs Certainty

You can know:

your car is parked outside

without being certain beyond all conceivable doubt.

Knowledge and certainty should not be conflated.

## Evidence

What counts as justification?

Possible sources include:

- perception,
- memory,
- testimony,
- inference.

Each can fail.

Epistemology studies when they are reliable enough.

## Perceptual Knowledge

You see a tree.

Under normal conditions, this justifies:

there is a tree.

But hallucination is possible.

Do you still know?

Fallibilists say yes if conditions are appropriate.

## Skeptical Possibility

What if you are dreaming?

What if you are in a simulation?

If knowledge requires ruling out every logical possibility, ordinary knowledge collapses.

This motivates contextualism and fallibilism.

## Contextual Standards

In ordinary conversation, evidence may be sufficient.

In a courtroom or philosophical discussion, stronger evidence may be demanded.

Knowledge attributions can be context-sensitive.

## Memory Knowledge

You remember:

you had breakfast.

Memory usually justifies belief.

But memory can be:

- distorted,
- false.

Reliability matters.

## Testimonial Knowledge

You know:

Tokyo is in Japan

largely through testimony and education.

Knowledge does not require direct observation.

Social transmission is essential.

## Expert Knowledge

You may know a medical fact because a reliable expert tells you.

This depends on:

- expertise,
- trust.

Knowing whom to trust becomes epistemically important.

## Inference

You know:

the streets are wet.

You infer:

it probably rained.

Inference can extend knowledge beyond direct observation.

But the conclusion may be wrong.

## Deductive Knowledge

If you know:

all humans are mortal

and:

Socrates is human,

you can infer:

Socrates is mortal.

Valid deduction preserves truth if premises are true.

## Inductive Knowledge

Much scientific knowledge is inductive.

It is supported by:

- repeated evidence,
- statistical inference.

It is not deductively certain.

## Knowledge and Probability

Could knowledge simply be:

very high probability?

Not obviously.

You can be 99.999% confident a lottery ticket will lose and still hesitate to say you **know** it will lose.

## Lottery Problem

Suppose one million tickets exist.

For any particular ticket, losing probability is:

0.999999.

Yet before the draw, we usually do not say:

"I know ticket 537 will lose."

Probability alone does not capture knowledge.

## Safety

One proposed condition is **safety**.

Roughly:

a belief is safe if in nearby possible situations where you form it in the same way, it would not easily be false.

This tries to reduce epistemic luck.

## Sensitivity

Another idea is **sensitivity**:

If P were false, would you still believe P?

If yes, the belief may be epistemically defective.

This has influenced externalist theories.

## Reliabilism

**Reliabilism** says a belief is justified or knowledgeable if produced by a reliable process.

Examples:

- normal vision,
- competent measurement.

The subject need not know why the process is reliable.

## Internalism

Epistemic **internalism** says justification depends on reasons accessible to the subject.

You should be able, in some sense, to justify your belief from within your perspective.

## Externalism

Epistemic **externalism** allows factors outside conscious access.

A reliable process can produce knowledge even if the person cannot articulate the justification.

## Chicken Sexers

A classic example involves experts who reliably distinguish chick sex but cannot explain how.

If the process is reliable, do they know?

Externalists are more comfortable saying yes.

## Clairvoyant Problem

Suppose someone has a perfectly reliable but inexplicable clairvoyant faculty.

Would resulting beliefs count as knowledge?

This tests externalist intuitions.

## Knowledge by Luck

The deepest issue is not merely justification.

A belief can satisfy:

- truth,
- belief,
- apparent justification

and still be true accidentally.

This is exactly what Gettier exposed.

## Knowledge-How

Not all knowledge is propositional.

You may know:

how to ride a bicycle

without being able to state every rule.

This is **knowledge-how**.

## Knowledge-That

Knowing:

that Paris is in France

is propositional knowledge.

The distinction is associated with Gilbert Ryle.

## Acquaintance Knowledge

You may know:

a person,
a place,
a sensation

through direct acquaintance.

This differs from knowing propositions about them.

## Knowing Red

Mary's Room raised this issue.

Knowing every fact about color may differ from:

knowing what red looks like.

Different epistemic relations exist.

## Knowledge and Understanding

You can know many isolated facts without understanding the system.

Understanding involves:

- relations,
- explanations.

Knowledge is not the highest epistemic state.

## Knowledge and Wisdom

Wisdom includes good judgment about:

- what matters,
- how to act.

A person can possess enormous knowledge without wisdom.

## Collective Knowledge

Organizations can possess knowledge distributed across:

- people,
- records.

No single person may hold it all.

Knowledge can be systemic.

## Scientific Knowledge

Science does not require certainty.

It builds:

- justified,
- testable,
- revisable beliefs.

Scientific knowledge remains provisional yet strong.

## Revision

A belief can count as knowledge today and later be revised if evidence changes.

Does that mean it was never knowledge?

Not necessarily under fallibilism.

Epistemic status depends on available justification.

## Defeaters

New evidence can **defeat** justification.

Suppose you see a barn.

Then learn the area is full of fake barn facades.

Your confidence should change.

## Defeater

A defeater is information that undermines:

- evidence,
- reliability.

Knowledge requires absence of relevant undefeated defeaters.

## Epistemic Responsibility

People have duties such as:

- check evidence,
- avoid careless belief.

But responsibility does not guarantee truth.

A careful person can still be wrong.

## Belief Is Psychologically Easy

You can form a belief through:

- rumor,
- emotion.

Knowledge requires epistemic discipline.

That is why the two concepts should never be merged.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Belief means:

taking something to be true.

Knowledge requires at least:

- belief,
- truth,
- epistemic support.

But even justified true belief may fail.

A belief can be justified and true yet still be true in the wrong way.

## The Next Question

In 1963, Edmund Gettier showed this with remarkably short counterexamples.

His cases changed epistemology.

The next essay asks:

Why is **justified true belief** not enough?

That is:

**The Gettier Problem.**
