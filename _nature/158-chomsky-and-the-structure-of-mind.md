Noam Chomsky transformed twentieth-century linguistics.

His central claim was not merely about grammar.

It was about mind.

Language, he argued, reveals an internal cognitive system capable of generating indefinitely many structured expressions from finite means.

This shifted linguistics toward the study of mental architecture.

## Before Chomsky

Mid-twentieth-century psychology was strongly influenced by behaviorism.

Behaviorists emphasized:

- observable behavior,
- stimulus,
- response,
- reinforcement.

Language learning was often treated as habit formation.

Chomsky challenged this framework.

## The Review of Skinner

Chomsky's famous review of B. F. Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* argued that reinforcement and imitation were insufficient to explain linguistic creativity.

Speakers routinely produce novel sentences.

They understand sentences they have never heard.

Language therefore seemed to require internal generative structure.

## Competence and Performance

Chomsky distinguished:

### Competence
A speaker's internal knowledge of language.

### Performance
Actual language use under limits of memory, attention, and context.

A speaker may know a structure that is difficult to process.

Observed behavior does not directly equal underlying grammar.

## Generative Grammar

A **generative grammar** specifies the structures that belong to a language.

The goal is not to list sentences.

It is to characterize the system capable of generating them.

Grammar becomes a formal theory of linguistic competence.

## Deep Structure and Surface Structure

Earlier Chomskyan theories distinguished:

- deep structure,
- surface structure.

Different surface sentences could share underlying relations.

The terminology and architecture changed substantially in later work.

Historical Chomsky should not be frozen at one stage.

## Transformations

Transformational grammar proposed rules connecting structures.

For example, declarative and interrogative forms could be related through transformations.

The key idea was that surface word order may hide deeper structural relationships.

## Hierarchy Over Sequence

Chomsky emphasized that language is organized hierarchically.

Rules refer to:

- phrases,
- constituents,
- structural relations.

They do not simply count words from left to right.

This became one of the enduring insights of generative syntax.

## Structure Dependence

Consider forming a question.

"The boy is happy."

→

"Is the boy happy?"

A naive rule might say:

move the first "is."

But more complex sentences show that grammatical operations depend on hierarchical structure, not simple linear position.

This is called **structure dependence**.

## Poverty of the Stimulus

Chomsky argued that children know structural principles not explicitly taught and not obviously inferable from sparse input.

This supported the idea of innate constraints.

The argument became one of the most debated topics in cognitive science.

## Universal Grammar

**Universal Grammar** refers broadly to biologically given constraints on possible human grammars.

Different versions proposed different content.

Some assumed rich grammatical principles.

Later approaches often reduced the proposed innate machinery.

The concept is not one fixed theory.

## Principles and Parameters

One influential period proposed:

- universal principles,
- language-specific parameters.

Children would set parameters based on input.

This offered a model for explaining both universality and diversity.

Many details of this framework have since been revised or abandoned.

## The Minimalist Program

Later Chomsky developed the **Minimalist Program**.

The goal was to explain language using the simplest possible computational mechanisms compatible with observed structure.

One central operation is often called **Merge**.

## Merge

Merge combines two syntactic objects into a larger one.

For example:

word + phrase → larger phrase.

Repeated Merge produces hierarchical structure.

A very small computational operation could, in principle, generate enormous syntactic complexity.

This fits the finite-rules theme.

## Recursion

Chomskyan traditions often treat recursive hierarchical combination as central to language.

Some proposals have suggested that recursion may be a uniquely human core capacity.

This remains debated.

Human language probably depends on several interacting abilities.

## I-Language

Chomsky introduced the idea of **I-language**:

internal,
individual,
intensional.

Language, in this view, is fundamentally a mental computational system.

Public languages such as English are secondary abstractions over individual grammars.

## E-Language

By contrast, **E-language** refers to externalized language:

public utterances,
social conventions,
corpora.

Chomsky generally prioritized internal cognitive structure.

Sociolinguistic and usage-based traditions often emphasize the external and social side more strongly.

## Language as Biology

Chomsky treated language as part of human biology.

The language faculty is a property of the brain.

This naturalized language.

Grammar became a target for cognitive science rather than only philology or cultural description.

## Rationalist Tradition

Chomsky's approach aligns with a rationalist tradition emphasizing innate mental structure.

Experience triggers and shapes development.

But not all structure comes directly from sensory input.

The mind contributes organization.

## Against Blank-Slate Models

A completely blank learner would face an enormous hypothesis space.

Chomsky argued that biological constraints narrow what can be learned.

This idea influenced:

- linguistics,
- philosophy,
- cognitive science.

The debate concerns how much constraint is necessary.

## Criticism from Usage-Based Linguistics

Usage-based researchers argue that much linguistic structure can emerge from:

- frequency,
- analogy,
- chunking,
- social cognition.

They question whether rich language-specific innate grammar is needed.

Language may be more learned than classical generative theories assumed.

## Statistical Learning Challenge

Modern experiments show that infants and adults can learn surprising regularities from exposure.

Machine-learning systems also demonstrate that large amounts of linguistic structure can emerge statistically.

These findings pressure stronger poverty-of-stimulus arguments.

They do not prove that innate structure is absent.

## Social Interaction Challenge

Language is used between people.

Critics argue that a theory focused narrowly on syntax risks underestimating:

- communication,
- gesture,
- joint attention,
- pragmatic inference.

Language is a biological capacity embedded in social life.

## Diversity Challenge

The world's languages vary greatly.

Some linguists argue that universal claims were sometimes based too heavily on a small number of well-studied languages.

Cross-linguistic diversity tests theories of universal structure.

## Pirahã Debate

Claims about Pirahã and recursion became a famous controversy.

Some researchers argued that the language challenges claims about universal syntactic recursion.

Others disputed the analysis.

The debate illustrates how empirical language description can bear on theories of human cognition.

## Formal Languages and Chomsky Hierarchy

Chomsky's work also transformed computer science.

The **Chomsky hierarchy** classifies formal grammars by expressive power.

This framework became foundational in:

- parsing,
- automata theory,
- compiler design.

Ideas about human language helped shape computation theory.

## Language and Mind

Chomsky's deepest contribution may be methodological.

He asked:

What kind of internal system must exist for linguistic behavior to be possible?

This moves from observed output to hidden mechanism.

The question remains central across cognitive science.

## The Mind as Generative System

The Chomskyan picture is:

finite brain,
finite rules,
unbounded structured expression.

This resembles:

- recursion,
- formal systems,
- computation.

Language becomes evidence that mind contains generative machinery.

## What Chomsky Did Not Solve

Generative grammar does not by itself explain:

- meaning,
- pragmatics,
- social use,
- language evolution,
- consciousness.

A theory of syntax is not a complete theory of language.

This limitation matters.

## Influence Beyond Linguistics

Chomsky's work helped drive the cognitive revolution.

Psychology increasingly studied:

- internal representation,
- rules,
- computation.

The mind could again be treated as a structured information-processing system.

## A Balanced View

Several Chomskyan insights remain highly influential:

- language is generative,
- structure is hierarchical,
- speakers possess implicit knowledge,
- children contribute biological capacities to learning.

More controversial are claims about exactly which grammatical principles are innate and universal.

The debate continues.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Language does not merely reflect the external world.

It also reveals something about the architecture of the organism that produces it.

To study grammar is partly to study mind.

But structure alone does not determine interpretation.

## The Next Question

Even perfectly grammatical expressions can have several interpretations.

Words are polysemous.

Sentences can have multiple parse trees.

Context resolves what grammar leaves open.

That brings us to:

**ambiguity and context.**
