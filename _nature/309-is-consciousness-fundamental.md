Physics treats some quantities as fundamental.

Examples include:

- mass,
- charge,
- spacetime structure.

What if consciousness belongs on that list?

Instead of asking:

How does consciousness emerge from nonconscious matter?

we might ask:

Is some primitive form of experience already part of reality?

This is the family of views that treat consciousness as **fundamental**.

## Fundamental Does Not Mean Simple

A fundamental property is not necessarily easy to understand.

It means the theory does not reduce it to something more basic.

Physics itself ultimately rests on primitives.

## Why Consider Fundamentality?

The motivation comes from the hard problem.

If no structural or functional account seems to entail experience, perhaps experience cannot be derived from nonexperiential facts.

It may need to be included at the foundation.

## Chalmers' View

David Chalmers has argued that consciousness may be a fundamental feature governed by psychophysical laws.

This is often called **naturalistic dualism**.

The physical world remains lawful.

Consciousness adds another fundamental property.

## Fundamental Laws

A psychophysical law would connect:

physical state

to:

experience.

For example, certain forms of information integration might correspond to certain phenomenal states.

The exact laws are unknown.

## Is This Scientific?

A fundamental theory can be scientific if it makes:

- precise,
- testable predictions.

Simply declaring consciousness fundamental is not enough.

The theory must constrain observations.

## Panpsychism

**Panpsychism** says that consciousness or proto-consciousness is widespread in nature.

At least some basic experiential aspect belongs to fundamental physical entities.

This avoids emergence from absolute zero experience.

## Panpsychism Does Not Mean Everything Thinks

A common misunderstanding is:

rocks have thoughts.

Most panpsychists do not claim ordinary objects have:

- beliefs,
- personalities.

The claim concerns primitive experiential properties.

## Microexperience

A panpsychist might say fundamental entities possess extremely simple experiential aspects.

Human consciousness arises from complex combination.

This shifts the explanatory burden.

## Combination Problem

How do many tiny experiential units combine into:

one unified consciousness?

This is the **combination problem**.

It is one of panpsychism's hardest challenges.

## Subject Combination

Suppose particles have micro-subjectivity.

Why should:

many micro-subjects

become:

one macro-subject?

Ordinary physical composition does not obviously explain subject composition.

## Unity Problem

Human experience is unified.

There is:

one visual field,
one stream.

Panpsychism must explain this unity without simply assuming it.

## Cosmopsychism

**Cosmopsychism** reverses the direction.

Instead of many micro-conscious entities combining upward, perhaps the universe as a whole has fundamental consciousness.

Individual minds derive from it.

## Decombination Problem

Cosmopsychism then faces the reverse challenge:

How does one cosmic consciousness divide into many local subjects?

The problem moves rather than disappears.

## Panprotopsychism

A weaker position says fundamental reality contains **proto-conscious** properties.

These are not experiences themselves.

But they are special properties capable of grounding consciousness.

## Does Proto-Consciousness Help?

The concern is that "proto-conscious" can become a placeholder.

Unless we know what the property is and how it yields experience, the mystery remains.

## Russellian Monism

**Russellian monism** begins from a different idea.

Physics describes matter largely through:

- relations,
- dispositions,
- causal structure.

Perhaps it does not tell us the intrinsic nature of the entities playing those roles.

## Structural Physics

Physics tells us:

what electrons do,
how they interact.

It characterizes relational structure with extraordinary precision.

But does it tell us what an electron is intrinsically?

Russellian monists say perhaps not.

## Intrinsic Nature

If physics leaves intrinsic nature open, perhaps phenomenal properties occupy that hidden side.

Then consciousness is not added to matter.

It is related to what matter intrinsically is.

## Neutral Monism

**Neutral monism** proposes that reality is fundamentally neither mental nor physical.

Mental and physical descriptions arise from a more basic neutral substrate.

This avoids choosing one side as primary.

## William James and Russell

Forms of neutral-monist thinking appear in William James and Bertrand Russell, among others.

The details differ.

The shared aim is to dissolve the mind–matter opposition.

## Two Aspects

A related family of views treats mental and physical as two aspects of one underlying reality.

From outside:

physical structure.

From inside:

experience.

This is sometimes called dual-aspect theory.

## The Appeal

These views promise to explain why mind and matter correlate so perfectly.

They are not two independent substances forced to interact.

They are two manifestations of one base.

## The Cost

The underlying neutral or dual-aspect reality can become obscure.

If we cannot specify it, the theory risks replacing one mystery with another.

## Fundamental Consciousness and Causal Closure

If experiential properties are intrinsic aspects of physical states rather than additional causes, physical causal closure may be preserved.

This is an advantage over interactionist dualism.

## Epiphenomenalism Avoided?

If consciousness is identical to or intrinsic to physical causal processes, it need not be causally idle.

The same event can have:

- physical description,
- phenomenal aspect.

This can avoid epiphenomenalism.

## Does Physics Leave Room?

Modern physics does not currently include phenomenal variables.

But a Russellian monist argues:

physics was never intended to describe intrinsic nature.

It only captures structure.

Whether this reading of physics is correct is debated.

## Structural Realism

Structural realism emphasizes that science may reveal relational structure more securely than intrinsic essence.

This provides a natural philosophical bridge to Russellian ideas.

## Information as Fundamental

Some thinkers propose **information** as a fundamental concept.

Could consciousness arise directly from informational structure?

This motivates theories such as IIT.

But information itself is not obviously experience.

## Information Is Cheap

Many systems process information:

- thermostats,
- computers,
- cells.

If information alone implies consciousness, consciousness becomes extremely widespread.

That may be acceptable to some theories.

Others see it as reductio.

## Integrated Information Theory

IIT proposes that consciousness corresponds to a system's intrinsic integrated causal structure.

It treats consciousness as fundamental in a specific theoretical sense.

The theory will receive separate treatment later.

## Panpsychism and IIT

Both can imply consciousness is more widespread than common intuition suggests.

But they are not identical.

IIT provides a formal criterion.

Panpsychism is a broader metaphysical position.

## Fundamental Field Theories

Some speculative views propose consciousness as associated with:

- fields,
- fundamental physical properties.

These ideas currently lack consensus empirical support.

They should be separated from established physics.

## Quantum Consciousness

Quantum mechanics is often invoked in consciousness discussions.

But quantum weirdness does not automatically imply consciousness.

Most consciousness theories do not require special quantum effects.

## Observer Misunderstanding

In quantum mechanics, "observer" often refers to measurement interaction, not necessarily a conscious human mind.

Consciousness is not built into standard quantum theory merely because the word observer appears.

## Quantum Collapse and Mind

Some historical interpretations have linked consciousness to wavefunction collapse.

These ideas remain controversial and are not required by mainstream quantum mechanics.

## Fundamental Consciousness and Evolution

If primitive consciousness is fundamental, evolution does not create experience from nothing.

It organizes basic experiential properties into complex forms.

This solves one problem.

It creates the combination problem.

## Evolution Still Matters

Even if experience is fundamental, the rich structure of:

- human vision,
- memory,
- self-awareness

still depends on biological evolution.

Fundamentality does not explain cognitive architecture.

## Levels of Consciousness

A panpsychist need not claim all entities have equal consciousness.

Complex systems may integrate primitive properties into vastly richer experience.

The theory must explain how.

## Emergence Still Appears

Ironically, fundamental-consciousness theories often still need emergence.

Primitive experience must become:

- unified,
- structured,
- self-aware.

The hard part moves from existence to organization.

## Ockham's Razor

Critics argue fundamental consciousness multiplies ontology.

Why add experiential properties to particles?

A physicalist prefers one physical story if it can explain everything.

## Explanatory Payoff

Defenders reply:

physicalism already leaves consciousness unexplained.

Adding a fundamental property may increase ontology but decrease explanatory mystery.

Theory choice balances simplicity and explanatory power.

## Empirical Equivalence

A major problem is that several metaphysical theories may fit the same neuroscience.

If:

physicalism,
panpsychism,
Russellian monism

predict identical observations, empirical discrimination is difficult.

## Testability Challenge

For a theory of fundamental consciousness to become scientifically powerful, it needs novel predictions.

Otherwise it remains mostly metaphysical.

## Other Minds

Fundamental-consciousness views complicate attribution.

If experience is widespread, where do meaningful subjects begin?

A rock contains matter.

Does it form a unified subject?

Most theories need an integration criterion.

## Combination vs Aggregation

A pile of conscious micro-elements does not automatically form a conscious whole.

Integration must be more than spatial proximity.

This is central.

## Borders of a Subject

Why is your consciousness associated with:

your brain

and not:

you plus the chair?

A theory must explain subject boundaries.

This is difficult for widespread-consciousness views.

## Causal Integration

One answer is that subject boundaries follow intrinsic causal integration.

Systems with stronger internal causal unity form subjects.

This is one motivation behind IIT-like approaches.

## The Exclusion Problem

If small subsystems and larger systems all have consciousness, which one is the real subject?

Some theories impose exclusion principles.

These are controversial.

## Fundamental Does Not Mean Mystical

A property can be fundamental without being supernatural.

Charge is fundamental in physics.

A consciousness fundamentalist can aim for naturalistic laws.

The issue is explanatory structure, not mysticism.

## The Risk of Semantic Inflation

If we call every physical entity conscious, the word may lose its ordinary meaning.

The theory must distinguish:

primitive experiential property

from:

rich consciousness.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Treating consciousness as fundamental avoids the problem of deriving experience from something wholly nonexperiential.

But it inherits new questions:

- combination,
- subject boundaries,
- testability.

There is no free metaphysical solution.

## The Next Question

Perhaps consciousness is neither a new emergent substance nor a fundamental property.

Another possibility is more radical:

Maybe what we think consciousness is does not exist in the way it appears.

That leads to:

**Is Consciousness an Illusion?**
