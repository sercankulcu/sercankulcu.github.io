Language feels so familiar that it is easy to underestimate how strange it is.

A finite organism learns a finite vocabulary and a finite set of structural regularities.

Then it produces and understands sentences it has never encountered before.

Language links:

- sound,
- gesture,
- symbols,
- structure,
- meaning,
- social intention.

Before asking how language works, we must first separate it from things it is often confused with.

## Language Is Not Speech

Speech is one way of realizing language.

Sign languages are full human languages.

They use:

- hand shape,
- movement,
- location,
- facial expression.

Language does not require sound.

## Language Is Not Writing

Writing is a technology for representing language.

Humans spoke languages long before writing systems appeared.

Many languages have existed without standardized scripts.

Writing preserves and externalizes language.

It is not language itself.

## Language Is Not an Alphabet

An alphabet is a set of written symbols.

Language includes much more:

- vocabulary,
- grammar,
- meaning,
- pragmatic use.

Two languages can use the same alphabet.

One language can be written in different scripts.

## Language Is Not Simply Communication

Animals communicate.

Cells signal.

Computers exchange messages.

Communication alone does not establish human-like language.

Language has distinctive forms of:

- generativity,
- compositionality,
- abstraction,
- social convention.

## Communication Without Language

A fire alarm communicates danger.

A bee dance communicates information.

A traffic light communicates instruction.

These systems are meaningful in broad senses.

They do not necessarily have open-ended grammar.

## Language Is Not Thought

We often think in words.

But not all thought appears linguistic.

Visual imagery.

Spatial reasoning.

Motor planning.

Emotional evaluation.

Infants and nonhuman animals can solve problems without human language.

Thought and language overlap.

They are not identical.

## Language Can Shape Thought

Although language is not thought, it can influence cognition.

Words provide categories.

Grammar highlights relations.

External language supports memory and reasoning.

The strong claim that language completely determines thought is difficult to defend.

Weaker influences are well supported.

## Language Is Not a Dictionary

Knowing words is not enough.

A list of vocabulary does not tell us how to form:

- questions,
- negation,
- relative clauses,
- tense.

Language requires structure.

## Grammar

**Grammar** is the system of regularities governing how linguistic elements combine and change.

It includes more than classroom rules about correctness.

Linguistic grammar describes the implicit knowledge speakers use.

## Descriptive vs Prescriptive Grammar

### Descriptive grammar
Studies how speakers actually use language.

### Prescriptive grammar
Recommends how language should be used under a standard.

Both have roles.

They answer different questions.

## Language Is Structured

Consider:

"Dog bites man."

"Man bites dog."

The same words appear.

Order changes meaning.

Language depends on relationships among elements.

Sequence alone is not always enough.

Hierarchy also matters.

## Hierarchy

A sentence contains phrases.

Phrases contain smaller units.

This hierarchical organization explains:

- ambiguity,
- long-distance dependency,
- recursive embedding.

Language is not a flat chain of words.

## Morphology

**Morphology** studies the internal structure of words.

For example:

un-help-ful-ness

contains smaller meaningful units.

Languages vary enormously in how much grammatical information they place inside words.

## Syntax

**Syntax** studies how words and phrases combine.

It addresses questions such as:

Which word modifies which?

Which noun is the subject?

What can be embedded where?

Syntax provides structural organization.

## Semantics

**Semantics** studies conventional meaning.

What does a word denote?

What proposition does a sentence express?

How do meanings combine?

Syntax without semantics is structure without interpretation.

## Pragmatics

**Pragmatics** studies meaning in use.

"Can you open the door?"

may function as a request rather than a literal inquiry about ability.

Human communication depends heavily on inference and context.

## Phonology

**Phonology** studies how languages organize sound patterns.

It is not merely acoustics.

Languages classify and contrast sounds differently.

The same physical sound difference may matter in one language and not another.

## Sign-Language Phonology

Sign languages also have phonological structure.

Their basic contrasts involve features such as:

- hand shape,
- location,
- movement.

This reinforces that phonology concerns structured form, not necessarily sound.

## Lexicon

The **lexicon** is a speaker's mental inventory of words and stored linguistic units.

But words are not isolated dictionary entries.

They contain information about:

- meaning,
- pronunciation,
- grammatical behavior.

## Language Is Productive

Speakers constantly create novel utterances.

You can understand:

"The purple robot quietly borrowed my umbrella."

even if you have never heard it before.

Language is generative.

## Finite Rules, Unbounded Expression

This connects directly to recursion.

A finite grammar can support indefinitely many expressions.

Human performance is finite.

The structural system has no simple fixed sentence list.

## Compositionality

We understand new sentences partly because meaning is compositional.

Meaning of the whole depends on:

- meanings of parts,
- how they are combined.

This is one reason grammar matters to semantics.

## Conventionality

Most linguistic signs are conventional.

English says:

"dog."

Turkish says:

"köpek."

No physical necessity connects one sound pattern to the animal.

Communities stabilize arbitrary mappings.

## Language Changes

Languages are historical systems.

Pronunciation changes.

Meanings shift.

Grammar evolves.

Words appear and disappear.

There is no permanently frozen language.

## Dialects

A language contains variation.

Regional.

Social.

Generational.

What counts as "one language" versus "different languages" is not always determined purely by linguistic structure.

Politics and identity can matter.

## Language Is Social

No individual invents ordinary language alone.

Speakers inherit conventions from communities.

Meaning depends on shared practice.

Language is simultaneously:

- cognitive,
- biological,
- cultural.

## Language Is Embodied

Language is produced and perceived through bodies.

Speech uses:

- lungs,
- vocal tract,
- hearing.

Sign uses:

- hands,
- vision,
- space.

Language is abstractly structured but physically embodied.

## Language Is Learned

Children acquire enormous linguistic competence with limited explicit instruction.

They infer patterns from usage and interaction.

How they do this remains one of cognitive science's major questions.

## Language and Innateness

Some theories emphasize innate grammatical structure.

Others emphasize:

- statistical learning,
- usage,
- social cognition.

The debate is not simply nature versus nurture.

Biological capacities and experience interact.

## Language Is Not Necessarily Unique in Every Component

Animals can show:

- symbolic learning,
- vocal learning,
- turn-taking,
- reference-like communication.

The distinctive power of human language may arise from a combination of capacities rather than one magical feature.

## Language and Machines

Computers can generate fluent text.

Does that mean they possess language in the human sense?

They clearly manipulate linguistic structure.

Whether this amounts to:

- understanding,
- intention,
- grounded semantics

is a deeper question.

We will return to it.

## Language as Representation

Language allows us to represent what is:

- absent,
- hypothetical,
- impossible,
- fictional,
- abstract.

We can discuss tomorrow.

Dinosaurs.

Infinity.

Justice.

Language detaches representation from the immediate environment.

## Language as Action

Language does not only describe the world.

It changes it.

Promises.

Orders.

Questions.

Declarations.

Laws.

Speech can become social action.

## Language as a Bridge

Language connects:

- private cognition,
- public symbols,
- shared culture.

It makes thoughts transmissible.

But the transmission is never perfect.

Interpretation remains necessary.

## The Next Question

If language and thought are not identical, how far can they come apart?

Can a creature think without words?

Can concepts exist before language?

Does language merely express thought, or does it help create new forms of thought?

The next question is:

**Can thought exist without language?**
