A visual state can guide behavior.

It can enter memory.

It can be reported.

But is that the same thing as:

having an experience?

Ned Block's distinction between **access consciousness** and **phenomenal consciousness** was designed to separate these questions.

## Access Consciousness

A representation is **access-conscious** when it is available for:

- reasoning,
- report,
- decision,
- voluntary action.

The idea is functional.

It concerns what information can do.

## Phenomenal Consciousness

A state is **phenomenally conscious** when there is:

something it is like

to be in that state.

This concerns experiential character.

## A-Consciousness and P-Consciousness

Block often abbreviates:

- A-consciousness,
- P-consciousness.

These are theoretical distinctions.

They are not separate substances.

## Why Separate Them?

Many theories explain:

- information access,
- reportability.

Critics argue this may leave phenomenal experience unexplained.

The distinction prevents one from silently replacing the other.

## Example

Suppose a visual signal influences:

- verbal answer,
- planning.

It is clearly access-conscious under a functional definition.

But whether such access fully constitutes experience is the disputed point.

## Blindsight

Blindsight provides an important case.

Some patients report:

"I see nothing"

in part of the visual field.

Yet they can guess stimulus properties above chance.

## What Blindsight Shows

Visual information can influence behavior without ordinary conscious access.

This separates:

processing

from:

normal conscious seeing.

## Is Blindsight Phenomenally Empty?

Some patients report vague sensations or feelings of presence.

Blindsight may not be a single uniform phenomenon.

Careful case distinctions matter.

## Access Without Phenomenology?

A philosophical possibility is:

information is fully available for reasoning and report,

yet:

there is no experience.

This would be A-consciousness without P-consciousness.

Zombies are an extreme version.

## Phenomenology Without Access?

The reverse possibility is:

there is rich experience

that exceeds what can be reported or used cognitively.

Block has argued that this may occur in perception.

This is more empirically controversial.

## Overflow

The **overflow hypothesis** says phenomenal consciousness can contain more detail than cognitive access can hold.

Experience overflows:

- working memory,
- report.

## Sperling Again

In Sperling's iconic-memory experiments, participants briefly see an array of letters.

They report only a few.

But when cued immediately afterward, they can report a selected row.

This suggests short-lived availability of more information.

## Does Sperling Show Phenomenal Overflow?

One interpretation:

participants consciously experience the whole array.

Access limitations prevent full report.

Another interpretation:

the extra information is high-capacity sensory memory but not fully conscious.

The experiment does not settle the philosophy alone.

## Change Blindness

People can miss large changes in scenes.

This weakens naive claims of richly detailed visual consciousness.

Perhaps the visual world feels more complete than what is actually represented.

## Inattentional Blindness

People can fail to notice visible stimuli when attention is elsewhere.

This links conscious access strongly to attentional selection.

But whether unattended stimuli are phenomenally represented remains debated.

## Partial Report

Experiments using partial-report methods attempt to estimate how much information was available before memory bottlenecks.

Measurement itself can alter the system.

Consciousness science is methodologically delicate.

## Reportability

Access consciousness is often measured through report.

But report requires:

- language,
- decision,
- motor output.

A theory of consciousness should not automatically identify report mechanism with consciousness itself.

## No-Report Paradigms

No-report paradigms infer conscious content from:

- eye movements,
- pupil changes,
- neural markers.

They aim to reduce confounding from report.

Interpretation remains controversial.

## Global Availability

Access consciousness naturally fits the idea of global availability.

Information becomes usable by:

- memory,
- language,
- planning.

This is central to Global Workspace theories.

## Broadcasting

A local perceptual representation may become globally broadcast.

Once broadcast, many systems can use it.

This creates flexible control.

## Why Access Matters

Access-conscious information supports:

- deliberate reasoning,
- novel action,
- report.

These are important functions.

Even critics of functionalism accept their scientific significance.

## Why Phenomenology Matters

The philosophical challenge is that these functions may not seem to explain:

what-it-is-like.

Phenomenal consciousness keeps the hard problem visible.

## Block's Motivation

Block introduced the distinction partly to resist theories that define consciousness entirely by cognitive accessibility.

He wanted to preserve the possibility that experience exceeds access.

## Functionalist Response

A functionalist may argue:

all meaningful facts about consciousness are captured by the relevant functional organization.

Then P-consciousness without access becomes doubtful.

The distinction may be conceptual rather than ontological.

## Phenomenal Realist Response

A phenomenal realist says:

access describes consequences of experience.

It does not define experience itself.

The difference is real.

## Cognitive Accessibility

Scientists often operationalize consciousness through what subjects can:

- discriminate,
- report,
- use.

This makes access scientifically convenient.

Phenomenology is harder to measure.

## Methodological Risk

Because access is easier to measure, researchers may accidentally study only access while claiming to study consciousness in full.

Operational convenience can shape theory.

## Neural Correlates

Suppose neural pattern X predicts reported awareness.

Does X correlate with:

- experience,
- access,
- report preparation?

Separating these possibilities is essential.

## Prefrontal Cortex Debate

Some consciousness theories emphasize prefrontal involvement in conscious access.

Others argue frontal activity may reflect:

- reporting,
- task demands.

No-report studies are partly motivated by this debate.

## Posterior Hot Zone

Some researchers emphasize posterior cortical regions as especially relevant to conscious content.

This contrasts with strongly frontally centered accounts.

The evidence remains actively debated.

## Attention vs Access

Attention prioritizes processing.

Access makes content available to flexible cognition.

They often interact.

They are not identical concepts.

## Working Memory vs Access

Working memory stores information temporarily for use.

Access consciousness may involve working memory.

But some conscious contents may not be maintained there.

## Automatic Access

Some information can influence action quickly without deliberate reflection.

Does this count as access-conscious?

Definitions vary.

Functional categories require precision.

## Recurrent Processing

Some theories argue local recurrent processing within sensory cortex may be sufficient for phenomenal consciousness.

Global access may come later.

This offers a direct empirical version of P-before-A.

## Feedforward Processing

A stimulus can propagate feedforward through visual pathways without entering conscious experience.

Recurrent interactions may correlate more strongly with awareness.

This is one influential hypothesis.

## Local vs Global Recurrence

Not all recurrent processing is global.

Local sensory loops may support one level.

Global broadcasting may support another.

This creates layered theories.

## Access in Machines

Artificial systems can clearly implement access-like functions.

Information can be:

- stored,
- broadcast,
- used by planning.

This does not settle machine phenomenology.

## LLM Context as Access?

A language model's context makes tokens available to computation.

That is a kind of informational access.

But calling it conscious access would stretch the term unless additional conditions are justified.

## Functional Analogy Requires Caution

Two systems can share an abstract operation:

broadcast information.

That does not prove they share:

subjective experience.

The metaphysical inference needs argument.

## Phenomenology in Nonverbal Animals

Animals may have rich experience without language-based report.

This is one reason access cannot simply mean:

verbal reportability.

Behavioral and neural indicators must be broader.

## Infants

Infants likely have phenomenal experience before mature:

- language,
- executive access.

Again, report cannot define consciousness universally.

## Dreams

Dreams can contain vivid phenomenology with reduced:

- external action,
- sensory input.

Access structures are altered.

Consciousness persists.

## Locked-In Syndrome

A person can be fully conscious while unable to move most muscles.

Behavioral output can collapse while access and phenomenology remain.

This is a powerful warning against equating consciousness with overt action.

## Covert Consciousness

Some patients classified behaviorally as unresponsive can follow instructions through patterns of brain activity.

Access may exist without ordinary motor report.

Measurement channels matter.

## Phenomenal Structure

P-consciousness includes:

- color,
- sound,
- pain,
- bodily feeling.

These contents have qualitative organization.

A theory must account for that structure.

## Access Structure

A-consciousness includes:

- availability,
- flexibility,
- cross-system influence.

These properties are computationally tractable.

The two structures may correlate strongly without being identical.

## Could They Always Coincide?

Perhaps in normal adult humans, access and phenomenology usually co-occur.

The distinction still matters because theory asks whether one explains the other.

Conceptual separation can reveal hidden assumptions.

## Scientific Strategy

One productive strategy is to study access mechanisms rigorously while remaining explicit about whether the theory claims more.

This avoids both:

- metaphysical inflation,
- scientific nihilism.

## The Philosophical Lesson

Access consciousness asks:

What information is available to the system?

Phenomenal consciousness asks:

What does the system experience?

They may be:

- identical,
- tightly coupled,
- partially dissociable.

The debate remains open.

## The Next Question

If conscious experience depends on physical brains, perhaps the brain **produces** consciousness.

But what does "produce" mean here?

Is consciousness:

- caused by,
- realized by,
- identical to

neural activity?

The next essay asks:

**Does the Brain Produce Consciousness?**
