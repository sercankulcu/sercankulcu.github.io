---
title: "The Coworker Whose After-Work Stories I Couldn't Write Down"
date: 2010-04-16
tags:

On 16 April 2010, I wrote about a coworker on the exact day our work relationship ended.

His name was Melih Göksel.

The entry is not long.

It does not explain where he was going.

It does not say how long we had worked together.

It does not preserve a farewell speech, a final handshake, or the last thing we said to each other.

Instead, it does something more useful.

It preserves the details that had become Melih-shaped in my mind.

He was strangely proud of being from Eskişehir and apparently mentioned it often.

He was handsome — emphatically so, according to the younger version of me.

We expected to attend his wedding the following summer.

He was hardworking.

I hoped to see him become a director one day.

And then there was the best line:

I could not write what we had done after work because I did not want it used as evidence against him.

That sentence contains more friendship than a formal tribute ever could.

There are people you know only through work.

You know their desk.

Their email style.

Their way of answering the phone.

Their role in the project.

You may sit near them for years and still know almost nothing about the person who leaves the building in the evening.

Then there are coworkers whose identities refuse to stay inside office hours.

Work becomes the place where you met.

Not the full relationship.

The after-hours version begins to matter.

That seems to have happened with Melih.

The archive tells me almost nothing about those evenings, deliberately.

That is important.

There is a difference between missing information and protected information.

The entry does not forget.

It withholds.

I knew the stories.

I simply chose not to preserve them in public.

That creates a funny paradox.

The unwritten part becomes one of the most memorable parts.

If I had described every night in detail, perhaps those anecdotes would now compete with one another.

Instead, the archive says:

There were stories.

They were good enough to mention.

And dangerous enough, at least jokingly, not to document.

That is all.

Sometimes discretion improves memory.

The blank space acquires personality.

This is especially true of friendships formed around work.

Office friendships exist in two parallel worlds.

During the day, everyone performs a role.

Engineer.

Manager.

Intern.

Specialist.

Customer.

Project member.

There are deadlines.

Documents.

Meetings.

Tasks.

Status.

Then the workday ends.

The badge still exists, but it matters less.

People eat.

Drink tea.

Go somewhere.

Laugh differently.

Complain more honestly.

Tell stories that would never appear in a meeting.

Hierarchy softens.

The person behind the title becomes visible.

The old entry suggests that Melih belonged comfortably to both worlds.

I called him hardworking.

That is the professional judgment.

I refused to document what happened after work.

That is the personal judgment.

Together they create a fuller person.

This matters because workplace memory becomes distorted over time if we preserve only professional qualities.

We say:

He was a good engineer.

She was very organized.

He was reliable.

She worked hard.

All useful.

All terribly incomplete.

Nobody remembers a friend because their Jira tickets were well maintained.

We remember the strange phrase they repeated.

The city they would not stop praising.

The song attached to them.

The joke everyone understood.

The future everyone assumed they would have.

Melih’s city was Eskişehir.

Apparently he was proud of being from there for reasons I found slightly excessive or at least amusing.

I wrote that I did not know why he was so proud of it, but he said it constantly.

That detail is wonderfully ordinary.

People attach themselves to places in ways that are difficult to explain.

Some carry their hometown almost as a second surname.

Mention food.

They compare it with home.

Mention weather.

Home was better.

Mention traffic.

Home was different.

Mention people.

You know how people are in our city...

The city travels with them.

Even when they do not.

Perhaps Melih did this with Eskişehir.

The source does not give me examples, so I should not invent them.

It only tells me that his pride was noticeable enough to become part of the description.

That means it was part of how people around him recognized him.

This is how personality works in groups.

A person repeats something enough and eventually the group gives it back to them as identity.

You are the coffee person.

The Beşiktaş person.

The one who is always cold.

The one who knows every restaurant.

The one from Eskişehir.

Nobody formally assigns these roles.

They emerge.

Then years later, one of those tiny unofficial identities may survive longer than the person's job title.

I no longer need to know what Melih’s exact professional responsibilities were in April 2010 to recognize him through the entry.

Eskişehir.

Hardworking.

Future wedding.

Future director.

Unpublishable evenings.

There he is.

This is one reason I increasingly distrust formal biographies as a way of preserving people.

Formal biographies are excellent at dates.

Education.

Positions.

Titles.

Achievements.

They are bad at texture.

“Worked as an engineer from X to Y.”

Fine.

Did he make the office better?

Did people want to spend time with him after work?

What did everyone tease him about?

What future did his friends imagine for him?

These questions belong to the unofficial record.

Personal writing catches them accidentally.

The line about wanting to see him as a director says something else.

It is praise, but not merely praise.

It is a prediction shaped by affection.

When we like competent people early in their careers, we begin imagining their future before they reach it.

You’ll be a manager one day.

You’ll run this place.

You’ll become a professor.

You’ll start your own company.

Sometimes these predictions are serious.

Sometimes they are jokes.

Often both.

They reveal that we see momentum in someone.

I apparently saw it in Melih.

The word I used was hardworking.

That is the only professional quality the source explicitly gives me.

So I will not decorate it with invented brilliance, leadership anecdotes, or specific successes.

Hardworking is enough.

It supports the hope.

Maybe one day director.

The entry also expected a wedding the next summer.

There is tenderness in writing about someone’s future in this casual way.

“We’ll do his wedding next summer.”

It assumes continuity.

Of course we will still know one another.

Of course we will be there.

Of course this next event already belongs to the shared calendar.

Young adulthood often feels like this.

Friends are not static people.

Everyone is in motion.

One is changing jobs.

One is getting married.

One is moving.

One is entering military service.

One is finishing school.

One is thinking about buying a car.

People are always becoming something.

Friendship means watching several futures develop at once.

Workplaces intensify this because careers add another timeline.

Today colleague.

Tomorrow former colleague.

Later manager somewhere else.

Director, perhaps.

People pass through companies, but the friendships formed there may continue in imagination even when daily contact ends.

That is why the phrase “our work friendship ended as of now” feels slightly formal and slightly sad.

Not friendship.

Work friendship.

The distinction matters.

A shared workplace creates automatic contact.

You do not need to plan to see the person.

They are simply there.

Morning.

Lunch.

Afternoon.

Every weekday.

Then one person leaves.

Suddenly a relationship that required no planning requires planning.

This is the moment when many adult friendships weaken.

Not because anyone stopped caring.

Because structure disappeared.

Work had been doing invisible social labor.

It placed two people in the same building over and over again.

Remove the building and friendship must create its own logistics.

Call.

Meet.

Arrange.

Remember.

Adult life is full of relationships lost not through conflict but through failed scheduling.

That is one of the quieter forms of separation.

A colleague leaves.

Everyone says:

We’ll still meet.

Sometimes they do.

Sometimes “we’ll meet” becomes one of those sentences said sincerely and fulfilled rarely.

The source does not tell me what happened with Melih afterward.

So I will not invent reunion or distance.

The entry freezes the relationship at the threshold.

Today, our work relationship ends.

That is enough.

Thresholds are good moments for writing because attention sharpens.

When something is about to disappear from routine, ordinary details become visible.

The way a person talks.

The thing they repeat.

Their desk.

Their jokes.

Their habits.

You may have ignored these for months.

Then they say they are leaving.

Suddenly the brain starts taking inventory.

That may explain why farewell writing often contains oddly specific details.

We notice because repetition is ending.

The last ordinary day is never ordinary once you know it is the last.

This is true far beyond work.

Last day at school.

Last night in an apartment.

Last drive in an old car.

Last coffee with someone before they move.

The object or person has not changed.

Our awareness has.

Endings improve eyesight.

The Melih entry looks like that kind of inventory.

What do I want to preserve?

Eskişehir.

Looks.

Wedding.

Work ethic.

Possible future.

And a sealed envelope marked:

after work.

I also dedicated a Serdar Ortaç song to him.

The original entry references it directly.

I do not need to reproduce lyrics.

The important part is the gesture.

Music is another way groups label people.

A song becomes attached to a colleague because of a joke, an event, a resemblance, a habit, or no defensible reason at all.

Then hearing it later can resurrect a whole period.

This is one of music’s most efficient forms of memory.

Songs do not only preserve emotions.

They preserve social context.

A song can mean:

that office.

that person.

that summer.

that drive.

that stupid joke.

The recording remains identical while its private meaning changes completely.

I do not know exactly why that particular song belonged to Melih.

The archive does not explain.

Good.

Not every joke needs archaeological reconstruction.

Sometimes the association itself is enough.

This restraint matters in memory writing.

There is a temptation to make every detail meaningful.

Why Eskişehir?

What did it symbolize?

Why that song?

What deeper truth did it reveal?

Maybe none.

People are not novels written by a careful author.

Some details exist because life is messy.

A coworker talks about his hometown too much.

Friends attach a song to him.

Everyone laughs.

That is life.

Not every object needs metaphor.

The strongest line remains the refusal to write down what happened after work.

It is funny, but it also shows a kind of loyalty.

Friendship creates archives and anti-archives.

Things we preserve.

Things we deliberately do not preserve.

There is maturity in knowing the difference.

Not every good story belongs online.

Not every funny detail should be public.

Not every memory becomes better when named.

In an era when people increasingly document everything — meals, trips, conversations, photographs, locations — forgetting to record something can feel like loss.

But some experiences are protected by remaining between the people who were there.

Their value does not depend on an audience.

Perhaps that is what the joke accidentally preserves.

There are stories from those evenings.

They are ours.

The archive can know that they exist.

It does not get to know what they are.

That boundary feels healthy.

It also gives Melih a strangely dignified place in the archive.

Many entries expose embarrassing details about friends.

His says, in effect:

I have material.

I am choosing not to use it.

That is affection disguised as blackmail.

A very recognizable male friendship language.

I could ruin you.

Therefore I love you.

Not literally, of course.

The humor depends on harmless exaggeration.

The underlying message is:

We have history outside work.

Enough history to joke about evidence.

That is richer than saying “we were close.”

Again, specificity wins.

I also like that the entry praises his appearance without embarrassment.

“Handsome, very handsome.”

Male friends often compliment one another through mockery, exaggeration, or suspiciously theatrical language because direct affection can feel too exposed.

The entry moves naturally between teasing and praise.

Why are you so proud of Eskişehir?

Handsome bastard.

Hardworking man.

Future director.

Future groom.

Cannot publish the rest.

It is a compact portrait built from affection that refuses to sound solemn.

That feels right for the age.

Young men often hide warmth inside jokes because jokes provide cover.

You can say something sincere as long as you immediately damage the sincerity a little.

This is not always ideal communication.

But it has a recognizable emotional grammar.

The entry is full of it.

The important thing is that the admiration gets through.

Melih mattered.

Enough to write about on the day the workplace stopped placing us together.

That alone distinguishes him from hundreds of colleagues who pass through a career and leave no sentence behind.

Work is strange in this way.

We spend enormous portions of life with people we did not choose.

Hiring chose them.

Projects chose them.

Departments chose them.

Office geography chose them.

Then, sometimes, we choose them afterward.

A colleague becomes a friend.

The institutional accident produces a personal relationship.

This may be one of the best things work gives us.

Not salary.

Not title.

Not professional development.

People we would never otherwise have met.

And then one of them leaves.

The institutional relationship ends.

The personal memory remains.

Years later, the project may be impossible to reconstruct.

What exactly were we delivering?

Which deadline?

Which customer?

Which version?

Who remembers?

But the colleague still has a shape.

Eskişehir.

Wedding.

Director.

After-work evidence.

Maybe this is why workplace nostalgia rarely focuses on the work itself.

People say they miss an old job when what they often miss is an ecosystem.

Lunch with certain people.

Coffee at a certain time.

A balcony.

A joke repeated too often.

Someone sending cartoons.

Someone insisting on a hometown.

The work was the scaffolding around a social world.

When the job ends, the scaffolding disappears.

Memory keeps a few people standing.

I have several such people in the archive.

That is probably not accidental.

Projects age badly.

People age well in stories.

Technical details become obsolete.

A friend remains funny.

A manager remains strange.

An intern remains capable of bringing playing cards to work.

A colleague remains the man whose after-work stories cannot safely be entered into evidence.

This is the kind of workplace history I want to preserve.

Not only what the company built.

What the people were like while building it.

On 16 April 2010, Melih Göksel stopped being my coworker.

That was the formal event.

The informal record is better.

He was proud of Eskişehir.

He was hardworking.

He was handsome enough that apparently I felt repetition was necessary.

We expected a wedding next summer.

I wanted to see him become a director one day.

And there were things we did after work that I considered better left unwritten.

Some friendships deserve detailed memoirs.

Others can survive beautifully inside one warning:

I know the stories.

I’m just not putting them in writing.
