---
title: "The Day I Said 'Come On' Instead of 'Come In'"
date: 2010-07-26
tags:

After three months in Canada, I should have been able to invite someone into a room.

This is not advanced English.

Nobody expects Shakespeare.

No phrasal verbs.

No complicated tense.

No technical vocabulary.

A person is standing at the door.

You want them to enter.

You say:

"Come in."

Simple.

Except that one day, when a woman came to my door to ask a few questions for some kind of survey or statistical study, my brain produced something else.

I invited her inside.

Or at least I tried.

"Come on! Come on!"

That is what came out.

Not once.

Twice.

Come on.

Come on.

There are mistakes you realize after the conversation.

There are mistakes you realize at the end of the sentence.

And then there are mistakes where the meaning arrives in your head half a second after the words leave your mouth, just early enough for you to understand what you have done and just late enough to stop it.

This was one of those.

I had meant:

Please come inside.

What I had said sounded much more like:

Come on.

Go ahead.

Move.

Let's do this.

The exact tone probably depended on my face, my hand gesture, and how confidently I delivered the disaster.

Fortunately, language errors rarely travel alone.

The woman answered something.

What I heard was:

"I am married."

Excellent.

Now the scene in my head was complete.

I had apparently invited a woman into my apartment with an enthusiastic "Come on, come on," and she had immediately informed me that she was married.

For one brief moment, I thought she had understood my intentions faster than I had.

The misunderstanding was beautiful.

I had no intentions.

I barely had English.

That old entry still makes me laugh because it captures what language learning actually feels like better than many textbooks do.

You can study vocabulary for years.

You can understand grammar.

You can read technical documents.

You can pass exams.

Then somebody appears unexpectedly at your door and suddenly the entire language disappears.

The brain opens every drawer.

Nothing useful is inside.

You know the phrase.

You have heard it hundreds of times.

"Come in."

It is almost impossible not to know it.

But knowing something and retrieving it at the exact second you need it are different skills.

This is one of the great humiliations of speaking a foreign language.

Your intelligence remains intact.

Your personality remains intact.

Your vocabulary, however, may temporarily become that of a confused child.

Inside your head, the thought is perfectly normal:

Please come inside. Sit down. What would you like to ask?

Outside your head:

Come on!

The gap can be painful.

It can also be extremely funny once enough time has passed.

Living abroad accelerates this process because there is no escape.

In a classroom, you can prepare.

The subject is known.

Today we practice ordering food.

Tomorrow we practice asking for directions.

The teacher asks a predictable question.

You construct a respectable answer.

Real life does not follow the unit plan.

The doorbell rings.

A stranger speaks.

The cashier asks something you have never heard before.

Someone makes a joke.

A bus driver says a sentence at full speed.

The person on the phone has an accent you were not warned about.

Suddenly language is not a school subject.

It is infrastructure.

If it fails, the day becomes more difficult.

That is when you begin learning differently.

You stop asking:

What is the correct English sentence?

You start asking:

How do I make this person understand me?

That is a much more useful question.

Sometimes the answer is elegant English.

Sometimes it is broken English plus hand movements.

Sometimes it is repeating a noun three times.

Sometimes both people give up and point.

Communication is not an exam.

If the meaning survives, the system worked.

The problem is that pride does not always agree.

When you are educated, especially when your work depends heavily on thinking, it can be surprisingly uncomfortable to sound less intelligent than you are.

A foreign language temporarily changes your social resolution.

You may have complex opinions.

You may know exactly what is wrong with a technical system.

You may be capable of explaining a difficult concept beautifully in your native language.

Then someone asks:

"What did you do last weekend?"

And you answer like a malfunctioning robot.

"I go... went... there. With friend. It was good."

This is humbling.

The temptation is to remain quiet until your language becomes good enough.

That is one of the worst strategies.

Speaking improves by speaking badly.

There is no secret dignified route around the embarrassing phase.

You make mistakes.

People misunderstand.

You misunderstand them.

You learn phrases you somehow never learned in years of formal education.

The language becomes attached to moments.

That is why I have forgotten countless vocabulary lists but still remember "come in" and "come on."

Emotion is a powerful indexing system.

Embarrassment especially.

A word learned from a book may disappear.

A word learned because you accidentally created a romantic misunderstanding at your own front door becomes permanent.

This is probably why people remember their foreign-language mistakes so vividly.

The mistake is not only linguistic.

It becomes a scene.

Where you were.

Who was there.

What you meant.

What they understood.

What you thought they understood.

The delayed realization.

The desire to rewind five seconds of reality.

Language becomes memory.

There is also something important in being misunderstood and surviving it.

Before speaking, we often imagine communication errors as disasters.

What if I say the wrong thing?

What if my pronunciation is bad?

What if they laugh?

What if I do not understand?

Then the error happens.

And mostly, nothing terrible follows.

The person asks again.

You correct yourself.

Both sides laugh.

The conversation continues.

You survive.

This changes your relationship with mistakes.

The first goal in language learning is usually correctness.

Eventually the more important goal becomes resilience.

Can the conversation continue after a mistake?

Can you repair meaning?

Can you say the same thing another way?

Can you admit that you did not understand?

These skills matter much more in real life than constructing one perfect sentence.

Native speakers repair conversations constantly too.

They mishear.

Interrupt.

Clarify.

Ask what someone means.

Change wording.

Communication has error correction built into it.

Foreign speakers sometimes forget this because school teaches language as if every sentence is submitted for grading.

Real conversation is much closer to networking.

Packets get lost.

You resend.

If necessary, use another route.

Eventually the message arrives.

The Canadian door incident also taught me something about confidence.

Confidence in a foreign language is strange.

Too little confidence and you never speak.

Too much confidence and you stand at a doorway saying "Come on! Come on!" with the authority of someone who has absolutely no idea what he is doing.

The ideal state is somewhere between.

Speak.

But keep listening to yourself.

Be willing to correct.

Do not assume the first interpretation is right.

That last part applies to listening as well.

The woman's response sounded to me like "I am married."

Was that actually what she said?

Maybe.

Maybe not.

My original entry treated it as something I had understood that way in the moment.

And that is what matters.

Once my brain decided she had said she was married, it instantly constructed a logical story:

Of course.

She thinks I am hitting on her.

Because I said "come on."

Everything fits.

Brains love a coherent explanation.

Even when the explanation is nonsense.

Language barriers make this worse because uncertainty invites invention.

You hear 70 percent of the sentence.

Your brain supplies the other 30.

Usually this works.

Sometimes the missing 30 percent changes everything.

This is another lesson that later became useful far beyond language learning.

Do not become too attached to the first interpretation that makes sense.

Especially when information is incomplete.

Ask.

Check.

Repeat.

Engineers do this with data.

People should probably do it more often with each other.

The funniest part is that by then I had already been in Canada for three months.

Three months feels long enough that you expect improvement.

And there had been improvement.

That is what makes mistakes at that stage more surprising.

At the beginning, every mistake is expected.

You are new.

Of course you misunderstand.

After a while, confidence rises.

Then an elementary phrase betrays you.

This happens in technical work too.

Beginners make beginner mistakes.

Experts also make beginner mistakes.

Experience reduces them.

It does not repeal the possibility.

Sometimes the mistake is so simple that it becomes more embarrassing precisely because you know better.

You stare at the result and think:

I know this.

Yes.

You do.

You still made the mistake.

Knowledge is not immunity.

That is a useful lesson for anyone who teaches.

Students sometimes make errors in concepts they clearly understand.

A wrong answer does not always mean the knowledge is absent.

Retrieval fails.

Attention fails.

Context changes.

Pressure interferes.

The mind is not a database query returning deterministic results.

I think I became more forgiving of this over time.

Both in myself and in other people.

You can know something and still fail to produce it at the right moment.

The solution is not necessarily more shame.

Usually it is more practice in varied situations.

Use the knowledge until retrieval becomes automatic.

That is how "come in" eventually becomes impossible to confuse.

Not because you memorized it harder.

Because you used it enough times that the phrase moved from knowledge to reflex.

Fluency is full of these reflexes.

You stop translating.

You stop constructing.

The phrase arrives before analysis.

That is why living in another language changes you gradually and then suddenly.

For weeks, everything requires effort.

Then one day you answer without translating.

You understand a joke immediately.

You notice you have been listening for ten minutes without thinking about the language itself.

The tool becomes transparent.

Those moments are satisfying.

But the embarrassing moments are often more educational.

Nobody tells stories about the Tuesday when they correctly said "come in."

Correct language disappears into the conversation.

Mistakes become stories.

So I am grateful for that one.

A woman came to the door.

I had been in Canada for three months.

I knew enough English to know better.

I opened the door.

I wanted to invite her inside.

And with complete confidence, I said:

Come on.

Come on.

For a few seconds, I apparently transformed a statistical survey into a proposal she needed to reject by announcing that she was married.

Then life continued.

My English survived.

So did I.

And I never confused "come in" with "come on" again.

Some lessons are taught by teachers.

Some by books.

The memorable ones occasionally ring your doorbell.
