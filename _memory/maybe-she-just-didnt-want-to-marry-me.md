title: "Maybe She Just Didn't Want to Marry Me"
date: 2009-01-09
tags:

There are sentences that sound universal until you realize they may have been written specifically for you.

"I don't want to get married."

That is a very clear sentence.

At least grammatically.

The problem is that human beings rarely communicate only through grammar.

There is timing.

Context.

Politeness.

Fear.

Family.

Another person you may not know about.

And sometimes there is the possibility that the sentence does not actually mean:

"I don't want to get married."

It means:

"I don't want to marry you."

In January 2009, I wrote an entry about a woman who did not want to get married.

My explanation was generous.

Perhaps too generous.

I imagined that she already had someone in her heart.

Some young man she loved.

Maybe he did not have a proper job yet.

Maybe his situation was uncertain.

Maybe her family would not approve.

So when other marriage proposals appeared, she could not simply say:

"There is someone else."

Instead, she said:

"I am not thinking about marriage."

A sentence that closed the conversation without opening the real one.

Then I added the important detail.

How did I know?

Because it had happened to me.

And then, almost immediately, I became suspicious of my own explanation.

Maybe she had tricked me.

Maybe there was no hidden love story.

Maybe there was no unfortunate young man waiting somewhere in the background.

Maybe the entire theory existed because it was easier for me to believe.

The entry ended with the only logical solution available to an impatient young man writing on the internet:

Maybe I should call her and ask.

That last sentence is still my favorite part.

Not because calling her would necessarily have solved anything.

Because it reveals how quickly the mind can build a complicated theory and then suddenly remember that another human being exists at the center of it.

We do this often.

Especially when we do not like an answer.

A direct rejection creates an empty space.

The mind hates empty space.

So it fills it.

There must be another reason.

Family pressure.

Bad timing.

A secret relationship.

Fear of commitment.

Financial problems.

A previous heartbreak.

Anything except the simplest possibility:

She does not want this with me.

That possibility is difficult because it cannot be solved.

If the problem is money, perhaps money can improve.

If the problem is a job, perhaps a job can be found.

If the problem is family, perhaps the family can eventually be convinced.

If the problem is timing, perhaps waiting will help.

But if the problem is simply lack of desire, there is no engineering solution.

You cannot debug attraction.

You cannot optimize somebody into loving you.

You cannot produce enough evidence to force chemistry into existence.

That is frustrating.

Especially for a mind that likes reasons.

We want rejection to have an external cause because external causes can be repaired.

The alternative is much less satisfying.

No.

Just no.

In 2009, marriage was also surrounded by a different kind of social machinery.

It was not always only about two people.

Families mattered.

Jobs mattered.

Where someone came from mattered.

Whether the man had stable work mattered.

Whether everybody approved could matter far more than the two people involved wanted it to.

A woman saying "I don't want to get married" could genuinely be protecting something more complicated.

Maybe she was avoiding pressure.

Maybe she was protecting someone.

Maybe she was protecting herself.

Or maybe she was simply declining an offer without wanting to hurt the person making it.

All of these are possible.

That is the trouble.

When several explanations are possible, we usually choose the one that hurts least.

I apparently chose a romantic one.

There must be another young man.

She loves him.

He has no proper job.

Her family would object.

She cannot say any of this openly.

So she tells everyone she does not want marriage.

It is almost a film.

A tragic little story with social pressure, hidden love, and bad timing.

Much more satisfying than:

She was not interested.

That version has no soundtrack.

The younger mind is very talented at producing alternative narratives.

Sometimes these narratives protect dignity.

Sometimes they preserve hope.

Sometimes they simply delay understanding.

I do not think that is always foolish.

People need time to absorb disappointment.

The first explanation does not have to be the final one.

But eventually, reality deserves a turn.

This is where the old entry becomes unexpectedly wise despite itself.

After constructing the whole story, I doubted it.

Maybe she had trapped me with a convenient sentence.

Maybe I was interpreting too much.

Then:

I should call and ask.

There is something useful in that instinct.

Not necessarily the literal phone call.

Sometimes calling is exactly what you should not do.

The useful part is recognizing the difference between what happened and the story you created about what happened.

Fact:

She said she did not want to get married.

Interpretation:

She secretly loved someone else but could not tell her family.

Possible alternative:

She simply did not want to marry me.

Those are very different categories.

We often mix them together.

The mind converts interpretation into fact so quickly that we stop noticing the transition.

This happens far beyond relationships.

A boss does not reply.

They must be unhappy with my work.

A friend cancels.

They must not want to see me.

Someone is quiet.

They must be angry.

A person rejects an invitation.

There must be another reason.

Maybe.

Maybe not.

Uncertainty is uncomfortable, so we invent certainty.

The stories can become elaborate.

Sometimes embarrassingly elaborate.

The funny thing is that direct questions do not always solve the problem either.

People are polite.

People protect themselves.

People change their minds.

People do not always understand their own reasons.

You can ask:

"Do you really not want to get married, or do you just not want to marry me?"

and still receive an answer that requires interpretation.

Human communication does not become transparent simply because a question is direct.

But clarity is still better than private mythology.

At some point, you have to stop writing the other person's half of the story for them.

That may be the real lesson hidden in that old entry.

I had written an entire explanation for her.

Her feelings.

Her family.

The unemployed young man she supposedly loved.

The reasons she could not say any of it.

Then I caught myself.

Maybe none of this was true.

That moment of self-doubt was more valuable than the theory.

Because adulthood is partly learning to distinguish between empathy and invention.

Empathy says:

There may be reasons I do not know.

Invention says:

I know exactly what those reasons are.

The first leaves room for another person.

The second replaces them with a character you created.

There is also something very 2009 about the ending.

No long reflection.

No careful emotional boundary-setting.

No modern vocabulary about communication styles.

Just:

Wait.

Maybe she fooled me.

I should call her.

There is a kind of innocence in that.

The belief that one phone call might settle the whole matter.

Maybe it would.

Maybe it would make everything worse.

Either way, the younger version of me was still looking for the answer in the other person.

Years later, I think some answers do not need to be extracted.

If someone says no, the practical meaning is no.

The hidden reason may be interesting.

It may even be important.

But it does not change the decision you have been given.

You do not need to solve the mystery before moving forward.

That took longer to understand.

At the time, though, I was still standing between two possibilities.

Either she did not want to get married.

Or she did not want to marry me.

And apparently, somewhere between those two sentences, I was preparing to make a phone call.
