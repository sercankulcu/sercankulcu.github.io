The first time I played bowling, I scored 170.

That was the problem.

A first attempt is supposed to be clumsy enough to create modest expectations. Mine gave me the opposite impression. I looked at the score and decided that bowling had apparently been waiting for me all my life.

I expected the future to be simple. Play a little more, improve the technique, pass 200, and then continue upward. Instead, the more I played, the worse I became.

After a while, scoring above 100 was difficult.

The first result had turned into an accusation. I had done 170 once, so every weaker game seemed to require an explanation. Was the ball wrong? The lane? My release? Too much force? Too little? Once I began thinking about technique, a movement that had felt natural became crowded with instructions.

Perhaps the first score was skill. Perhaps it was luck. I never found a satisfying answer.

What I learned was that one good result can be more misleading than one bad one. A poor beginning leaves room for improvement. A brilliant beginning creates a standard before you have earned it.

If I had started at sixty and reached one hundred, I might have felt successful. Because I started at 170, the same one hundred felt like decline.

The pins did not care about my expectations.

They simply kept standing.

Numbers become memorable when they detach from arithmetic. 170 could be a price, a distance, a score, a room, a bus, a weight, a count. Without context it is almost nothing. With context it becomes a small hook in the mind.

That is how memory often behaves. It keeps the label after the explanation fades.

The number remains.

The life around it has to be reconstructed.

## Past 170

This is the kind of moment that survives by being oddly specific. The scene keeps one clear fragment and lets the rest stay blurred.

The smallness is not a weakness. It is the reason the memory can remain exact. The memory does not ask to be important; it only asks not to be erased.
