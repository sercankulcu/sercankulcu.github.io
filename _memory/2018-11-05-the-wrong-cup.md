I woke in the middle of the night because the baby needed feeding. I went to the kitchen, prepared something with the confidence of a person who had repeated the same movement many times, and returned carrying a cup of coffee.

The mistake took a moment to become visible. My body had followed the older habit while my mind was still somewhere between sleep and the kitchen. For years, waking tired and preparing something hot had usually meant coffee. The new household routine had arrived faster than the old one had disappeared.

There was no great lesson in the room. There was only a tired parent, a baby waiting for the correct drink, and a cup that had no business being there. That was enough to describe the period better than saying I was exhausted.

Before parenthood, sleeping too little had often been voluntary. Work, university, the internet, a late conversation — there was usually some decision behind the tiredness. Now a sound in the night could stand me up before I had fully agreed to wake.

Responsibility did not feel noble at that hour. It felt like walking through a dark house and trying to remember why I was in the kitchen. Most of care was like that: small actions repeated until they became ordinary, with nobody watching and very little ceremony.

I went back and made the formula.

The coffee remained as evidence that, for a few minutes, two versions of my life had met in the same kitchen. One knew exactly how to make coffee in the middle of the night. The other was still learning what the night now required.
