In July 2010, our office was preparing to ban breakfast.

Starting in August:

no breakfast in the office.

This sounded like a policy for people setting tables.

Eggs.

Cheese.

Tea.

Plates.

Long conversations.

Perhaps a small village breakfast occupying half the morning.

Our actual “breakfast,” according to the entry, was one poğaça.

One.

That was why the rule annoyed me.

The grand category and the actual behavior did not match.

“Breakfast in the office” sounded like a productivity problem.

What we were doing was eating a pastry.

This is how rules become larger than the thing they regulate.

Someone notices a behavior.

The behavior receives a label.

The label sounds serious.

Then policy attacks the label.

Breakfast.

Ban it.

Nobody asks what breakfast means in practice.

A poğaça becomes a prohibited meal.

Organizations do this constantly because categories are easier to manage than context.

No food at desks.

No personal devices.

No remote work.

No social media.

No breakfast.

Policies prefer clean edges.

Real life does not.

The person writing the rule wants something enforceable.

The person living under the rule experiences edge cases.

What about coffee?

What about a biscuit?

What about someone who arrives with a sandwich?

If one poğaça is breakfast, is two definitely breakfast?

At what pastry count does productivity collapse?

Bureaucracy begins where ordinary judgment is replaced with categorical certainty.

This does not mean workplace rules are useless.

Shared spaces need norms.

Food can create smell.

Mess.

Noise.

Long breaks.

Unequal habits.

Customer-facing environments may need standards.

The source does not explain why management wanted the ban, so I should not assign an unfair motive.

Perhaps there had been a real problem.

Perhaps some people did turn breakfast into a long ritual.

Perhaps the rule was responding to something I did not mention.

All we know is how it looked from my seat:

we ate one poğaça.

Therefore the ban felt ridiculous.

That gap is important.

Management often governs aggregate behavior.

Employees experience individual behavior.

Management sees:

People are having breakfast.

Employee sees:

I am eating this.

Both views can be true.

Conflict begins when the rule designed for the aggregate feels absurd at the individual scale.

This is one reason workplace policy often produces resentment even when it has a rational origin.

The person affected does not see the dataset.

They see their own exception.

I only use Facebook for two minutes.

I only come five minutes late.

I only eat one pastry.

I only work from home on Fridays.

Rules flatten these differences.

Sometimes they must.

Sometimes they should not.

Good management is partly knowing when a principle can survive exceptions.

Bad management solves every variation by adding another rule.

Then nobody remembers the purpose.

A workplace becomes a game of procedural compliance.

Do not eat breakfast.

Fine.

Eat the poğaça before entering.

Same calories.

Same employee.

Different side of door.

Policy achieved.

That is the danger of regulating form instead of effect.

If the actual concern is lost work time, regulate work time.

If the concern is mess, regulate mess.

If smell, smell.

If customer perception, define the customer-facing expectation.

“Breakfast” is a proxy.

Proxies are convenient.

They are also sloppy.

The old entry does not make this argument explicitly.

It just complains.

That is often where better questions begin.

Why does this rule feel stupid?

Maybe because the named behavior is not the actual problem.

This insight scales surprisingly well.

Schools ban phones because distraction is difficult to measure.

Companies ban websites because productivity is difficult to measure.

Cities ban categories of activity because nuisance is difficult to measure.

Institutions prefer observable proxies.

Phone visible.

Website open.

Food on desk.

Breakfast happening.

The proxy is easy to enforce.

The underlying goal is harder.

But once people understand the proxy, they optimize around it.

No breakfast?

Eat before.

No personal phone?

Use browser.

No social media?

Open another site.

Humans are very good at passing tests without satisfying intentions.

This is why rules work best when the people subject to them understand and accept the purpose.

Then compliance can be intelligent.

Without purpose, compliance becomes theatrical.

The one-poğaça breakfast is funny because there is almost no distance between the banned behavior and harmless normal life.

A pastry is not an institution.

Yet language inflated it.

This reminds me how much power labels have inside organizations.

“Meeting.”

“Break.”

“Training.”

“Personal use.”

“Overtime.”

“Remote work.”

Once a behavior receives an official label, it inherits rules.

Sometimes changing the label changes treatment even when the behavior is nearly identical.

A five-minute conversation at a desk is normal.

Put it on the calendar and it is a meeting.

A coffee while answering email is work.

Call it breakfast and perhaps it becomes forbidden.

Categories are administrative reality.

This is not uniquely corporate.

Families do it.

Schools.

Governments.

Online communities.

People need names to manage complexity.

The trouble begins when the name replaces observation.

Look again.

What is actually happening?

One poğaça.

Perhaps I am being unfair to the rule.

Maybe the poğaça was only my breakfast while someone else brought an entire buffet.

The archive cannot answer.

That uncertainty should stay in the story.

Personal memory is not an audit.

It records perspective.

From my perspective, the policy was ridiculous.

That is enough to preserve the emotional truth without claiming the office had no legitimate reason.

What I enjoy most now is the scale of rebellion.

Not a strike.

Not a resignation.

Not a formal complaint.

Just:

Come on, breakfast means one poğaça.

That sentence contains an entire class of workplace frustration.

Small rules feel especially irritating because they enter daily routine.

A major policy change may happen once.

A tiny prohibition annoys you every morning.

Daily friction accumulates.

The spoon removed from the kitchen.

The printer moved.

The parking rule.

The dress code detail.

The website blocked.

The poğaça forbidden.

Organizations underestimate how much culture is built from these tiny decisions.

Employees remember what management thinks is too small to matter.

Because small things are where authority becomes physical.

A strategy document is abstract.

A banned breakfast touches your hand at 9 a.m.

That does not make the small issue objectively important.

It makes it experientially vivid.

Good managers understand symbolic scale.

Sometimes allowing the poğaça says:

We trust you to behave like adults.

Sometimes banning it says:

Something has gone wrong enough that we no longer do.

The policy itself communicates beyond the food.

Perhaps that is why I wrote it down.

The pastry was not the only subject.

The rule was a statement about how the office saw us.

Or at least that is how rules can feel from below.

Sixteen years later, I do not need to decide whether management was right.

The memory works better as a small organizational parable.

Before creating a rule, look closely at the thing.

Maybe “breakfast” is actually just one poğaça.
