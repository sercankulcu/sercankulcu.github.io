title: "The Day I Walked Toward the Wrong Classroom"
date: 2010-07-12

There are decisions adults pretend children are making even when everybody already knows the answer will not count.

My first days of school seem to contain one of those decisions.

In July 2010, I wrote about what I imagined I looked like around twenty years earlier.

A child in a black school smock.

A pencil eraser with a hole through the middle, hanging from the neck on a piece of string.

Not all the teeth had come in yet.

Messy blond hair.

Blue eyes.

Then I immediately corrected the image.

Only the last part was fictional.

The important part was what happened when I arrived at school.

I had just started primary school.

Somewhere nearby, I saw children playing.

They were in the kindergarten section.

That looked promising.

So naturally, I began walking in that direction.

My mother intervened and redirected me toward Class 1-B.

The correct destination.

Correct according to the adults, anyway.

I apparently had a different interpretation of the school system.

One side contained games and drawing.

The other side contained lessons, responsibility, and the beginning of the long project known as "getting ready for life."

The choice seemed obvious.

Unfortunately, nobody had actually given me the choice.

This is what makes the memory funny.

Before school started, I remember being asked something like this at a neighbor's house:

Imagine there are two kinds of school.

In one, you play games and draw pictures.

In the other, you start lessons, learn everything, grow up early, and begin taking life seriously.

Which one would you choose?

I do not remember what answer I gave.

But I wrote in 2010 that the answer should have been obvious.

Ask a child who has not started school yet whether they prefer play or "getting a head start on life."

What exactly do we expect?

A six-year-old does not usually say:

I have reviewed the long-term productivity implications and would prefer formal instruction.

The child chooses the room with toys.

That is rational.

From the child's perspective, perhaps more rational than the adult alternative.

The funny part is that the question begins with:

"What would you choose?"

But the decision had already been made.

I was going to primary school.

I was going to 1-B.

The adults were not conducting a referendum.

They were explaining the transition using the language of choice.

Then, on the first day, I accidentally tested whether the choice was real.

I saw the children playing.

I moved toward them.

My mother corrected the experiment.

No.

This way.

Class 1-B.

End of democratic process.

That small scene has stayed interesting to me because it captures something universal about childhood.

Adults ask children questions for many reasons.

Sometimes they genuinely want the answer.

Sometimes they want the child to feel involved.

Sometimes they are teaching.

Sometimes they are entertaining themselves.

And sometimes the question is ceremonial.

"Which would you choose?"

The child answers.

Then reality proceeds according to the adult plan.

This is not necessarily malicious.

Children cannot decide everything.

They should not.

A six-year-old should not be responsible for designing an education.

Parents make decisions because children do not yet have the information, experience, or legal capacity to make them.

The problem is not that adults decide.

The funny part is pretending the decision belongs to the child when it does not.

Children notice this eventually.

Maybe not in language.

But they notice the pattern.

Do you want to wear the blue sweater or the red sweater?

A real choice.

Do you want to go to school?

Not exactly.

Would you like to visit your relatives?

Maybe the car is already running.

Would you prefer to go to bed now or in five minutes?

A highly controlled negotiation.

Childhood is full of bounded choice.

Adults create a box.

The child gets freedom inside it.

This is often good parenting.

A small amount of control helps children develop independence without requiring them to manage the whole world.

But the boundaries matter.

If the child thinks the box is larger than it is, conflict arrives.

My route toward kindergarten was a boundary test.

Apparently the available options were not:

Kindergarten or primary school.

They were:

Walk to 1-B yourself or be redirected by your mother.

I selected the undocumented third option.

The system rejected it.

The visual memory is stronger than the theory.

A school entrance.

Children playing somewhere nearby.

Me moving toward the fun.

My mother turning me toward the classroom.

It is almost too perfect as a metaphor.

Life, over there:

games.

Adults:

No, no. Your future is this way.

That is probably unfair to education.

School also contains games.

Friendships.

Discovery.

Excitement.

Teachers who change lives.

Skills that create freedom later.

But first grade does represent a shift.

Before school, time is organized largely around play and family.

Then a clock appears.

Start time.

End time.

Class.

Break.

Homework.

Attendance.

Rules.

A desk.

A teacher.

You enter one of society's first large institutions.

The institution has a schedule and does not particularly care whether you would rather be drawing.

This is a major transition.

Children experience it in small physical details.

A uniform.

A bag.

A notebook.

A pencil case.

The smell of school.

The sound of many children in a corridor.

The first time somebody tells you where to sit and expects you to remain there.

Adults speak about education in abstract terms.

Learning.

Development.

Future.

Opportunity.

Children experience chairs.

That is why the eraser hanging around my neck matters to me.

It is ridiculous and specific.

An eraser with a hole through it.

A piece of string.

Worn like a necklace.

This is not the kind of detail that appears in official childhood biographies.

It is exactly the kind of detail memory should preserve.

Why was the eraser around my neck?

Practicality, presumably.

Children lose things.

Attach the eraser to the child.

Problem solved.

Very efficient.

Today, the idea is funny.

At the time, perhaps completely normal.

Every period has objects that make perfect sense while you are living in it and become strange later.

Black school smocks belong to that category for many people from my generation in Turkey.

They are not just clothes in memory.

They are the visual language of school.

Rows of children.

White collars.

Dark fabric.

School photographs.

Ceremonies.

A uniform can compress an era into one image.

The old entry places me inside that image.

Not all my teeth.

Messy hair.

Eraser necklace.

Heading in the wrong direction.

That is a much better portrait than a polished studio photograph.

The photograph might show what I looked like.

The story shows what I wanted.

I wanted to go where the children were playing.

That is useful information about a six-year-old.

Perhaps also about adults.

We spend a surprising amount of life walking toward 1-B while glancing at the playground.

Work begins.

Responsibilities accumulate.

Deadlines.

Bills.

Meetings.

Forms.

Calendars.

The serious part of life becomes permanent.

Adults develop a strange relationship with play.

Children play without justification.

Adults often need to explain it.

A hobby should improve you.

Exercise should improve health.

Reading should teach something.

Travel should broaden the mind.

A game should develop strategy.

Even leisure receives performance metrics.

Sometimes an activity is allowed only if it can defend itself as useful.

Children are better at refusing this logic.

Why are you doing that?

Because it is fun.

Complete argument.

No appendix required.

The neighbor's hypothetical choice is funny because one option was play and the other was framed as beginning life early.

As though play were outside life.

As though the serious part were the real part.

This idea is deeply embedded in education and work cultures.

Prepare for school.

School prepares for university.

University prepares for work.

Work prepares for retirement.

A sequence of preparation can consume the thing it claims to prepare for.

At every stage, life is about the next stage.

Then one day you ask when the real part begins.

Perhaps the kindergarten children already knew.

They were playing now.

Of course, this can be taken too far.

Children need education.

Adults need work.

Planning for the future matters.

Discipline matters.

The lesson is not to choose play over every responsibility.

The lesson is to be suspicious of a life constructed entirely as preparation.

Education is most alive when learning itself is meaningful, not only useful later.

Work is most sustainable when the present day contains something worthwhile, not only salary at the end of the month.

A good life cannot be permanently deferred.

I doubt the child walking toward kindergarten had any of these philosophical concerns.

He saw kids playing.

He went that way.

Correct decision according to available evidence.

My mother possessed additional requirements.

She redirected me.

This is another feature of childhood memory:

parents appear as forces of navigation.

Literally and otherwise.

Go there.

Do this.

Not that.

Study.

Eat.

Sleep.

Be careful.

The child experiences these instructions as restrictions.

Years later, the same actions look more complicated.

A parent is steering someone who cannot yet see the road.

Sometimes correctly.

Sometimes not.

Always with incomplete information.

The child's task is eventually to take over navigation.

This transition is gradual.

At first, adults decide everything.

Then some things.

Then fewer things.

Eventually they can advise but no longer redirect your body toward Class 1-B.

At least not physically.

The internal version of parental direction can remain much longer.

People carry instructions after the instructor is gone.

Study hard.

Do not waste food.

Call when you arrive.

Take a jacket.

Choose the safe option.

Be polite.

Do not trust everyone.

Some inherited rules remain useful.

Some have to be examined.

Growing up is partly deciding which directions still belong to you.

This is why the line at the end of my old entry makes me laugh.

The question had been:

"What would you choose?"

My later response was:

But I was not the one who got to choose, so what exactly were we discussing?

That sentence contains a child's objection expressed by an adult.

The objection is logically perfect.

Do not offer an imaginary branch in the decision tree if the code will never execute it.

Maybe that is the computer engineer in me reviewing childhood specifications.

Invalid user input.

Option displayed but disabled.

Bad interface.

The adults had designed a UI dark pattern.

"Choose your school experience."

Button A: Play and draw.

Button B: Start formal education.

User selects A.

System response:

Your selection has been updated to B.

Thank you.

The joke practically writes itself.

But I do not feel cheated by the memory.

That would be too dramatic.

What I feel is affection for the misunderstanding.

The child had not yet learned how institutions work.

He assumed visible possibilities might be available possibilities.

That is a beautiful kind of innocence.

You see something.

You want it.

You walk toward it.

No internal committee.

No cost-benefit analysis.

No social embarrassment.

No question about whether you are allowed to desire the obvious thing.

Adults become much better at suppressing those movements.

We see the playground and keep walking toward the assigned room.

Often correctly.

Sometimes automatically.

Perhaps adulthood requires occasionally checking whether the old redirection is still necessary.

Am I going this way because I chose it?

Because I have to?

Because I once had to and forgot the rule could change?

These are different conditions.

A child has little control.

An adult usually has more than they feel.

Not total control.

Never total.

But more.

The irony is that when choice finally becomes real, deciding becomes harder.

At six:

Games or lessons?

Games.

Easy.

At thirty:

Career A or B?

City A or B?

Stay or leave?

Security or freedom?

The columns multiply.

The future becomes less legible.

Children lack authority but often have clear preferences.

Adults gain authority and lose clarity.

Maybe that is why childhood choices seem so pure in retrospect.

There were fewer variables.

The playground was better.

Done.

We should be careful not to romanticize childhood too much.

Children also experience fear, helplessness, boredom, exclusion, and confusion.

Memory edits.

Still, the simplicity of desire is real.

I like this story because it preserves that simplicity before the world taught me to explain myself more.

There were children playing.

I moved toward them.

My mother said no.

Class 1-B.

This way.

I went.

And then, presumably, school began.

The archive does not tell me what happened in the classroom that day.

That is fine.

The doorway is enough.

There are moments that become symbolic only later.

At the time, it was probably a few seconds.

Walk.

Redirect.

Continue.

In memory, it becomes the border between one kind of childhood and another.

On one side:

play.

On the other:

the first classroom.

And in the middle:

my mother, doing traffic control.

The older I get, the more I think education works best when those two sides are not treated as enemies.

Children learn through curiosity.

Experiment.

Play.

Mistakes.

Movement.

Questions.

Formal education gives structure, language, shared knowledge, and access to worlds a child cannot discover alone.

The best version should not say:

Play is over. Life begins.

It should say:

We are going to make the world larger.

Sometimes seriously.

Sometimes playfully.

Always with room for curiosity.

Perhaps that is what I would tell the child now.

You cannot stay in kindergarten forever.

You are going to 1-B.

But do not worry.

The playground is not the opposite of learning.

Keep walking toward it sometimes.

Even when nobody asks you to choose.
