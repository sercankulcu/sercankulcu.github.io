title: "The Nightclub Where We Didn't Understand the Rules"
date: 2009-07-26

In July 2009, I walked into a nightclub in Montreal and spent the first half hour trying to understand what kind of place I had entered.

The club was called 1234.

At the time, I described it as the biggest and most crowded nightclub I had seen in Montreal.

It had three levels, if I ignore the basement, which I jokingly described as possibly the biggest and most crowded bathroom in the city.

The entrance level had a bar.

The middle had a very large dance-floor-like area, although people did not seem particularly interested in limiting dancing to the official dance floor.

Another level had no bar at all and seemed to serve mostly as a place for couples who wanted more privacy.

The top level looked like a recovery zone.

People sat around trying to sober up.

People smoked.

Some had taken their shoes off.

The whole building seemed to contain a different social rule on every floor.

The entrance fee was fifteen dollars.

That last detail mattered enough to make the original entry.

Fifteen dollars was measurable.

Everything else required interpretation.

That was the real difficulty.

When you travel, you expect unfamiliar streets.

Different food.

Different accents.

Different currency.

You know those things will require adjustment.

What catches you off guard are the unwritten rules.

How close people stand.

What counts as flirting.

What is considered normal in a bar.

What people wear.

Who talks to whom.

How strangers behave with one another.

Whether touching someone is ordinary, bold, rude, or an invitation.

These rules rarely come with signs.

You learn them by watching.

That night, watching was exactly what we did.

For about half an hour.

The original entry says that if you were Turkish, the place felt strange at first.

That sentence was written quickly and too broadly.

There is no single Turkish reaction to a nightclub, just as there is no single Canadian one.

But it accurately preserves my own feeling.

I had arrived with expectations built somewhere else.

The room did not behave according to them.

So I did what people usually do when their internal manual stops working.

I observed.

That is one of the hidden activities of travel.

You are not simply seeing a new place.

You are constantly updating your idea of what is normal.

The process begins almost immediately.

Even before we entered, there was a misunderstanding.

We spoke to the man at the door.

The way I wrote it in 2009, we tried to explain that we had no women with us and asked, in effect, whether that would prevent us from entering.

The assumption behind the question was ours.

We thought the composition of our group might matter.

The man at the door did not seem to understand why we were explaining any of this.

We tried again.

Still nothing.

Eventually the conversation reached its logical conclusion:

Fine.

We are going in.

That tiny exchange now seems much more interesting than it did then.

We were not only asking for entry.

We were revealing the rules we expected.

The doorman's confusion was an answer.

Not necessarily a verbal answer.

A cultural one.

Why are you telling me this?

That can be one of the fastest ways to discover that a rule you considered obvious is local rather than universal.

Travel is full of these moments.

You prepare for a problem.

The other person does not recognize the problem.

Or you behave casually and suddenly discover that everyone around you thinks you have violated a rule.

Either way, your own assumptions become visible.

At home, cultural assumptions are difficult to see because everyone keeps confirming them.

Abroad, somebody looks at you blankly.

That blank look can be educational.

Inside, the education continued.

The original entry describes women dressed for the club in language I would not use now.

The younger version of me was writing with the crude vocabulary of a young man reporting nightlife to an equally informal audience.

What matters to the memory is not the judgment.

It is the surprise.

People were dressed more openly than I was accustomed to seeing in similar places.

Physical interaction between strangers seemed more relaxed.

The boundaries I expected were not the boundaries the room appeared to be using.

Again, there was no orientation session.

No guide came over and said:

Welcome to Montreal nightlife. Here are the rules.

We watched other people.

Then adjusted our understanding.

Human beings are extremely good at this.

We enter a room and begin collecting signals almost unconsciously.

Who is comfortable?

Who is performing?

Who seems out of place?

What are people allowed to do?

What behavior receives no reaction at all?

That last question is often the most useful.

Normality is partly defined by what nobody bothers to notice.

The things that surprised us were not surprising anyone else.

That told us more than a hundred explanations could have.

Eventually we stopped being pure observers and participated more.

The old entry describes brief interactions with women in the club and the casualness of physical contact.

I do not need to reconstruct more than the source says.

The important point is that what had initially seemed difficult to decode became understandable through participation.

This is another pattern in travel.

Observation can take you only so far.

At some point you have to order the food.

Get on the bus.

Try the phrase.

Enter the bar.

Dance.

Ask.

Make a small mistake.

Then the place becomes less theoretical.

Before that moment, you are looking at the culture.

Afterward, you are temporarily inside it.

The three levels of 1234 almost felt like separate environments.

The first gave you the recognizable nightclub structure.

Bar.

Crowd.

Music.

Large open area.

The next seemed organized around intimacy.

The top floor, meanwhile, looked like what happens after the nightclub has already happened to you.

Recovery.

Smoking.

Shoes off.

Bodies losing their polished beginning-of-the-night presentation.

I remember that contrast because nightlife has stages.

People arrive assembled.

Clothes right.

Hair right.

Energy high.

Then hours pass.

The building changes without moving.

Jackets disappear.

Posture changes.

People sit where they can.

Someone is tired.

Someone is too drunk.

Someone is trying to recover enough to continue.

Someone no longer cares how they look.

By late night, formality leaks out of the room.

The top floor of 1234 seems to have concentrated that transformation.

The original description is rough and comic.

People sitting without shoes.

People clearly very drunk.

People behaving in ways that would have been impossible to mistake for elegance.

It was less the glamorous nightlife advertised in photographs and more the biological aftermath of nightlife.

That is worth remembering.

Cities sell night through images taken near the beginning.

Clean glasses.

Good clothes.

Perfect lighting.

Nobody advertises the final hour.

The final hour is where sociology begins.

What interests me now is how intensely the younger version of me categorized everything.

This floor is for this.

That floor is for that.

These people are doing this.

Those people are doing that.

When we encounter unfamiliar environments, categorization gives us control.

If I can name the pattern, I am no longer lost.

The first half hour is confusion.

Then:

Ah.

I understand.

This is the dance area.

This is where people disappear together.

This is where people recover and smoke.

Map complete.

Whether the map was entirely accurate is another question.

Probably not.

Visitors simplify places.

We have to.

We do not have enough time to understand all the local nuance.

So we make a functional model.

Good enough for tonight.

That model later becomes the memory.

This is one reason travel writing should be humble.

A traveler sees something once and can easily mistake observation for knowledge.

I saw a nightclub.

I did not discover Canada.

I spent a night in a room.

I did not become an anthropologist of Montreal.

The 2009 entry sometimes speaks with more confidence than that distinction deserves.

That is normal for young travel writing.

Everything feels representative because everything is new.

Today I would frame it differently.

I was learning one place.

One night.

One crowd.

My reaction says as much about me as it does about the club.

Perhaps more.

That is often true of travel.

We say:

This city is strange.

What we usually mean is:

This city is not behaving according to the habits I brought with me.

That does not make the reaction meaningless.

It makes it personal.

And personal is exactly what a memory should be.

The fifteen-dollar entrance fee is one of my favorite details because it lands at the end of all the chaos with complete practicality.

You can be overwhelmed by culture, behavior, architecture, noise, and unfamiliarity.

Still:

How much was it?

Fifteen dollars.

Money makes travel concrete.

Old travel memories often survive through prices.

Entry: five dollars.

Beer: this much.

Taxi: that much.

Hotel: too expensive.

Meal: surprisingly cheap.

These numbers later become timestamps.

Inflation changes them.

Our incomes change.

Our relationship with money changes.

A price that once mattered enormously can later look trivial.

Or the opposite.

But the remembered number preserves the younger traveler’s scale.

Fifteen dollars was worth mentioning.

Therefore fifteen dollars mattered.

Montreal in 2009 appears repeatedly in my archive through these practical details.

Work.

Bars.

Nightclubs.

People.

Prices.

Streets.

Music.

The city does not survive as an abstract travel destination.

It survives as encounters.

This is much better.

Generic memory says:

I spent time in Montreal.

Specific memory says:

We stood at the entrance of 1234 explaining a concern the doorman did not understand.

Then we went inside and spent half an hour figuring out the rules.

That scene has life.

It also preserves a version of me that no longer needs to be defended.

Young.

Foreign.

Curious.

Sometimes crude.

Sometimes judgmental.

Sometimes amused by things simply because they were unfamiliar.

Maturity should not erase that person.

It should add perspective.

The younger version saw people and quickly assigned labels.

The older version can ask:

Why did that seem strange to me?

What assumptions had I carried into the room?

Which judgments were really descriptions of my own discomfort?

That is a more useful form of nostalgia than pretending I had always been perfectly enlightened.

Travel changes people partly by producing embarrassment in retrospect.

You discover how much of what you called “normal” was merely familiar.

This discovery rarely happens all at once.

It arrives through hundreds of small contradictions.

The doorman does not understand your question.

The crowd does not react to something you expected them to react to.

A stranger behaves differently from what your social script predicted.

Nothing collapses.

The world continues.

Your script gets one revision.

Then another.

That is education without a classroom.

Nightlife is especially good at exposing these differences because it contains fewer formal rules and more social ones.

At an airport, procedures are written.

Passport here.

Security there.

Gate number.

Boarding time.

A nightclub is mostly atmosphere and implicit permission.

People learn by imitation.

The traveler has to read the room.

That night, we were slow readers.

Half an hour.

Not terrible.

Eventually the place stopped seeming impossible to understand.

This may be the most important transition in the story.

At first, everything unfamiliar looks like a single block.

What is this place?

Then details separate.

This happens here.

That happens upstairs.

People come here for this.

The chaos gets structure.

The same thing happens when moving to a new city.

First:

Everything is strange.

Then:

This street is expensive.

That neighborhood is quiet.

This café is good.

That bus is useless.

This route gets me home.

Strangeness breaks into knowledge.

One day, without noticing, you stop being a visitor in your own mind.

I never became local to 1234.

One night is not enough for that.

But the mechanism was the same in miniature.

Confusion.

Observation.

Participation.

A rough map.

Memory.

There is also something funny about the seriousness with which young men can analyze nightlife.

A room exists primarily so people can drink, dance, flirt, and have fun.

Yet a group of visitors can study it like an unfamiliar machine.

Entrance protocol.

Floor functions.

Behavioral norms.

Cost structure.

Observed user activity.

By the end of the night, someone could almost produce documentation.

Perhaps that tendency never completely leaves engineers.

But I do not want to turn this memory into another engineering metaphor.

It was simpler than that.

We did not know what was going on.

So we looked around.

Then we figured out enough to enjoy the place.

That is a good travel skill.

You do not need complete understanding.

You need enough understanding to stop being afraid of misunderstanding.

There is a point where curiosity becomes more useful than judgment.

The younger entry is strongest whenever it is curious.

What is happening here?

What is this floor for?

Why did the doorman react like that?

How does this work?

The weaker parts are the labels.

Labels end inquiry.

Curiosity continues it.

If I were preserving only one lesson from that night, it would be this:

When a place seems strange, watch longer.

The first explanation is often just your old habits complaining.

A half hour later, the room may make sense.

Not because the room changed.

Because you did.

And that is probably why I still find the memory worth keeping.

Not because 1234 was the largest club I had seen in Montreal.

Not because it had three levels.

Not because the entrance cost fifteen dollars.

Not even because of everything we saw upstairs.

I remember the uncertainty.

Standing inside a social world that seemed to be following rules no one had given us.

Looking around.

Comparing.

Adjusting.

Realizing that everyone else appeared perfectly comfortable.

Then slowly becoming comfortable enough ourselves.

A foreign city can teach you in museums.

In offices.

On streets.

At universities.

And sometimes, apparently, in a crowded nightclub at an hour when nobody has any intention of teaching you anything.
