The watch was advertised at 579 lira.

Beside it was another number.

1947.

The page wanted me to look at the difference and feel lucky.

1947 to 579.

A huge fall.

A rare opportunity.

Then I checked the normal price elsewhere.

629.

Now the story was different.

629 minus 579.

Fifty lira.

The watch had not changed.

The final price had not changed.

Only the number standing beside it had changed.

That was enough to change the entire feeling of the offer.

A discount is never just one number. It is a relationship between two numbers, and the first one tells you how excited you are supposed to become about the second.

If the page had shown only 579, I would have asked a simple question:

Is this watch worth 579 lira to me?

But once 1947 appeared, another question entered:

How can I miss such a bargain?

That is the trick.

The customer begins thinking about money supposedly saved instead of money actually spent.

A product page can say:

You saved 1368 lira.

The bank account says:

You spent 579.

I preferred the second version.

The funny part is that the arithmetic felt almost insulting once I saw it. The page had built a small theater around the price. The old number was the spotlight. The new number was the hero. I was supposed to applaud before asking whether the stage was real.

Online shopping is full of these little pressures.

Only two left.

Sale ends tonight.

People are viewing this now.

The product becomes less important than the fear of losing the moment.

I understand why it works. A normal price asks us to decide. A dramatic discount asks us to react. Reaction is faster, and faster customers are often better customers for the seller.

So I slowed the moment down with the least glamorous tool available.

Subtraction.

629 - 579 = 50.

That was the real discount I saw.

Still a discount.

Just not the miracle advertised on the page.

Sometimes consumer skepticism does not require expertise.

A calculator is enough.
