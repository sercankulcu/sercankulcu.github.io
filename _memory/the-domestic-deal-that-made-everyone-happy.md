In July 2010, I described washing dishes as a “put in one, get three back” kind of job.

The mathematics was not literal.

The strategy was.

I did not like washing dishes.

That part was ordinary.

But I also did not know much about cooking.

So if the person beside me also disliked washing dishes, there was an obvious arrangement:

You cook.

I wash.

On paper, that looks like fair division.

In my old entry, however, I believed I had found something much more sophisticated.

The other person did not have to wash dishes.

Good.

I did not have to cook.

Even better.

And if the food was terrible, I could still declare it wonderful.

“Very good. I’m practically eating my fingers.”

Praise delivered.

Mood improved.

Then I washed the dishes.

You cannot really be bad at washing dishes in the same theatrical way someone can be bad at cooking.

Perhaps I used too much water.

Perhaps I was inefficient.

But the plates eventually became clean.

Mission accomplished.

Then:

feet up.

Rest.

This was, according to the younger version of me, domestic genius.

The joke works because the arrangement is presented as manipulation while actually being a reasonable division of labor.

One person does the task the other cannot do.

The other handles the task the first dislikes.

Nobody gets exactly what they want.

Everybody avoids something worse.

That is most cooperation.

We like to imagine good partnerships as equal splits.

Half the cooking.

Half the cleaning.

Half the shopping.

Half the planning.

Symmetry looks fair because it is easy to count.

Real households are often better when they are asymmetric.

One person cooks more.

Another cleans.

One handles bills.

Another remembers birthdays.

One drives.

Another plans.

One fixes things.

Another notices they are broken.

The important question is not whether every task is divided fifty-fifty.

It is whether the overall system feels fair to the people inside it.

Fairness is not arithmetic.

It is perceived balance.

The old entry understood this accidentally.

I was not trying to design a theory of household labor.

I was trying to avoid cooking.

Still, the result is revealing.

When people specialize according to preference or ability, total misery can decrease.

That sounds obvious.

Yet many arguments begin because people count tasks instead of effort.

“I did three things.”

“Yes, but mine took two hours.”

“I cooked.”

“Yes, but I cleaned everything.”

“I always do X.”

“I always remember Y.”

Domestic accounting can become impossible because the units are incompatible.

Minutes.

Energy.

Mental load.

Skill.

Disgust.

Urgency.

There is no common currency.

One person may consider washing dishes trivial and cooking exhausting.

Another the opposite.

The best division exploits those differences.

If I hate a task you do not mind, and you hate one I tolerate, we have discovered trade.

Civilization begins near the sink.

The old entry adds another layer through praise.

The food could be bad.

I would still say it was excellent.

That part deserves a more mature look.

There is kindness in encouraging someone who cooked for you.

There is also danger in praise that becomes completely disconnected from reality.

If every meal is “amazing,” the word stops carrying information.

Still, not every dinner needs formal peer review.

Sometimes the correct response to an imperfect meal made by someone you care about is gratitude rather than analysis.

You can notice the salt and still appreciate the effort.

You can distinguish:

This is objectively the best food I have eaten

from:

I am happy you made this for us.

The younger version of me collapsed both into exaggerated praise.

That was comic.

The deeper truth is that people often need appreciation more than scoring.

Nobody wants to finish cooking and immediately receive a technical report.

Texture: 6.2.

Salt distribution: inconsistent.

Presentation: adequate.

Recommendation: major revision.

Home is not a restaurant.

The cook may want evidence that the effort mattered.

The plate being eaten.

A smile.

A compliment.

Then someone else taking the dirty dishes away.

That is a decent ending.

There is also something honest in choosing the task where failure is less visible.

Cooking asks for judgment.

Dishes mostly ask for completion.

The food can be too salty, too dry, undercooked, overcooked, badly timed.

A clean plate is a clean plate.

I had discovered a low-risk department.

This is a classic human move.

People gravitate toward work where the success criteria are clearer.

A task with objective completion feels safer than one requiring taste.

This is true in offices too.

Some people prefer building.

Some reviewing.

Some planning.

Some operating.

Part of career fit is finding the kind of uncertainty you tolerate.

At home, I apparently preferred detergent to cuisine.

The source says I might waste more water.

That small admission is good.

Even my supposedly foolproof task had a way to be inefficient.

There is no perfect escape from competence.

You can avoid cooking and still wash badly.

But the threshold is forgiving enough that the system works.

The final phrase — now it is time to put your feet up — reveals what the whole scheme was really optimizing.

Not domestic excellence.

Peace.

The food is made.

The dishes are clean.

The other person is happy.

I am no longer working.

This is perhaps the most realistic household KPI ever designed.

Is everyone fed?

Is the kitchen usable?

Is anybody angry?

Can we sit down now?

Success.

We often overcomplicate domestic life by importing standards from professional life.

Optimization.

Efficiency.

Best practice.

Perfect organization.

But a home is not a factory.

The output is not maximum productivity.

It is a livable rhythm.

Sometimes a slightly inefficient arrangement survives precisely because it keeps people happier.

Maybe one person uses too much water.

Maybe the other makes mediocre food.

If both prefer the deal, the system has value.

This is why outsiders are often poor judges of household arrangements.

They see asymmetry and assume unfairness.

Perhaps it is unfair.

Perhaps not.

Only the people doing the work know which tasks cost them more.

The better question is:

Does anyone feel trapped?

Does anyone's work become invisible?

Can the arrangement change when circumstances change?

If yes, specialization can be healthy.

If not, it becomes expectation.

That distinction matters.

“you cook, I wash” is charming when freely negotiated.

It becomes less charming if one role is permanently assigned without consent.

The old entry assumes a playful voluntary arrangement.

That is how it should remain.

I also like the source because it preserves a younger kind of confidence.

I had discovered a loophole.

Cooking?

No.

Dishes?

Fine.

Bad food?

Praise.

Everybody happy.

Case closed.

Life eventually teaches that domestic systems are rarely closed that neatly.

There are groceries before the meal.

Planning.

Cleaning surfaces.

Putting dishes away.

Remembering what is missing.

Deciding what to eat.

The visible tasks are only part of the work.

But for one evening in 2010, the equation was beautifully simple.

You cook.

I wash.

I praise.

We rest.

Sometimes the best domestic agreements are not fifty-fifty.

They are just arrangements where nobody is keeping score.
