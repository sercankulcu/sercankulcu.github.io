title: "The Night We Became Eskişehirspor Fans"
date: 2010-03-08

Some business trips produce reports.

Some produce expense forms.

Some produce one strange evening that survives longer than the work itself.

In March 2010, I was in Eskişehir for work.

The archive does not tell me what the assignment was.

That part has disappeared.

Which is almost perfect.

Whatever professional reason had taken us there was apparently less memorable than what happened after work.

We were sitting around wondering what to do that evening when our manager, a devoted Galatasaray supporter, produced football tickets he had already bought.

He had not asked us.

There had been no vote.

No discussion.

No careful scheduling.

He simply announced:

Come on, guys. We are going to the match.

That was the plan.

And once the manager has already bought the tickets, "I don't really feel like going" becomes a theoretically available but socially interesting option.

So we went.

The match was Eskişehirspor against Galatasaray.

We were Galatasaray supporters.

At least that was how we entered the story.

By the end of the night, our behavior would make that classification slightly harder to defend.

The first problem was getting into the stadium.

We had tickets.

This sounds like the main requirement for entering a football match.

Apparently it was only the beginning.

The game had already started by the time we managed to get inside.

We struggled through the entrance despite having legitimate tickets, and when we finally reached the stands, the next problem became obvious:

There was nowhere to stand.

Not nowhere in the philosophical sense.

There was space.

Just not the kind of space occupied by relaxed spectators politely waiting for strangers to arrive.

We had somehow ended up among the hardest-core Eskişehirspor supporters.

The kind of supporters for whom standing quietly during the match is not a neutral act.

It is evidence.

Possibly hostile evidence.

The old entry says we nearly got beaten up.

I do not need to exaggerate that further.

It already contains all the atmosphere required.

We were visitors from another city.

Supporting the opposing team.

Standing inside the most enthusiastic part of the home crowd.

This is not advanced strategic planning.

We tried to explain ourselves.

Wait.

Calm down.

We came from out of town.

We are here to support.

Support whom, exactly, may have been a question best left vague.

Thankfully, one man in the crowd helped us.

He spoke up on our behalf and allowed us, in effect, to remain there.

For about five minutes.

Then the local terms and conditions became clearer.

If you are going to stand here, you shout.

If you are not going to shout, go somewhere else.

The language in the original entry was somewhat less diplomatic.

Fair enough.

Football terraces are not diplomatic institutions.

This is where the evening changed.

At first, we had gone to watch a match.

Now we had been assigned a role.

Spectator was no longer enough.

Participation was mandatory.

There is a huge difference between observing a crowd and being absorbed by one.

From outside, supporters look like a group.

From inside, the group becomes a physical environment.

Noise behind you.

Noise in front of you.

People moving together.

Chants beginning somewhere you cannot identify.

Arms rising.

Bodies reacting before thought catches up.

A football crowd can turn emotion into weather.

You do not merely hear excitement.

You stand inside it.

This is one reason stadium experiences cannot be reproduced fully on television.

Television gives you a better view.

Replays.

Commentary.

Angles.

Statistics.

A stadium gives you worse information and better contagion.

You may miss part of the action because someone is standing in front of you.

You may not know exactly what the referee called.

You may spend half the time trying not to lose your place.

But when tens of thousands of people react at once, knowledge becomes secondary.

The body understands before the brain does.

Something happened.

Everybody rises.

You rise.

Everybody shouts.

You shout.

Emotion travels faster than explanation.

That night, we had an additional incentive.

Our immediate social environment had made silence inadvisable.

So we shouted.

This is how identity becomes flexible under pressure.

I had entered as a Galatasaray supporter.

The crowd around me was Eskişehirspor.

There was no questionnaire.

No one asked about childhood loyalty.

No one requested evidence of previous matches.

The practical question was:

Are you participating or not?

And participation has a way of becoming sincere faster than expected.

At first, perhaps you shout because everyone else is shouting.

Then the match continues.

You start watching the home team's attacks with more interest.

The crowd reacts.

You react to the crowd.

The separation becomes weaker.

Then Eskişehirspor scores.

And we lose our minds.

That is what the old entry remembers most clearly.

We were Galatasaray supporters.

Eskişehir scored against Galatasaray.

And we celebrated so enthusiastically that anyone watching us could have mistaken us for Fenerbahçe supporters enjoying Galatasaray's suffering.

That is a beautiful betrayal.

Not serious betrayal.

Football betrayal.

The harmless kind created by geography, adrenaline, self-preservation, and collective energy.

For one goal, team identity became less important than being exactly where we were.

Eskişehir.

Inside the crowd.

Among people who had already made it clear that enthusiasm was not optional.

So when the goal came, we celebrated.

Maybe partly because we were supposed to.

Maybe partly because the crowd carried us.

Maybe partly because by then the absurdity of the situation had become too enjoyable to resist.

The source does not divide the percentages.

It does not need to.

We celebrated.

That is the fact.

Years later, I like this memory because it reveals how weak some of our supposedly fixed identities become when context changes.

People say:

I am this kind of person.

I support this team.

I never do that.

I always do this.

Then life places them somewhere unusual and the sentence becomes negotiable.

This does not mean identity is fake.

It means identity has layers.

Galatasaray supporter.

Employee on a work trip.

Guest in Eskişehir.

Man currently surrounded by very committed Eskişehirspor fans.

All true at once.

Which identity wins depends partly on the minute.

When the home team scores and everyone around you explodes, the last identity can become surprisingly persuasive.

Human beings are social animals in ways we prefer not to admit.

We like to imagine independent judgment.

My opinions.

My tastes.

My decisions.

Then a crowd begins chanting.

Crowds are ancient technology.

No electricity required.

A few hundred or a few thousand people doing the same thing can alter behavior quickly.

Clapping.

Singing.

Booing.

Laughing.

Panicking.

Celebrating.

One person begins.

Others join.

Soon nobody knows who began.

The action belongs to the group.

This can be beautiful.

It can also be dangerous.

The same mechanism that makes strangers celebrate together can make groups cruel, irrational, or violent.

The emotional power is neutral.

What matters is where it is directed.

That night, fortunately, the direction was football.

Shout.

Celebrate.

Survive the terrace.

Go home with a story.

I also like the manager's role in all this.

A committed Galatasaray supporter buys tickets to an away match during a work trip and simply announces that the team is going.

There is something very 2010 about the informality of it.

Perhaps there is something timeless about managers on business trips too.

Work hours end.

The hierarchy does not disappear completely, but it softens.

People eat together.

Walk somewhere.

Find entertainment.

The person who decides things during the day may also decide what everyone is doing at night.

Except now the decision is:

Football.

This is how colleagues become temporarily different people outside the office.

The quiet engineer becomes the loudest person in the stadium.

The manager becomes the man holding tickets.

The person you only discuss deadlines with turns out to know every chant.

Work travel compresses relationships because ordinary boundaries change.

You are away from home.

In another city.

Eating meals together.

Spending evenings together because your usual social worlds are absent.

A week of work travel can produce more informal interaction than months in an office.

This can be exhausting.

It can also create memories completely unrelated to the work.

The strange thing is that those are often the memories that last.

I do not remember the technical meeting from that evening.

At least the archive does not preserve it.

I remember almost getting into trouble in the stand.

This should be alarming to employers who spend enormous time documenting professional activity.

Memory has its own retention policy.

Project details expire.

The man who saved you from angry supporters survives.

There is another Eskişehir thread elsewhere in my archive.

Four friends on a short work trip.

Kalabak water.

People joking that once we drank it, we were half Eskişehirli.

Then returning to the city regularly.

Another memory reduces Eskişehir to a sequence of places around a hotel, a shop, a soup place, and a pastry shop.

Cities enter memory through repetition and accidents.

Not monuments.

Not necessarily tourist attractions.

A city becomes:

the hotel we stayed in,
the place we ate after midnight,
the water everyone talked about,
the football match where we accidentally became home supporters.

This is how a personal city differs from a travel guide.

A guide tells you what is important.

Memory tells you what happened to you.

Those lists overlap less than expected.

Eskişehir in my archive is not primarily a collection of landmarks.

It is work trips becoming social life.

That gives the city an intimacy beyond tourism.

You begin as someone visiting for work.

Then you acquire routines.

Faces.

Food.

A stadium story.

The city stops being "somewhere I went."

It becomes somewhere that contains versions of you.

That may be what belonging really is.

Not ownership.

Stored versions of the self.

One version in Ankara.

One at university.

One in Montreal.

One in Eskişehir shouting for a goal scored against his own team.

The more places contain such versions, the harder it becomes to say where you are entirely from.

I also think about football allegiance itself.

Supporting a team is one of the strangest durable relationships people voluntarily enter.

The team changes players.

Managers change.

Owners and administrators change.

Stadiums change.

Sponsors change.

Kits change.

The supporter says:

Same team.

The continuity exists partly in symbols and memory.

That makes the attachment real even though almost every material component can be replaced.

But football loyalty also permits temporary exceptions because the deeper pleasure is often the event itself.

A good match.

A great atmosphere.

An underdog goal.

A crowd erupting.

For one night, appreciation can outrun allegiance.

The old entry ends simply:

It was a good match.

That sentence is important.

After everything —

the forced plan,
the difficult entrance,
the lack of space,
the near-conflict,
the shouting,
the disloyal celebration —

the verdict is positive.

Good match.

This is the transformation of inconvenience into memory.

While things are going wrong, we complain.

Later, the trouble becomes the story.

Had we entered easily, found comfortable seats, watched quietly, and left without incident, would I have written about it?

Probably not.

Smooth experiences are pleasant and forgettable.

Friction gives narrative shape.

This does not mean we should seek danger or inconvenience.

It means memory values recovery.

Something went wrong.

Then we adapted.

That is a story.

There is a useful difference between bad experiences and difficult experiences.

Some things are simply bad.

They do not become charming with time.

But manageable disorder can improve in memory.

Wrong turn.

Missed train.

Bad weather.

Unexpected crowd.

Confusing entrance.

The present self suffers.

The future storyteller is grateful.

This is unfair.

But common.

The safest travel itinerary often produces the weakest story.

Again, not an argument for carelessness.

Only an observation about narrative.

The story requires deviation.

Our deviation began when the manager revealed the tickets.

From then on, the evening no longer belonged to the original plan.

Perhaps there had never been a plan.

We had only asked:

What should we do tonight?

The answer arrived already printed.

Football has another useful feature for memory: it gives the night a score and a date.

Many evenings blur.

This one was 8 March 2010.

Eskişehirspor versus Galatasaray.

The match anchors everything.

Without it, perhaps the details would scatter.

With it, the night becomes searchable in the mind.

I can place the people around it.

Work trip.

Manager.

Stadium.

Supporters.

Goal.

That is why public events often become hooks for private memory.

Where were you during this match?

This election?

This snowfall?

This concert?

This blackout?

The shared event provides a timestamp.

Your private life wraps around it.

History and autobiography meet.

To millions of people, the match is a sporting event.

To me, it is the evening we entered the wrong part of the stadium and temporarily changed teams.

Both histories are true.

The public record may preserve the score better.

My archive preserves the embarrassment.

I know which one I prefer.

There is also a little lesson in hospitality hidden in the man who defended us.

We were outsiders.

Possibly irritating outsiders.

We clearly did not understand where we were standing.

He could have ignored us.

Instead, he helped.

Then, naturally, the group eventually demanded that we behave like the group.

Hospitality has conditions.

You may stay.

But participate.

There is something almost traditional in that arrangement.

Welcome.

Now eat.

Welcome.

Now drink.

Welcome.

Now sing.

Welcome.

Now support Eskişehirspor.

Guests are often invited into belonging through ritual.

Do what we do and for a moment you are one of us.

This can be affectionate or coercive depending on the setting.

In a football stand, it is both.

We accepted.

Very enthusiastically, apparently.

By the time Eskişehir scored, we no longer needed much instruction.

That is how quickly ritual works.

People sometimes mock football because "it is just twenty-two men chasing a ball."

Technically true.

Also irrelevant.

Humans can make ritual out of almost anything.

A ball.

A song.

A flag.

A meal.

A graduation robe.

A birthday cake.

The physical object is not the point.

Shared attention is.

Football gives thousands of people permission to care about the same small event at exactly the same second.

That synchronized attention is powerful.

Modern life rarely synchronizes people so completely.

Everyone is usually looking at different screens, different feeds, different tasks.

A stadium says:

Look there.

Now.

And when the ball crosses the line, everyone understands why the room — or rather the entire concrete structure — has changed.

That night I understood it from the wrong side.

Or maybe, for ninety minutes, there was no wrong side.

I had gone to the stadium as a Galatasaray supporter.

I left with an Eskişehir story.

The distinction is useful.

Teams claim loyalty.

Places claim memory.

I may remember who I supported.

But I remember where I stood.

The crowd.

The pressure to shout.

The stranger who helped us.

The ridiculous joy when Eskişehir scored.

That belongs to the city now.

And perhaps this is why work travel mattered more to me than I realized at the time.

Work was the excuse to arrive.

Life happened after the meeting.

Some evening in Eskişehir, our manager pulled tickets from his pocket and said:

Come on, guys.

We are going to the match.

Nobody asked whether the story would still matter years later.

That is usually how the good ones begin.
