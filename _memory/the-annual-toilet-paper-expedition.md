In April 2009, I had a logistical solution to a social problem nobody had assigned me.

I did not like carrying toilet paper in the street.

So I bought roughly a year's worth at a time.

And I did it early in the morning.

Very early.

The logic was magnificent.

If carrying one package is embarrassing, carry enough to supply a small institution.

Just make sure fewer people are awake to witness it.

This is the kind of strategy that only makes sense when embarrassment has already taken control of planning.

The object itself is ordinary.

Everyone buys toilet paper.

Every house needs it.

Nobody who sees someone carrying it can reasonably conclude anything shocking.

Yet some objects feel private even when purchased publicly.

Toilet paper.

Underwear.

Certain medicines.

Personal-care products.

Things connected to the body's less glamorous requirements.

The supermarket places them under fluorescent lights.

The mind still whispers:

Everyone is looking.

They are not.

That is one of youth's recurring discoveries.

People are thinking about us far less than we imagine.

The stranger across the street is not building a theory about why you have twelve rolls.

They are late for work.

The cashier has scanned the same product a hundred times.

The neighbor has their own groceries.

But self-consciousness turns public space into theater.

You become both actor and audience.

Look at me carrying toilet paper.

How terrible.

The old solution was not psychological.

It was operational.

Change the time.

Increase the quantity.

Reduce frequency.

If the event is unpleasant, optimize it.

Once per year.

Dawn.

Done.

This says something funny about engineering instincts applied to emotional problems.

Instead of asking whether the embarrassment makes sense, redesign the supply chain.

Annual procurement.

Low-traffic delivery window.

Bulk inventory.

Minimum social exposure.

Problem solved.

Except now you need somewhere to store a year's worth of toilet paper.

The source does not mention storage, brand, number of packs, transport, or whether the system actually lasted exactly twelve months.

Those details should not be invented.

The phrase “enough for a year” is clearly the comic scale.

What matters is the impulse to minimize repetition.

Humans do this all the time.

We avoid discomfort by changing logistics rather than changing the belief beneath it.

Afraid of calling?

Send email.

Hate queues?

Go at opening time.

Embarrassed to ask?

Search online.

Do not like being seen buying something?

Buy in bulk.

These workarounds are not always bad.

Sometimes they are intelligent.

The question is whether they free us or quietly organize life around an unnecessary fear.

A workaround can preserve the problem indefinitely.

If I never carry toilet paper at a normal hour, I never get evidence that nobody cares.

Avoidance protects embarrassment from reality.

That is a useful psychological mechanism to notice.

We often think avoiding discomfort proves we handled it.

Sometimes it only prevents us from discovering that the discomfort was survivable.

Again, this is a tiny example.

There is no need to turn toilet paper into therapy.

The humor is enough.

But the pattern is general.

We build elaborate routines around small anxieties.

Always sit near the exit.

Never be first to speak.

Never wear that.

Never ask directly.

Shop when nobody is around.

After years, the routine feels like preference.

We forget it began as fear.

The 2009 entry preserves the origin before it could disappear into habit.

I was embarrassed.

Therefore:

annual toilet-paper expedition.

I like this memory because it belongs to an era before delivery made private shopping much easier.

Today almost anything can arrive at the door.

Bulk household supplies are normal online purchases.

Nobody needs to walk down the street carrying them unless they want to.

Technology has solved many embarrassments accidentally.

Not by making us more confident.

By removing the public moment.

This has advantages.

Convenience.

Privacy.

Time.

It also means some tiny social fears no longer get tested.

You can order without ever learning whether the fear was justified.

Modern life increasingly lets us avoid low-stakes awkwardness.

Food arrives.

Groceries arrive.

Customer service happens through chat.

Tickets are bought online.

Directions come from maps.

You can move through a city with fewer reasons to ask strangers anything.

That efficiency is wonderful.

It may also make ordinary public embarrassment feel rarer and therefore larger when it happens.

In 2009, carrying a ridiculous amount of toilet paper at sunrise was my personal compromise between necessity and visibility.

There is something cinematic about the image even though the source contains almost no scene.

Morning.

Street.

Bulk toilet paper.

A person hoping not to encounter civilization.

No dialogue needed.

The package itself explains the anxiety.

This kind of memory is valuable precisely because it is trivial.

Major events preserve themselves.

Nobody forgets graduation because they forgot to write an entry.

But strange shopping habits disappear unless recorded.

Years later they reveal personality better than formal milestones.

A biography says where you studied.

An archive says you tried to buy a year's worth of toilet paper before the city woke up.

Which one makes the person visible?

The second.

Minor embarrassment is deeply personal because it rarely appears in official memory.

We edit ourselves when telling important stories.

We want motives to sound coherent.

Choices to look mature.

Embarrassment resists that editing.

Why were you embarrassed?

I do not know.

Why buy a year's supply?

Because apparently that seemed easier.

Perfect.

Human behavior is full of decisions that were rational only inside a temporary emotional world.

The older self can laugh without humiliating the younger one.

He was solving the problem available to him.

Perhaps inefficiently.

Perhaps brilliantly.

At least he did not run out.

That last claim is not in the source, so I cannot actually promise it.

Maybe the annual strategy failed in November.

The archive does not say.

That uncertainty makes the whole operation even better.

Imagine building a one-year procurement plan and miscalculating.

Back to the street.

Daylight this time.

Disaster.

I will leave that imaginary catastrophe outside the memoir.

The real memory is already enough.

Once a year.

Morning.

As much toilet paper as dignity allowed.

There are worse systems.
