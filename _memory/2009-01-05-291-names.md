One evening, I decided that my own first name needed evidence.

I had always heard it as unmistakably male, but apparently not everyone did. The disagreement was small enough to ignore and irritating enough not to. So I opened Facebook and searched.

The result gave me 291 men with the same first name.

That number pleased me more than it should have.

I kept going. I found percentages, rankings, neighboring names, and enough numerical material to turn a minor annoyance into a small research project. What had begun as “Surely everyone hears this name the way I do” had acquired something resembling a dataset.

Of course, the numbers could not solve the real problem.

A name is both private and public. It belongs to one person every time somebody calls across a room, yet thousands of strangers may carry the same word. Each listener also brings previous classmates, relatives, actors, headlines, and associations into the sound.

The search page made that contradiction visible.

There they were: hundreds of people under one label.

None of them me.

I had gone online hoping to prove what kind of name I had.

Instead, I found out how little of a person a name can contain.
