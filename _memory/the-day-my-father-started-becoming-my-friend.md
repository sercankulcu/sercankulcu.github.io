title: "Come Here, Man"

We were sitting together after dinner when I noticed that something had changed between us. I cannot remember what we were talking about, and that is probably important. The subject was ordinary enough to disappear, but the feeling stayed.

My father made a joke, and I answered with another one. Years earlier, I would have been more careful with him. There had always been a small distance between us, not a cold one, but a clear line between father and son. That evening, the line seemed much lighter.

I looked at him and imagined that, at this rate, in a few years I might put my arm around his neck and say, “Come here, man.” The thought made me laugh. He looked at me as if he expected an explanation, but I only said, “Nothing.”

The change had probably been happening slowly for years. When I was younger, most of our conversations had a purpose: where I was going, what time I would return, how school was going, whether I needed money. Later, he began asking what I thought about things and actually waiting for the answer.

That was new. I also started seeing him as more than the person who always knew what to do. He could be tired, uncertain, stubborn, funny, or simply wrong, and strangely, that did not make him smaller in my eyes. It made him easier to understand.

One evening, while we were drinking tea, he told me a story from his younger years. For the first time, I pictured him at my age instead of as someone who had always been a father. For a few seconds, I saw a young man trying to understand his own life. Then he reached for his tea and became my father again.

After that, our conversations seemed easier. We could disagree without turning the room into a courtroom, and silence no longer felt dangerous. Sometimes there was simply nothing to say, and that was comfortable.

Affection between fathers and sons does not always arrive in beautiful sentences. Sometimes it sounds like practical advice: take a jacket, call when you get there, check the tires. Sometimes it appears as a joke about putting your father in a headlock.

That evening, I did not hug him or say anything serious. I only looked at him and imagined doing it one day. “Come here, man.” It was a ridiculous sentence, but it was also the first time I understood that my father was slowly becoming my friend.
