title: "The Day My Father Started Becoming My Friend"
date: 2010-07-21
tags:

There is a strange thing that can happen between a father and a son.

For years, the roles seem fixed.

He is the father.

You are the child.

Even when the child becomes an adult, the old geometry remains.

He asks where you are going.

You explain.

He gives advice.

You pretend not to need it.

He knows things because he has lived longer.

You know things because you have recently discovered the internet.

The balance feels permanent.

Then, slowly, without a formal announcement, something changes.

You begin talking differently.

The distance shortens.

The hierarchy softens.

One day you realize you are not only talking to your father.

You are talking to a man you actually like.

In July 2010, I wrote a short entry about my father.

I said that, for reasons I could not quite explain, we seemed to become more like friends as the years passed.

At the rate we were going, I joked, in another two or three years I would probably grab him around the neck, pull him toward me and say something like:

"Come here, man."

Then hug him.

The image makes me smile because it contains both affection and awkwardness.

Affection was there.

The problem was translation.

How does a son who has spent years relating to someone as "father" suddenly express closeness as if the man were also a friend?

There is no ceremony for this.

No moment when somebody says:

Congratulations. The parent-child relationship has now entered peer mode.

It happens in fragments.

A joke that would not have been possible five years earlier.

A conversation in which he tells you something not as instruction, but as opinion.

A moment when you disagree and neither of you treats disagreement as rebellion.

A story from his younger years that you finally understand because you are now old enough to imagine him as young.

That last part may be the biggest change.

Children do not naturally imagine their parents before parenthood.

A father appears to have always been a father.

He arrives in your consciousness already carrying authority, habits, responsibilities, weaknesses, and answers.

You do not see the boy.

The student.

The young man.

The person who once had no idea what his future would look like.

You know intellectually that he existed.

Emotionally, it takes longer.

Then adulthood begins to reveal him.

You encounter problems he must once have encountered.

Money.

Work.

Fatigue.

Responsibility.

Family.

Illness.

People depending on you.

Choices without perfect answers.

Suddenly, some of his old behavior becomes legible.

Not necessarily correct.

Legible.

There is a difference.

You begin seeing the person behind the role.

That is one of the privileges of growing older.

It can also be painful.

Authority becomes human.

The person you once assumed could solve everything turns out to have spent years improvising like everybody else.

Parents are experts at appearing more certain than they are because children need certainty.

Perhaps they need it too.

A household cannot operate if everyone constantly announces:

I have no idea what I am doing.

So adults make decisions.

Children interpret decisions as knowledge.

Later, you discover how much of adult life is educated guessing.

That discovery changes how you judge your parents.

Some judgments become harsher.

Others become softer.

You realize they were not abstract institutions.

They were people under constraints.

This does not excuse everything.

Understanding is not the same as absolution.

But context changes the shape of memory.

With my father, the change seems to have produced friendship.

The old entry does not explain exactly how.

That absence is useful.

It keeps me from inventing a cinematic turning point that probably never existed.

Relationships usually do not change that way.

They accumulate.

One conversation at a time.

A father and son may spend years speaking mainly through practical language.

Did you eat?

What time will you be back?

Did you pay that bill?

How is the car?

How is work?

Do you need anything?

Practical language can hide enormous affection.

Families are full of people who almost never say the thing directly.

They say:

Take a jacket.

They mean:

I worry about you.

They say:

Call when you get there.

They mean:

Your safe arrival matters to me.

They say:

Do you need money?

They mean several things at once.

Care is often disguised as logistics.

Maybe friendship appears when logistics are no longer enough.

When the son grows older, there is more room for conversation that does not need a purpose.

You can sit.

Talk.

Tease.

Complain.

Remember.

Discuss someone neither of you likes.

Laugh at the same thing.

Silence becomes easier too.

That matters.

Comfortable silence is one of the strongest signs that a relationship no longer needs constant maintenance.

You do not have to prove closeness.

You simply occupy the same room.

The joke about grabbing my father around the neck also says something about male affection.

Men are often given a limited vocabulary for tenderness.

Especially fathers and sons.

Affection becomes physical only through acceptable disguises.

A slap on the back.

A handshake held slightly longer.

A shoulder squeezed.

A mock wrestling move.

An arm around the neck.

The emotional message travels hidden inside roughness.

"Come here, man."

Then a hug.

As if the joke grants permission for the affection.

This is not universal, of course.

Some families say "I love you" easily.

Some fathers hug constantly.

Others do not.

My old entry suggests that, at least in my imagination then, openly affectionate closeness still needed humor as a bridge.

Humor is good at that.

It lowers the stakes.

You can say something serious while pretending not to.

If the moment becomes uncomfortable, the joke remains available as an exit.

Maybe that is why family humor can carry so much intimacy.

You can tease someone only after learning the boundaries of their pride.

You know what will make them laugh.

What will annoy them.

What is forbidden.

What is safe.

Shared humor is a map of accumulated familiarity.

My father and I must have been building more of that map.

The entry also mentioned that he had been paying closer attention to what he ate, drank, and how he lived because of a long-term health problem.

The younger me expressed that in the rough, joking language I often used then.

Under the joke was another change that happens as children become adults:

you begin worrying about your parents.

This reverses something fundamental.

For most of childhood, concern flows downward.

Parents monitor the child's health.

Food.

Sleep.

Safety.

Whereabouts.

Then the direction begins to change.

You notice what your father eats.

Whether he is tired.

Whether he is taking care of himself.

Whether he is driving too much.

Whether he is going to appointments.

Whether he is ignoring something he should not ignore.

At first, this feels almost illegitimate.

Who are you to tell your father what to do?

He was telling you what to do before you could tie your shoes.

But time is indifferent to hierarchy.

Bodies age.

Children become adults.

Responsibility starts moving in both directions.

That transition can be one of the hardest parts of growing up.

You do not simply become independent.

You begin becoming responsible for the people from whom you once wanted independence.

The word "adult" changes meaning.

At twenty, adulthood can mean:

Nobody can tell me what to do.

Later, it often means:

People I love now depend on me too.

Freedom and responsibility arrive together.

The friendlier relationship with my father may have been connected to this.

Friendship requires a certain recognition of equality.

Not equality of age.

Not equality of experience.

Equality of personhood.

You stop seeing only "my father."

You see him as someone with preferences, limits, fears, habits, stories, and contradictions.

At the same time, he may stop seeing only "my son."

He sees a grown man.

Perhaps not completely.

Parents probably never stop seeing earlier versions of their children.

That may be impossible.

But the ratio changes.

This is why conversations with parents can feel strange even when you are fully grown.

At work, people may trust you with serious decisions.

Students may listen to you.

Colleagues may ask for advice.

You may manage projects.

Then you visit home and someone asks whether you remembered to take a sweater.

Two identities collide.

Neither is false.

You are the competent adult.

You are also the child who once forgot the sweater.

Parents have archives.

They remember versions of us we can barely access.

This gives them a permanent advantage.

No matter how impressive you become, somewhere in their memory you are still five years old doing something ridiculous.

Perhaps friendship becomes possible when everyone learns to hold both truths at once.

Yes, I am your son.

Yes, I am also an adult.

Yes, you are my father.

Yes, you are also a man I can sit with as a friend.

The categories do not cancel one another.

They layer.

That layering is what makes long relationships so different from new ones.

A new friend knows who you are now.

A parent knows who you were before you became the person introducing yourself today.

That can feel restrictive.

It can also be deeply grounding.

There are people in life before whom you do not need to explain the entire backstory.

They were there.

Sometimes that is wonderful.

Sometimes you wish they would forget a few chapters.

But the continuity matters.

The 2010 entry captures a moment when I was apparently noticing that continuity becoming less hierarchical.

I did not say:

My relationship with my father has undergone a developmental transition toward reciprocal adult companionship.

Thank God.

I said, essentially:

We are getting more and more like buddies. At this rate I will soon put him in a headlock and hug him.

That is much better.

The sentence sounds alive.

It also preserves the age I was when I wrote it.

Old personal writing is valuable partly because it resists the polished version of memory.

If I wrote only from the present, I might make the relationship too neat.

I might describe gradual mutual respect, aging, tenderness, and changing family roles.

All true enough.

But the archive gives me the original texture:

"Come here, man."

That is how affection wanted to come out.

The difference between those versions matters.

We often rewrite our younger selves into people who already understood what we understand now.

They did not.

That is the point.

The younger me noticed something before he could fully explain it.

My father was becoming my friend.

That was enough.

Maybe parent-child relationships are healthiest when they are allowed to change.

A father who insists forever on being unquestioned authority eventually becomes distant.

A son who insists forever on being treated as a child never becomes fully responsible.

Both people have to surrender something.

The parent surrenders some authority.

The child surrenders some dependence.

What appears between them can be friendship.

Not exactly the same friendship you have with people your own age.

History makes it different.

There is gratitude in it.

Old arguments.

Unequal beginnings.

Family obligations.

A lifetime of roles.

But friendship nonetheless.

You can enjoy the person, not merely belong to the relationship.

That is significant.

Blood creates kinship.

It does not guarantee liking each other.

When affection grows beyond obligation, it deserves notice.

The old entry noticed it.

Very casually.

Perhaps that is another good sign.

The closeness was not announced.

It was becoming ordinary.

Years passed.

We talked differently.

We became more comfortable.

Somewhere in that process, the man I had always called "Dad" was becoming someone I could imagine pulling toward me with the rough affection usually reserved for an old friend.

There is something else hidden in all this.

Time.

In 2010, I joked that in another two or three years the friendship might progress to that ridiculous embrace.

The joke assumes time is abundant.

Parents will be there.

Children will grow.

Relationships will keep developing.

Usually we live as if this is guaranteed.

It cannot be otherwise.

You cannot spend every conversation thinking about mortality.

That would make ordinary affection impossible.

But growing older changes the background knowledge.

Parents age.

So do we.

The number of future conversations is finite even when nobody knows the number.

This makes small changes in a relationship more precious in retrospect.

A better conversation.

A joke.

A shared meal.

An unexpected moment of openness.

None seems historic at the time.

Together they become the relationship.

We often wait for dramatic opportunities to express affection.

There may not be one.

Most love is transmitted in ordinary moments.

Sit down.

Drink tea.

Call.

Ask.

Listen.

Laugh.

Tell the same old story again.

Maybe put your father in a playful headlock if your family constitution permits it.

The form matters less than the repetition.

A relationship becomes close because people keep returning to it.

That is the lesson I see now in an entry that did not pretend to contain a lesson.

My father and I were becoming friends.

I did not know exactly why.

Maybe adulthood made the distance smaller.

Maybe I was finally seeing him as a person.

Maybe he was seeing me that way too.

Maybe years simply gave us more shared language.

The archive does not say.

I should not improve the past by inventing certainty.

It gives me only a small truth:

As the years went by, we became closer.

At the time, I turned that truth into a joke.

Now I think the joke was another way of saying something much simpler.

I was glad.

Not everyone gets to discover that they like their father as much as they love him.

I was beginning to.
