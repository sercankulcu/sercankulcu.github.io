title: "The Cat Became Head of the Household"
date: 2010-07-21

There was a period when I was technically living at home but was hardly ever there.

I came back mostly to sleep.

That was enough to create a constitutional crisis.

In July 2010, I wrote that the title of "head of the household" should be transferred to the cat by majority vote.

My reasoning was simple.

The cat was the living creature spending the most time in the house.

If the title did not belong to the cat, who should get it?

Me?

The person who appeared mainly at night to sleep?

The case was weak.

The cat had occupancy.

Continuity.

Institutional memory.

Probably better knowledge of everything happening in the apartment than I did.

I had a key.

The cat had presence.

Presence should count for something.

The phrase "head of the household" has always sounded more serious than most households deserve.

It suggests authority.

Responsibility.

Decisions.

Somebody sitting at the top of an invisible organization chart.

In real homes, power is distributed much more strangely.

The person who pays the bills may not decide what is for dinner.

The person who owns the furniture may not control the sofa.

The person who thinks they run the house may discover that a four-kilogram animal has rejected that interpretation.

Cats are particularly good at exposing fictional authority.

You buy the sofa.

The cat decides which part belongs to the cat.

You buy the bed.

The cat determines how much of it remains available to you.

You establish feeding times.

The cat establishes the appeals process.

You close a door.

The cat immediately identifies this as a governance failure.

Human ownership and feline administration are different concepts.

This is probably why the old joke still works for me.

I may have been legally responsible for the place.

But operationally?

The cat had better uptime.

There is something revealing in the sentence "I only came home to sleep."

At the time, I wrote it casually.

Years later, it sounds like a summary of a particular stage of adult life.

You have a home.

But much of your life happens somewhere else.

Work.

Roads.

Friends.

Restaurants.

Offices.

Labs.

Meetings.

Errands.

You leave in the morning.

You return when the day is effectively finished.

The apartment becomes less of a place where life happens and more of a charging station for the person living in it.

Keys on the table.

Clothes off.

Sleep.

Wake.

Leave.

Repeat.

This can continue for months without feeling strange.

Then one day something makes you notice.

A plant grows while you are not watching.

Dust accumulates.

A neighbor knows more about the building than you do.

A cat has developed routines that have nothing to do with you.

You realize the home has a life during the hours you are absent.

That should be obvious.

It still feels surprising.

We often imagine places as frozen when we leave them.

The office exists when we are at work.

Home waits when we are away.

Of course it does not.

Sunlight moves across the floor.

The refrigerator turns on and off.

Sounds come from other apartments.

The street changes through the day.

Deliveries arrive.

Doors open.

People leave.

Animals listen.

The house continues.

A pet makes this continuity visible.

A cat occupies the hours you do not.

That creates a strange asymmetry.

You know the cat's evening.

The cat knows your absence.

You come home and assume nothing happened.

The cat has spent ten hours observing a world you never saw.

Maybe a bird sat on the window.

Maybe someone used the stairs repeatedly.

Maybe there was construction outside.

Maybe the patch of sunlight moved from one room to another.

Maybe nothing happened at all.

But the cat was there for it.

This makes pets silent archivists of domestic life.

They know routines.

Arrival times.

Departure sounds.

The exact noise of your key.

The difference between opening the refrigerator and opening a cabinet.

The sequence that means you are going to leave.

The sequence that means you are going to bed.

Humans tell ourselves stories about what animals understand.

We should be careful with that.

But behavior makes one thing obvious:

animals learn patterns.

And a person who lives on routine is easy to model.

I probably believed I was unpredictable.

The cat likely had a simpler assessment.

He leaves.

Eventually he comes back.

Food may be involved.

That is enough.

The older I get, the more I like the idea that a home is defined by presence rather than ownership.

Who is actually there?

Who knows the rhythms?

Who notices when something changes?

This applies to people too.

We sometimes confuse formal roles with lived roles.

Manager.

Parent.

Owner.

Leader.

Friend.

Title says one thing.

Presence may say another.

A person can officially be responsible and practically absent.

Another person can have no title and quietly hold everything together.

Organizations work this way.

Families work this way.

Friend groups work this way.

The person with authority is not always the person with influence.

The person with the name on the document is not always the person who knows what is happening.

The cat version is funny because the mismatch is extreme.

No salary.

No rent.

No paperwork.

No recognized legal authority.

Yet possibly the most consistent occupant of the home.

By the criterion of presence alone, the election was over.

This raises an uncomfortable question about work.

How much time away from home is normal?

There is no universal answer.

Some people love long workdays.

Some careers require periods of intense effort.

Some people live alone and prefer spending time elsewhere.

The issue is not the number itself.

The issue is whether the life you are living matches the life you think you have.

You can say:

My home matters to me.

Then spend almost no waking time there.

You can say:

My friends matter to me.

Then never see them.

You can say:

Health matters.

Then organize every day against it.

You can say:

I value free time.

Then give all of it away.

Values are easy to state.

Schedules are harder to argue with.

A calendar is often a more accurate autobiography than a self-description.

Where did the hours go?

That question can reveal more than what we say we care about.

In 2010, I did not write a work-life-balance essay.

I wrote a cat joke.

That is better in some ways.

The joke contains the evidence without pretending I had solved it.

I came home mostly to sleep.

Therefore the cat had a stronger claim to household leadership.

Case closed.

There is also something very early-adult about treating home as a low-maintenance base.

When you are younger, being out feels like life.

Being home can feel like inactivity.

The city is outside.

People are outside.

Possibility is outside.

Home is where you return after something happens.

Later, this often changes.

A quiet evening at home stops feeling like no plan.

It becomes the plan.

You begin appreciating things you once considered boring.

A clean kitchen.

A comfortable chair.

Cooking slowly.

Reading without going anywhere.

Fixing something.

Being in your own space while fully awake.

This is not necessarily aging into dullness.

It may be learning that life does not need an external venue to count.

There is a stage when you want to escape the house.

Another when you work hard to create a house you do not want to escape from.

That transition is interesting.

It changes what ownership means.

A home is not valuable because your name is attached to it.

It is valuable because you inhabit it.

Use it.

Rest in it.

Invite people into it.

Build routines in it.

Remember things in it.

Without presence, a home becomes storage with utilities.

The cat understood occupancy better than I did.

Cats are experts in making a place theirs through repetition.

Same windowsill.

Same chair.

Same corner.

Same afternoon route through the rooms.

Humans sometimes need more elaborate claims.

Lease.

Title deed.

Furniture.

Decoration.

The cat simply sits somewhere every day until the matter is settled.

There is confidence in that.

Perhaps too much confidence.

Anyone who has lived with a cat knows the experience of discovering that an object you thought belonged to you has been reassigned.

Laptop.

Chair.

Blanket.

Box.

Keyboard.

The distinction between "my things" and "things currently controlled by the cat" becomes administrative rather than philosophical.

A cardboard box can arrive in the house empty and be claimed faster than property law can respond.

Maybe that is what household leadership really is:

the ability to occupy first and resist appeals.

The old entry says the title should pass to the cat "by majority vote."

This raises another problem.

Who was voting?

If the household consisted mostly of me and the cat, the constitutional process may not have been robust.

Perhaps the cat had already secured one vote.

Mine made it unanimous.

Democracy survived.

There is a deeper reason I like small entries like this more now.

Big memories explain themselves.

Graduation.

First job.

Moving.

A relationship ending.

Travel.

Military service.

You know why they matter.

Small entries preserve the texture between milestones.

The cat being home more than me.

A strange phone call.

A bad cup of tea.

Running into a metro.

A useless-looking pair of gym gloves.

These details tell you how a life actually felt.

Biography is usually written in major events.

Memory is often built from minor ones.

A person can remember the year they changed jobs and still have no sense of what an ordinary Tuesday looked like.

Then a one-line joke survives.

Suddenly the Tuesday returns.

You were rarely home.

The cat was.

That was the arrangement.

Even without the exact apartment, exact schedule, or exact week, the sentence reconstructs the rhythm.

Busy.

Out.

Returning late.

Home as sleeping place.

Cat as permanent resident.

That is enough to create a room around the memory.

There is also affection hidden inside the joke.

You do not promote a cat to head of the household unless the cat is already part of how you imagine the household.

Pets occupy a strange category.

Not people.

Not objects.

Not merely animals in the abstract.

They become part of the emotional architecture of the home.

You speak to them.

Make decisions around them.

Notice their moods.

Build routines together.

Their absence changes the room.

Again, this should not be exaggerated into pretending animals are humans.

They are not.

That is part of what makes the relationship interesting.

Two different species build a shared routine without sharing language in the normal sense.

The arrangement is mostly behavior.

Expectation.

Repetition.

Trust.

Food, admittedly, helps.

A lot.

The cat may not have understood my work schedule.

But the cat understood that I returned.

There is something comforting in that.

Many relationships depend on explanation.

Pets often depend more on consistency.

Be there.

Come back.

Feed me.

Do not close that door.

Accept that this chair has changed ownership.

Simple terms.

Maybe the cat was a better household administrator because the requirements were clearer.

Human beings complicate homes with symbolic ideas.

Success.

Responsibility.

Status.

Who leads.

Who owns.

Who earns.

Animals reduce the question.

Are you here?

That is a useful question.

Not only for homes.

For relationships too.

Are you actually present?

Not nominally.

Not theoretically.

Not in the sentence "we should meet sometime."

Present.

Time is one of the clearest forms of attention.

Where we place it matters.

The 2010 version of me gave most of his waking time elsewhere.

There may have been perfectly good reasons.

The archive does not say.

I should not invent them.

It only preserves the observation:

The cat spent more time at home than I did.

That makes the humor stronger because the conclusion requires no extra facts.

The cat deserved the title.

I was a visiting executive.

Perhaps chairman emeritus.

Mostly unavailable.

The real chief executive was on the sofa.

Years later, I would phrase the lesson differently.

A home is not the place you pay for.

It is the place you repeatedly return your attention to.

If you are never there, somebody else may understand it better.

Sometimes that somebody has four legs.

So yes.

In July 2010, by majority vote, household leadership passed to the cat.

The transition was peaceful.

No objections were recorded.

I was probably at work.
