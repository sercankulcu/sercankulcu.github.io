My headphones had a cable so long that I could leave the computer and continue walking through the house.

Ten meters, more or less.

That length turned an ordinary pair of wired headphones into a small transportation system. I could go to the kitchen and make tea, open a window, cross the room, or briefly disappear into another part of the house without removing them.

The solution was primitive and excellent.

Today, freedom from headphones usually means no cable at all. Back then, freedom could mean simply having far more cable than a reasonable person expected. The source stayed fixed on the desk while I moved inside a circle drawn by wire.

The headphones themselves had already lasted for years. I no longer remembered exactly when I had bought them, which was a good sign. Objects that remain useful long enough eventually lose their purchase date and become part of the room.

They were not perfect. In summer they could make my ears hot, and the cable was obviously still a cable. But they did not need charging, pairing, or negotiation. Plug them in and the private world appeared.

Specifications usually say things like cable length.

Memory translates them.

Ten meters meant I could make tea without stopping the song.

For me, that was the real measurement.

## Near Ten Meters of Freedom

This is the kind of moment that survives by being oddly specific. The family part of the scene is not sentimental to me; it is practical, almost procedural, and that makes it more tender.

The room matters because everyone in it already knows rules nobody has bothered to write down. The rest of the day may be gone, but this little arrangement still has edges.
