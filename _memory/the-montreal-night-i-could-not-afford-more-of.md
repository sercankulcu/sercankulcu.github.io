In July 2009, I wrote down the economics of a night at Club Chez Paree in Montreal.

Saint Catherine Street.

A place I described as busy almost every day.

Entrance:

5 dollars.

Beer:

7.75 dollars.

A four-minute contact dance:

15 dollars.

With tips, I estimated that about 35 dollars could buy a very good night.

Then came the line that makes the entry personal.

While I was watching the stage, women came over offering private dances.

I turned them down because I did not have enough money.

I wrote that I would probably understand their value better once I returned to Turkey.

The younger voice is crude.

The memory beneath it is familiar:

travel enlarges desire while shrinking the wallet.

You are somewhere new.

Everything looks available.

Restaurants.

Bars.

Experiences.

Trips.

Tickets.

Small upgrades.

One more drink.

One more night.

Money suddenly becomes a visible limit on curiosity.

At home, you can say:

Another time.

While traveling, another time may not exist.

That changes the emotional weight of spending.

A 15-dollar decision is not merely 15 dollars.

It is also:

I am here now.

When will I be here again?

This is how travelers overspend.

Not because they lose arithmetic.

Because scarcity changes what arithmetic means.

Time becomes the expensive variable.

The Club Chez Paree entry is interesting because I did not overspend.

I refused.

The experience remained incomplete.

And incompleteness created memory.

We often think travel memories come from what we did.

Some come from what we could not afford to do.

The restaurant we only looked at.

The tour skipped.

The item not bought.

The extra night not booked.

The person who says no to an experience still experiences the no.

That moment becomes part of the city.

Montreal was not only what I saw.

It was also the things priced just beyond comfort.

The entry lists prices almost like field notes.

5.

7.75.

15.

35.

Travel memories often preserve money unusually well.

Perhaps because price is part of orientation.

Is this cheap?

Expensive?

Normal?

Can I do this twice?

What does a night cost here?

The visitor builds an economic map alongside the physical one.

A city becomes distances and prices.

Metro ticket.

Coffee.

Beer.

Hotel.

Taxi.

Club.

Numbers create the scale of life.

Years later, exact exchange rates disappear from memory.

But local prices can still recover the feeling.

I should not convert those 2009 dollars into today's money here.

The source does not need that modernization.

The emotional fact is enough:

15 dollars was enough for me to say no.

That locates the decision better than any inflation calculation.

Travel while financially comfortable is different from travel while calculating each evening.

Neither is inherently more authentic.

But limited money makes choices sharper.

You cannot have everything.

Therefore preferences become visible.

Beer or dance?

Tonight or tomorrow?

Taxi or walk?

Meal or event?

Every purchase consumes another possibility.

Economists call this opportunity cost.

Travelers call it:

Do I really need this?

The answer changes according to fatigue, loneliness, excitement, and how many days remain.

The old entry does not tell us how much money I had overall, how long I was in Montreal, whether I was there for work or study in that particular moment, or what else I chose to spend on.

Those details should not be reconstructed.

The small scene stands by itself.

Watching stage.

Approached.

Decline.

Money.

Later joke.

There is another layer that only appears with time.

Things we could not afford sometimes gain exaggerated value in memory.

Because we did not experience them, reality never had the chance to disappoint us.

The restaurant might have been average.

The concert seat uncomfortable.

The private dance forgettable.

But the unchosen possibility remains perfect because it remains imagined.

This is one reason missed opportunities can grow in memory while completed ones shrink.

Completed experiences become specific.

The music was too loud.

The food was cold.

The view was blocked.

Imagined experiences keep all options open.

They remain ideal.

The younger me predicted that once I returned to Turkey, I would appreciate what I had refused.

That is exactly how distance changes valuation.

While something is available, you compare price.

After it disappears, you compare absence.

Availability says:

15 dollars.

Absence says:

you may never be in that place again.

Different currencies.

Travel constantly teaches this lesson.

At home, abundance makes us selective.

Away, temporary access makes us sentimental.

A mediocre café can become beloved because it belonged to Paris.

A normal walk becomes important because it happened in Montreal.

The place lends value to ordinary events.

This can be irrational.

It can also be one of travel's gifts.

Attention rises because the environment is unfamiliar.

You notice signs.

Prices.

People.

Architecture.

A normal evening becomes recordable.

The Club Chez Paree entry is not a profound travel story.

That is why I like it.

It does not pretend Montreal transformed me spiritually.

It says:

here is the entrance price,

here is the beer price,

here is what four minutes costs,

I could not afford the extra experience.

That is honest travel.

Foreign cities are still made of budgets.

Even adventure has a price list.

There is also some self-mockery in refusing because of money and then imagining future regret.

I knew what was happening.

Present self:

Too expensive.

Future self:

Idiot.

Humans conduct this argument constantly.

Present self protects resources.

Future self accuses present self of missing life.

Neither is always right.

Spend everything in the name of experience and future self may have a different complaint.

Save everything and memory may feel thin.

Wisdom probably lives somewhere between.

Spend deliberately on things you will actually value.

Not because travel creates a moral duty to consume.

Not because every opportunity must be seized.

Because money is a tool for choosing which experiences matter enough.

The source does not tell me whether refusing was a mistake.

I cannot settle the argument now.

Maybe it was exactly the right financial decision.

The joke only says I expected nostalgia to make it look more valuable later.

That prediction itself is enough.

Many old decisions become funny because the cost disappears but the missed possibility remains visible.

You no longer feel the 15 dollars leaving your pocket.

You can still remember saying no.

Memory has terrible accounting.

It writes off expenses faster than regrets.

That is why old travel stories often make us sound more adventurous than we were.

The budget lines disappear.

The city remains.

I prefer keeping the numbers.

They make the story honest.

Montreal, 2009.

Five dollars to enter.

7.75 for a beer.

Fifteen for four minutes.

And at least one experience I left on the other side of the budget.
