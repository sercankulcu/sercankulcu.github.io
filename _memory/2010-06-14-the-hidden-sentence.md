She suggested going for a walk, and I agreed even though I did not really feel like going.

That should have been the first warning.

My spoken sentence was “Okay.” The hidden sentence was, “I would rather stay home, but I do not want to disappoint you.” Only one of those sentences reached the other person.

Later, after we had walked for a while, she suggested going somewhere busier. I agreed again and said we could stop by home first so she could change clothes. My reason was practical: I thought the weather might get colder.

She heard criticism.

“If my clothes are so bad, why did you not say so before?”

I had meant temperature. She had heard appearance. The distance between those two meanings was large enough to turn an ordinary walk into an argument.

At the time, I treated this as proof that men and women communicated differently. Years later, the simpler explanation is harder to avoid: I was being indirect too.

I had not said that I did not feel like walking.

I had not said that I was worried about the cold.

Then I was surprised when another person failed to receive the explanations that had remained entirely inside my own head.

That is the dangerous confidence of intention. We know why we said something, so we assume the reason must be visible.

It rarely is.

Sometimes the sentence that prevents the argument is not the clever one.

It is the hidden sentence said aloud.
