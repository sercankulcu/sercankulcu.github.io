In November 2010, I wrote a short piece called "Nöbet" — night watch.

It was not an explanation of military service.

It was not a complaint list.

It was not even really a story in the conventional sense.

It was a room.

A night.

A countdown.

Outside, there was a frightening silence.

Cold.

A sun that seemed unable to decide whether to rise.

Everyone else was in the last part of their sleep.

Inside, I was alone between four walls.

There was music coming from the next room.

Huge fans were humming.

I had gone through the whole night without cigarettes.

My mind was in my village, where for the first time I had not been able to go.

And at the end I wrote two numbers.

Four hours until I could return to life.

Sixty-two days until I could return to living like a human being.

That was the emotional mathematics of the night.

Four hours.

Sixty-two days.

Military service teaches counting in a way ordinary life rarely does.

Not arithmetic.

Waiting.

You count the days remaining.

Then the weeks.

Then sometimes the hours.

A future date becomes physically important.

It is not simply written on a calendar.

You carry it.

The number follows you into breakfast, into work, into sleep, into conversations.

Sixty-two.

Then sixty-one.

Then sixty.

Progress measured not by what has happened, but by how much less remains.

There is something strange about living toward subtraction.

Most of ordinary life is additive.

Another year of experience.

Another trip.

Another project.

Another memory.

Military countdowns reverse the direction.

Success means the number getting smaller.

The perfect result is zero.

That changes how time feels.

A normal day can be valuable because something happened in it.

A countdown day can feel valuable simply because it is over.

One less.

That is enough.

The old entry captures this better than any later explanation I could invent.

"Four hours until life."

"Sixty-two days until living like a human being."

The exaggeration belongs to the moment.

Of course, I was alive.

Of course, I was still a person.

But exhaustion and lack of control change vocabulary.

When your time is not fully yours, freedom begins to look like life itself.

This may be one of the strongest effects of compulsory routines.

Ordinary freedoms become visible.

Sleeping when you want.

Going somewhere without permission.

Seeing family.

Smoking a cigarette if you choose to smoke.

Leaving a room.

Choosing what to wear.

Deciding how to spend a weekend.

At home, these things are too ordinary to appreciate.

Remove them and suddenly they become enormous.

Freedom is often most noticeable in its absence.

The night watch made that absence especially sharp.

Everyone sleeping while you remain awake creates a particular loneliness.

Daytime loneliness is social.

People are moving around somewhere.

Phones ring.

Cars pass.

Shops are open.

Even if you are alone, the world feels available.

Night watch is different.

The sleeping world feels closed.

You are awake outside the normal rhythm.

There are lights, walls, equipment, maybe distant sounds — but ordinary life is temporarily inaccessible.

The old entry says there was a "deathly silence" outside.

That is not objective weather reporting.

It is mood.

The kind of silence that becomes noticeable because you are forced to stay awake long enough to hear it.

Then there was the cold.

An ayaz.

The dry, cutting cold that belongs especially well to pre-dawn hours.

Cold changes waiting.

Four hours in warmth and four hours in cold are not the same duration.

This is one of the lies clocks tell.

Time is uniform only mechanically.

Human time stretches and contracts according to discomfort, fear, boredom, company, anticipation.

A minute before something good can disappear instantly.

A minute waiting for something painful can become architecture.

Night duty has its own time zone.

The hands move.

You do not believe them.

The entry also mentions a sun uncertain whether to rise.

That image matters because dawn is not only light in this context.

It is release.

The sun is part of the countdown.

If morning arrives, the watch ends.

If the watch ends, some version of normal life resumes.

So the sky becomes personal.

You watch brightness not as weather, but as evidence.

Almost there.

The darkness is losing.

This is why people who work nights understand dawn differently.

It is not merely beautiful.

It is functional.

It means shift change.

Coffee.

Bed.

Movement.

Relief.

For someone on watch, sunrise can be administrative salvation.

The original piece says everyone else was in the last moments of sleep.

That contrast made the room more isolating.

Their night was ending in sleep.

Mine was ending awake.

They would wake and begin the day.

I was waiting for the right to stop guarding it.

There is no need to dramatize this beyond what the source gives.

The power is already in the asymmetry.

Sleeping people and the person watching.

That arrangement is ancient.

Someone always stays awake.

A sailor.

A guard.

A nurse.

A parent.

A driver.

A soldier.

Night duty is one of the oldest forms of responsibility.

Its emotional cost is also ancient.

You are awake so somebody else does not have to be.

Sometimes that feels meaningful.

Sometimes it only feels unfair.

Often both.

The entry uses the phrase "fake obedience."

That catches another part of military life.

Outward compliance and inward resistance can coexist perfectly.

You stand where you are supposed to stand.

Do what you are supposed to do.

Answer correctly.

Appear calm.

Inside, you may be counting every remaining day.

This is not necessarily rebellion.

It is psychological separation.

The body is present.

The future self is elsewhere.

Sixty-two days away.

People survive rigid environments partly by creating this internal distance.

You cooperate with the present while emotionally investing in the end.

It can become almost automatic.

Yes, commander.

Yes, understood.

Sixty-two.

Yes, I'll do it.

Sixty-two.

When does the watch end?

Four hours.

How long until all of this ends?

Sixty-two days.

Numbers become private doors.

You cannot leave yet.

But you know where the exit is.

That knowledge matters.

Indefinite hardship is much harder than finite hardship.

Even a long countdown provides shape.

There is an end.

Maybe not today.

But it exists.

This is why people ask sentences like:

How many days left?

The answer is not informational only.

It is emotional support.

Sixty-two means you can imagine sixty-one.

Then sixty.

The future is approaching whether you perform well or badly.

Time itself is doing part of the work.

There is comfort in that.

The old entry also mentions giant fans.

I like that detail.

It keeps the piece from becoming abstract.

There was machinery.

Noise.

A room that had a physical sound.

Memory often survives through hums.

An air conditioner.

A refrigerator.

A fluorescent light.

A computer fan.

A generator.

Machinery does something peculiar at night.

During the day, human activity covers it.

At night, mechanical sounds become enormous.

They fill the absence.

The fan does not care that you are tired.

It continues.

Constant.

Impersonal.

This can be comforting or maddening depending on the hour.

In the entry, the fans accompany the night almost like another guard.

They do not sleep either.

Humans project companionship onto repetitive sound when there is nobody else available.

You begin hearing rhythm.

Patterns.

The machine becomes part of the room's personality.

Years later, I do not need to know the exact model of the fan.

I remember the fact that it was loud enough to enter the writing.

That means it belonged to the night.

Then there was music from the next room.

A line from a song appeared in the original entry.

I will not reproduce lyrics here.

What matters is that the music was about goodbye and absence.

Of course it was.

Night duty is almost designed to make certain songs more powerful.

You are tired.

Alone.

Away from where you would prefer to be.

Time moves badly.

Then music arrives.

A normal song can become dangerous under those conditions.

It does not need to be profound.

Context does the work.

This is something I knew well in those years.

Certain songs were not background.

They changed the room.

They gave shape to whatever you were already feeling.

When you are happy, you barely hear sad lyrics.

When you are waiting to go home, every goodbye becomes personal.

The next detail is cigarettes.

A whole night without them.

That line has more meaning when placed beside the rest of my archive from that period.

Smoking was deeply embedded in my daily life.

There were years when cigarettes were companion, routine, interruption, excuse, reward.

So a cigarette-free night on duty was not a neutral health achievement in the way I described it then.

It was deprivation inside deprivation.

Another small choice removed.

I would not romanticize smoking now.

The fact that dependence could make one night feel harder is not evidence of friendship with cigarettes.

It is evidence of dependence.

That distinction is clearer with distance.

But to rewrite the old feeling as though I did not care about cigarettes would be dishonest.

I cared.

A lot.

And on that night, their absence was part of the discomfort.

This is one of the useful things about archives.

They preserve versions of us we might otherwise clean up.

Today, it would be easy to say:

Obviously it was good not to smoke.

Yes.

That is medically and practically true.

It is not the same as saying the 2010 version of me enjoyed being forced not to.

He did not.

Personal history becomes false when we replace old emotion with current judgment.

Better to hold both.

Then: I wanted the cigarette.

Now: I understand the dependence differently.

Both belong.

The strongest personal detail, though, may be the village.

My mind was in my village because it was the first time I had not been able to go.

The source does not explain the occasion in detail.

It contains a phrase associated with Bayram mornings — children being told to wake early.

That suggests the emotional weight of a holiday period and family tradition.

I do not need to invent who was there, what breakfast looked like, or what exactly I would have done.

The important fact is absence.

For the first time, a familiar return did not happen.

That is how separation often becomes real.

Not because you are geographically far away in the abstract.

Because a ritual arrives without you.

A holiday.

A birthday.

A wedding.

A family meal.

Something repeats every year.

Then one year your place in the repetition is empty.

That hurts differently from ordinary distance.

Routine is evidence of belonging.

"I always go."

"I am always there."

Then:

Not this time.

Military service interrupted more than personal freedom.

It interrupted continuity.

The village continued.

The holiday continued.

Morning came.

Children woke.

People visited.

Food was eaten.

Conversations happened.

And I was elsewhere listening to fans.

This is one of the hardest facts of absence:

The place you miss does not freeze until you return.

Life continues without you.

That realization is uncomfortable.

It is also part of adulthood.

When we are young, home can feel permanent and waiting.

Leave for university.

Return.

Leave for work.

Return.

The room may still exist.

People may still be there.

Traditions repeat.

It creates the illusion that home is a fixed point and we are the only moving object.

Then eventually you miss something important.

A holiday.

A season.

A family event.

You understand that home has its own time.

You are not the center of it.

That does not reduce belonging.

It makes belonging more fragile.

Perhaps that is why people become more protective of family rituals with age.

When you are always able to attend, tradition can feel boring.

When you cannot, it becomes precious.

Again, absence reveals value.

The night-watch entry does this repeatedly.

Sleep is valuable because I cannot sleep.

Morning is valuable because it has not arrived.

Cigarettes are valuable to the dependent smoker because they are unavailable.

The village is valuable because I cannot be there.

Civilian life is valuable because sixty-two days remain.

The entire piece is built from missing things.

And yet nothing explicitly catastrophic happens.

That matters.

Some of the hardest memories are not disasters.

They are long periods of wanting to be elsewhere.

No dramatic event.

Just displacement.

Waiting.

This kind of discomfort is difficult to explain afterward because once it ends, the mind compresses it.

Sixty-two days become a sentence.

The four-hour watch becomes an anecdote.

But while you are inside those hours, they are full size.

This is where writing helps.

The old entry preserves scale.

It tells me that four hours felt "huge."

It tells me sixty-two days was the distance to humane life.

Those words may be exaggerated.

Their function is accurate.

They restore emotional proportion.

Memory is unfair to duration.

A ten-minute event can dominate a year.

A two-month period can disappear almost completely.

Written fragments fight that compression.

They say:

No, this night mattered while it was happening.

You were cold.

You were awake.

You were counting.

You wanted to be in your village.

You wanted morning.

I also notice that the piece was written on November 16, 2010, but describes a pre-dawn atmosphere.

That creates the feeling that the memory was close to the event.

Not polished over decades.

The language was immediate.

That is valuable because later retellings tend to become cleaner.

We create themes.

Lessons.

Narrative arcs.

The original experience rarely had one.

It was simply:

Outside.

Cold.

Silence.

Inside.

Fans.

Music.

No cigarettes.

Village.

Four hours.

Sixty-two days.

This is enough.

Maybe good personal writing should sometimes resist improvement.

Do not add a dramatic commander.

Do not invent conflict.

Do not create a heroic moment.

The real story is waiting.

Waiting is an event when you have no choice but to do it.

There is also something almost comic about how military service turns grown adults into countdown specialists.

Numbers become greetings.

How many?

What is your dawn?

How long left?

These questions replace ordinary biography.

You may know somebody's remaining days before you know where they studied.

Shared captivity, even in a relatively ordinary compulsory-service sense, creates its own vocabulary.

Everyone understands the arithmetic because everyone wants the same zero.

This gives countdowns a social function too.

Someone with fewer days becomes evidence.

He will leave.

Therefore leaving is real.

Someone with many days becomes painful to look at.

Poor guy.

The hierarchy of remaining time may matter almost as much as rank in casual conversation.

I had sixty-two.

Not close enough to stop counting.

Not far enough to lose hope.

A number with shape.

Today, sixty-two days is a little over two months.

In normal life, two months can vanish.

A semester begins and suddenly two months are gone.

A project starts.

Summer passes.

Children grow.

You look at the calendar and ask where the time went.

But when the goal is the end of a constrained period, two months can feel architectural.

You can walk around inside them.

This is another paradox of age.

Later life accelerates.

Compulsory waiting slows it down.

Perhaps we need both experiences to understand time.

The fast years teach us not to waste it.

The slow nights teach us how heavy it can be.

The night eventually ended.

That statement is obvious because I am writing years later.

But the original entry did not need to say it.

Morning was four hours away.

It arrived.

The sixty-two days also ended.

Zero came.

Civilian life resumed.

This is the strange fate of every countdown.

The number that dominates your mind eventually becomes irrelevant.

Nobody asks you your remaining days after the service is over.

You stop checking.

The calendar loses its authority.

The great number reaches zero and disappears.

What remains is not the arithmetic.

It is the memory of caring about it so much.

That is why "62" now feels less like a number and more like an atmosphere.

Cold.

Fans.

No cigarettes.

A village elsewhere.

A sun not yet willing to rise.

Perhaps adulthood is full of numbers like this.

Exam scores.

Days until graduation.

Months until a wedding.

Weeks until a birth.

Loan payments.

Hospital dates.

Retirement years.

We give numbers emotional jobs.

A number becomes fear.

Hope.

Freedom.

Pressure.

Then life passes through it and the number becomes ordinary again.

The date you waited for becomes a date you barely remember.

This is why keeping the original number matters.

Sixty-two.

Not "a couple of months."

Sixty-two is how it felt then.

Precise waiting.

And four hours.

Not "until morning."

Four hours is more painful.

Specificity restores the watch.

If I read the old entry now, I do not feel a need to turn military service into a grand coming-of-age story.

It does not need that burden.

Some experiences matter because they teach you something.

Others matter because they expose ordinary things.

That night exposed freedom.

Sleep.

Home.

Routine.

Choice.

It made them visible by taking them away temporarily.

And perhaps the most honest lesson is not that I became stronger.

It is that I wanted to go home.

There is no shame in that.

People sometimes rewrite difficult periods to make them sound noble.

"I learned discipline."

"I discovered myself."

"I grew."

Maybe.

But sometimes a cold night is just a cold night.

Sometimes the person on duty is not having a transformation.

He is looking at the clock.

Four hours.

He is counting days.

Sixty-two.

He is thinking about the village.

He is listening to machines.

He wants a cigarette.

He wants sunrise.

He wants his own life back.

That is enough of a story.

Maybe more than enough.

Because freedom is one of those things that sounds abstract until you meet it as a countdown.

Then it becomes very simple.

Four hours until morning.

Sixty-two days until home.
