By 2010, my laptop no longer behaved like a machine that could simply be switched on and used. It had conditions.

The left speaker worked when it wanted to, so I moved the audio balance toward the right and accepted that stereo sound was now optional. Sometimes the laptop refused to start while the battery was installed, so I removed the battery before turning it on. This worked perfectly until I forgot the battery was missing and unplugged the power cable.

The machine shut down instantly.

A workaround had created a new problem, which is one of the oldest traditions in computing.

Then the graphics driver joined the negotiation. It was a driver I had used for years, but suddenly installing it caused the computer to restart after ten or fifteen minutes. My final technical solution was to stop installing the graphics driver.

This was not repair in the traditional sense. It was diplomacy.

New computers are expected to obey. Old computers develop local laws. One USB port should not be touched. A cable must remain at a certain angle. A particular program should not be opened immediately after startup. The owner eventually learns the rules so well that the machine appears usable only to that person.

Mine had become like that. To an outsider, the laptop was clearly broken. To me, it was fine as long as I remembered all the exceptions.

Speaker? Use the right side.

Battery? Remove it.

Power cable? Do not forget what you just did.

Graphics driver? Absolutely not.

The laptop had stopped being portable, stopped being properly stereo, and stopped using software that was supposedly required for its own hardware. Yet it still worked well enough for me to continue.

There is a point in the life of an old device when “working correctly” becomes less important than “staying alive.” My laptop had crossed that point.

We no longer had a user-device relationship.

We had an agreement.
