During military service, everyone was given a whistle.

I never used mine.

Long after that period had ended, I found it again and did what should probably have been done on the first day: I blew into it.

Nothing happened.

The whistle did not work.

My first thought was that I should have tested it when it was issued and returned it as defective. The idea of making a warranty complaint inside military bureaucracy pleased me far more than it should have.

Then I put the whistle away again.

A broken whistle has almost no practical value. If I had bought it from a shop, the problem would have been easy: return it, replace it, or throw it away. But the object had already acquired another job.

It remembered something.

That is how souvenirs escape normal rules. An old ticket no longer gets you anywhere. A key may open nothing. A badge may have no official meaning left. Their usefulness disappears, yet throwing them away can feel strangely final.

The whistle was even better because it had failed at its only real function. It had survived an entire period of my life without being used, then returned years later simply to reveal that it may never have worked at all.

I kept it.

It could not make a sound.

It had already said enough.
