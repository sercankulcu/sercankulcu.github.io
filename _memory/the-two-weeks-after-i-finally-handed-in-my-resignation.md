In May 2009, I wrote about a very specific kind of relief.

The relief that comes after resigning from a job when the resignation has been postponed for too long.

In my case, military service was approaching.

That complicated the decision.

There were reasons to wait.

Compensation.

The awkwardness of taking another short-term job before leaving.

The uncertainty of exactly when one chapter would end and the next would begin.

So the resignation letter could remain in your pocket while your mind slowly deteriorated around it.

That is how I described the period.

Then military service became close enough that postponement no longer made sense.

You handed in the letter.

And suddenly, for roughly two weeks until reporting for service, there was an extraordinary lightness.

The strange part is that the future had not become easier.

Military service was still ahead.

The uncertainty had not disappeared.

My working life had not become more secure.

In one sense, I had fewer options than before.

Yet I felt lighter.

That is what delayed decisions do to us.

We often assume the burden comes from the outcome.

Sometimes the burden comes from keeping the decision open.

Should I resign?

Not yet.

Maybe next week.

What about the money?

What if plans change?

Should I wait?

Every morning the same unresolved question has to be loaded into memory again.

Nothing happens, but energy is consumed.

Indecision has maintenance cost.

This is easy to underestimate because it produces no visible work.

You do not submit anything.

You do not move.

You do not announce a change.

From outside, nothing is happening.

Inside, the question runs constantly.

That is why making a difficult decision can feel good even when the decision leads toward something unpleasant.

Certainty reduces cognitive noise.

The path may be bad.

At least it is now a path.

Before that, you are standing at several doors, reopening the same argument every day.

The resignation letter in the old entry is almost a physical symbol of this.

Carrying the solution.

Not using it.

A piece of paper that could change the situation but remained in a pocket because timing still felt negotiable.

There are many adult equivalents.

An email written but not sent.

A form completed but not submitted.

A conversation rehearsed but postponed.

A ticket not booked.

A relationship not ended.

A job application not withdrawn.

A decision can exist privately long before it becomes real.

That in-between state is exhausting because you experience both lives at once.

You remain where you are while mentally preparing to leave.

You keep working while imagining the last day.

You continue making plans you know may not matter.

You participate and detach simultaneously.

No wonder the mind becomes tired.

The younger version of me connected this especially to men approaching military service.

That was the context of the entry.

The military deadline created an unusual kind of forced clarity.

There was a date beyond which ordinary postponement would no longer work.

This is one thing deadlines do well.

They end philosophical freedom.

Before the deadline:

Maybe.

After:

Do something.

We complain about deadlines because they restrict us.

They also rescue us from endless optionality.

Too many possibilities can become paralysis.

Once the boundary becomes real, the question changes from “What should I eventually do?” to “What must I do now?”

Action becomes easier.

This does not mean every deadline is healthy.

Artificial urgency can create bad decisions.

But genuine external limits often reveal which choices were never going to remain available.

Military service provided exactly that kind of boundary.

The job could not simply continue indefinitely under the same conditions.

At some point the letter had to leave the pocket.

Then came the two weeks.

I called the feeling “the unbearable lightness of resigning.”

That phrase obviously borrowed literary drama for a very practical employment event.

But the exaggeration was emotionally accurate.

The relief was not because unemployment had become attractive.

It was because the internal negotiation had stopped.

This is a pattern I recognize in many transitions.

Before moving house, the uncertainty is worse than the boxes.

Before giving notice, the imagined conversation is worse than the conversation.

Before an exam, waiting can be worse than sitting down to take it.

Before surgery, anticipation can be worse than the procedure.

Before a difficult phone call, the hour before dialing can contain more anxiety than the call itself.

Action converts imagination into reality.

Reality is often narrower than imagination.

The mind can simulate hundreds of bad outcomes.

The actual event gets only one.

That is why action can reduce fear even when the outcome remains difficult.

Once the resignation is submitted, several questions disappear immediately.

Should I submit it?

When?

How?

What will happen when I do?

Those loops terminate.

New questions appear, of course.

But the old ones stop consuming bandwidth.

There is also something psychologically clean about having a countdown.

Two weeks.

Not forever.

Not “sometime.”

A short defined interval between two identities.

Employee.

Then soldier.

The source does not describe what I did during those two weeks, how I spent the days, or whether I was still technically working through a notice period.

I will not invent the schedule.

The only thing worth preserving is the feeling.

Lightness.

That is enough.

Transition periods often become memorable not because of events but because ordinary obligations temporarily lose their authority.

The future has already claimed you.

The present cannot fully hold you.

You continue moving through familiar places with an expiration date attached.

Last Monday.

Last commute.

Last lunch.

Last time opening this door as an employee.

Even if you do not consciously label every moment, endings change the texture of repetition.

Things that irritated you yesterday may become irrelevant today.

The annoying process?

Not my problem much longer.

The office politics?

Good luck.

The project timeline?

I will be elsewhere.

That can produce a dangerous amount of serenity.

People sometimes become their happiest at work immediately after resigning.

The institution has lost much of its psychological power.

Evaluation matters less.

Future promotion no longer matters.

Small conflicts shrink.

This can make resignation feel like discovering the correct distance from a problem.

The building is the same.

You are different because the future has changed.

Again, the 2009 entry does not give me enough detail to say whether I personally behaved this way at work.

It supports only the broader feeling of relief.

So the rest belongs to reflection, not reconstructed scene.

The key distinction is between leaving and deciding to leave.

Leaving can be difficult.

Deciding can be liberating.

That is why people sometimes remain too long in situations they already know they will exit.

They imagine postponement is preserving security.

Maybe financially it is.

Psychologically it can be charging rent.

Each extra day contains the same unresolved argument.

There is no universal rule that says decide quickly.

Some decisions deserve time.

Resignation can affect income, family, career, housing, insurance, and many other things.

Careful delay is different from repetitive delay.

Careful delay gathers information.

Repetitive delay merely reruns fear.

That distinction is useful.

Ask:

What new information am I waiting for?

If there is an answer, waiting may be rational.

If there is no answer, perhaps the decision is already made and only the discomfort remains.

In 2009, military service eventually removed the luxury of endless delay.

The letter was submitted.

The psychological weather changed.

For approximately two weeks, I experienced the unusual peace that follows a decision whose consequences have not arrived yet.

That is a narrow window.

Afterward, a new reality begins.

Beforehand, uncertainty dominates.

In between, there can be lightness.

Perhaps that is why the memory survived.

Not because resigning was heroic.

Because for a short time, one large unresolved question had finally stopped asking itself.
