In November 2009, I wrote that God seemed to enjoy playing games with me.

That was my explanation for two things.

First, during the previous month, I had found four cars I really liked.

Every single one had been sold the day before I got there.

Second, I had met a woman through friends, liked her, decided I should finally talk to her properly, and then somehow managed not to cross paths with her for five months.

That was enough evidence for me.

Clearly, the universe was involved.

This is how coincidence feels when you are young and impatient.

One unlucky event is bad timing.

Two become suspicious.

Three begin to suggest intent.

By four, you are no longer dealing with probability.

You are dealing with a conspiracy.

The cars were especially good evidence.

Not because cars are important in some grand philosophical sense.

Because wanting to buy something puts your attention into a very specific mode.

You search.

Compare.

Imagine.

You begin owning the thing before you actually own it.

This one looks right.

The color works.

The price is possible.

Maybe this is it.

You picture yourself driving it.

Then you call.

Sold yesterday.

Fine.

Unlucky.

You continue.

Another one appears.

Better, perhaps.

Maybe the first one was not meant to be.

That is how quickly people invent meaning when disappointed.

The second car seems almost like compensation.

Then:

Sold yesterday.

Now the first sale changes retroactively.

What looked random begins to look like a pattern.

By the fourth time, I apparently had enough material to accuse the divine.

This is one of the funniest things about human pattern recognition.

We are extremely good at finding structure in events that may have no shared cause at all.

That ability is useful.

Patterns help us learn.

They also make us slightly ridiculous.

A traffic light turns red three times in a row and suddenly the city has a personal problem with us.

The supermarket line we choose stops moving.

Of course it does.

The one time we leave the umbrella at home, it rains.

Naturally.

The car sells one day before we arrive.

Again?

Come on.

The world becomes an opponent because an opponent is easier to understand than randomness.

Randomness has no personality.

You cannot argue with it.

You cannot insult it.

You cannot ask what its problem is.

But if life is "playing with you," at least you have a story.

Someone is responsible.

Even if the someone is metaphysical.

The second half of the old entry is even better.

I had met a woman through a group of friends.

I found her attractive.

At some point I decided:

All right.

Next time I see her, I will talk to her.

Then I did not see her for five months.

Five months.

This is excellent comic timing.

Before the decision, presumably, she existed in the social environment normally.

After the decision, she vanished.

Nothing in the archive says why.

Maybe schedules changed.

Maybe the friend group stopped meeting in the same configuration.

Maybe pure chance was enough.

The source does not support an explanation.

That uncertainty is part of the story.

Because from the inside, the sequence feels too neat.

See person.

Do nothing.

See person again.

Do nothing.

Eventually decide to act.

Person disappears from the known universe.

That is almost a law of romantic hesitation.

Opportunity seems abundant while you are postponing action.

The moment you decide to use it, the supply collapses.

This is not actually how the universe works.

It is how regret narrates time.

If I had spoken earlier, perhaps the five-month absence would not matter.

Because I waited, the absence became meaningful.

This is one reason delay creates emotional drama.

The external event may be ordinary.

The internal context makes it painful.

If you do not care about buying a car, a sold listing means nothing.

If you have not decided to talk to someone, not seeing them means nothing.

Desire changes the weight of coincidence.

That may be the real connection between the two events.

Cars and romance have very little in common.

Except that in both cases I had reached the stage where I wanted something to move from possibility into reality.

And in both cases, reality moved first.

The car was gone.

The woman was absent.

That is why the entry works as one joke.

It is not really about God.

It is about timing.

Timing is one of those things we only notice when it is bad.

Good timing feels natural.

You arrive and the car is still available.

You meet someone and the conversation happens.

You apply and the position is open.

You call and the person answers.

Nothing dramatic.

Life appears cooperative.

Bad timing calls attention to itself.

One day late.

One minute late.

Five months without crossing paths.

Then we begin asking larger questions.

Why now?

Why me?

Why always?

"Always" is an especially dangerous word.

Usually it means:

I remember the failures more vividly.

The four cars sold one day before my arrival were memorable precisely because the timing was so neat.

How many cars did I see that had been sold three days earlier?

How many were still available but I rejected?

How many listings disappeared before I noticed?

Those events do not become stories.

Pattern memory is selective.

It keeps the suspiciously elegant examples.

This is why anecdotes can make chance look more organized than it is.

The same is true of people.

We remember the person we kept almost meeting.

The missed phone call.

The train we nearly caught.

The relationship that might have begun if one evening had gone slightly differently.

Counterfactuals are addictive.

The real event is small.

The imagined alternatives are infinite.

Maybe if I had called one day earlier, I would have bought that car.

Maybe if I had spoken to her sooner, something would have happened.

Maybe.

That word can consume a surprising amount of mental energy.

Youth is full of maybes because so much of life still appears open.

Career.

City.

Relationships.

Money.

Identity.

Nothing feels fully settled.

Every decision seems capable of branching into a different life.

A car is not only transportation.

It might change where you go.

Who you see.

How you feel.

A conversation is not only a conversation.

It might become a relationship.

Or not.

When futures feel numerous, missed opportunities feel larger.

Age changes some of this.

You learn that many "life-changing" branches would probably have led back into ordinary life.

Different car.

Different evening.

Different person.

Different inconvenience.

The fantasy becomes less absolute.

But I do not want to erase the 2009 perspective.

At that age, timing really could feel personal.

That feeling belongs to the period.

The old entry is also interesting because four days later — on November 13 — I wrote about actually owning a Ford Focus.

So the car-search story did not end with permanent cosmic injustice.

I bought a car.

I wrote that I was not exactly floating with happiness, but I thought it deserved the money I had paid.

I also said something revealing about my driving.

With many cars, I would stall the engine two or three times before I got out.

With the Focus, I had not managed to stall it yet.

That apparently made it an excellent car for a beginner.

Then I added an edit in advance:

One month later I would probably be saying, "This thing flies."

That entry changes the first one in a satisfying way.

On November 9:

Every good car disappears before I arrive.

On November 13:

I have a Ford Focus.

Four days.

That is all.

The universe apparently ended its campaign against me very quickly.

This is a useful correction to how frustration feels in the moment.

When things are not working, the pattern seems permanent.

Nothing ever works.

Everything goes wrong.

Then four days later, something works.

Not because a philosophical breakthrough occurred.

Because time continued.

That sounds obvious.

It is surprisingly hard to remember while frustrated.

Current emotion makes itself look permanent.

A bad week impersonates a bad life.

A failed search impersonates permanent failure.

A missed connection impersonates destiny.

Then the situation changes.

The old certainty becomes embarrassing.

This is one of the quiet benefits of keeping dated writing.

You can put despair and resolution next to each other.

The distance is often much smaller than memory suggests.

November 9.

November 13.

The first entry complains that every car is sold before I arrive.

The second says I own one.

This is better than any motivational quotation.

Not because everything always works out.

It does not.

Because moods are poor forecasters.

We mistake current conditions for long-term truth.

Archives expose the mistake.

That is one reason I enjoy reading old complaints.

They often contain no knowledge of what is about to happen.

The writer is trapped inside the present.

He says:

This is how life works.

Then the next page says:

Apparently not.

That is deeply human.

We narrate too early.

The woman in the entry is different because the archive does not provide a clean resolution here.

The source only says I had not encountered her for five months after deciding to talk to her.

I will not add an ending.

That restraint matters.

Not every missed encounter turns into romance.

Not every coincidence has a payoff.

Sometimes a person simply passes through your social orbit and disappears.

The lack of resolution may be the most accurate part.

We are trained by stories to expect significance.

Introduce a person in chapter one and she should matter later.

Real life wastes characters constantly.

Someone appears.

You think:

Interesting.

Maybe.

Then schedules change.

Nothing happens.

No lesson.

No reunion.

No tragedy.

They simply leave the narrative.

This can feel unsatisfying because the mind expects completion.

But incompleteness is normal.

Most possibilities do not become stories.

They remain possibilities we remember only because we once noticed them.

There is something almost funny about how seriously we can take a person we barely know.

Attraction fills missing information efficiently.

You see enough to become interested.

Then imagination supplies the rest.

The person becomes partly real, partly projection.

If you never speak, the projection has no competition.

This is one reason hesitation can preserve attraction.

Reality does not get a chance to interfere.

Maybe the conversation would have been wonderful.

Maybe it would have lasted three minutes and revealed that you had nothing to say to each other.

The unrealized version remains perfect because it never has to survive contact.

That is another parallel with the cars.

A car you never buy cannot disappoint you.

It remains the one that got away.

Maybe it had mechanical problems.

Maybe you would have hated it after a month.

Sold yesterday protects it from reality.

Missed opportunities can become ideal because they were missed.

This is not an argument against acting.

Quite the opposite.

Reality is useful.

It replaces fantasy with information.

Call.

Ask.

Look.

Speak.

Inspect the car.

Meet the person.

Sometimes the answer is disappointing.

At least the uncertainty shrinks.

The 2009 version of me seems to have understood the frustration but not yet fully trusted action.

That is normal.

Talking to someone you like can feel disproportionately difficult when the emotional stakes exist mostly in your own head.

Before the first real conversation, rejection can become enormous because it threatens an imagined future rather than an existing relationship.

Once you gain more experience, the scale changes.

A conversation is a conversation.

Maybe it goes somewhere.

Maybe it does not.

The world survives.

But that calm perspective is earned partly through earlier awkwardness.

You cannot simply explain it to your younger self and expect the body to agree.

Fear is physical.

You can know intellectually that nothing catastrophic will happen and still avoid walking across the room.

Then one day you finally decide.

And she disappears for five months.

Excellent.

If the universe wanted to teach procrastination, it could hardly have designed a better lesson.

Again, I do not literally believe that was the purpose.

But stories like this make lessons memorable because the timing is so rude.

This is where humor becomes useful.

Without humor, bad timing feels like victimhood.

With humor, it becomes material.

Four cars.

One day too late each time.

Five months without seeing the person you finally decided to approach.

At some point the pattern is too absurd not to laugh.

Humor gives control back.

You cannot control the sale.

You cannot control whether somebody appears at the next gathering.

But you can decide what kind of story the coincidence becomes.

A complaint.

A cosmic accusation.

A joke.

In 2009, I chose all three.

I even ended the entry with a crude little expression asking God to stop behaving like someone who shows something desirable and then refuses to give it.

The phrasing belongs to the younger voice.

I would not build the modern piece around it.

But I would not pretend that voice was more refined than it was.

Part of revisiting an archive is accepting that younger versions of ourselves spoke differently.

They were impatient.

Cruder.

More categorical.

Sometimes funnier because of it.

Maturity does not require editing them out of existence.

It requires understanding them without automatically endorsing everything they said.

The emotional core remains simple:

Come on.

Give me a chance.

That is the sentence beneath the whole entry.

A chance to buy the car before somebody else does.

A chance to see the woman after deciding to speak.

The complaint is about doors closing just before arrival.

We all know that feeling.

You finally have money.

Price goes up.

You finally have time.

Event ends.

You finally decide.

Opportunity moves.

Life does not coordinate itself around readiness.

This is one of adulthood's least charming rules.

The world is not waiting for us to become prepared.

Jobs fill.

Homes sell.

People move.

Relationships begin elsewhere.

Parents age.

Children grow.

Places close.

Readiness has a cost when it takes too long.

That does not mean rushing every decision.

It means recognizing that delay is also a decision.

Sometimes caution protects you.

Sometimes it simply lets the car sell on the previous day.

The tricky part is knowing which situation you are in.

Cars at least provide external data.

Price.

Condition.

Mileage.

Inspection.

People do not arrive with equivalent reports.

Romantic hesitation is harder because the uncertainty cannot be solved privately.

At some point, another human being has to enter the process.

You have to risk a response you do not control.

That is why people spend so much energy searching for signs before speaking.

Eye contact.

Tone.

Messages.

Coincidences.

Friend opinions.

Anything that reduces the risk.

Sometimes even the absence itself becomes a sign.

I have not seen her for five months.

Maybe that means something.

Probably it means we have not seen each other for five months.

Humans are uncomfortable with that level of simplicity.

We prefer symbolic weather.

Still, I like the fact that these two frustrations appeared in the same entry.

It creates an accidental portrait of the period.

I was looking for a car.

I was noticing women in friend groups.

I was impatient with timing.

I was interpreting coincidence dramatically.

That is a recognizable young-adult mixture.

Practical independence and emotional uncertainty arriving at the same time.

Buy a car.

Build a career.

Figure out relationships.

Pretend to know what you are doing.

The car may actually be easier.

By November 13, I had one.

People take longer.

There is also something period-specific about buying used cars through listings and discovering by phone or contact that they had just sold.

The online world offered more visibility but not necessarily real-time certainty.

A listing could still be there after the opportunity was gone.

You could spend time considering an object that no longer existed as an option.

This resembles many forms of online life.

Availability is displayed imperfectly.

Profiles.

Listings.

Jobs.

Products.

A screen can make possibility feel current even when reality has already changed.

That gap creates a special kind of disappointment.

You are mentally interacting with the past.

The car was available when the listing was created.

By the time you arrive, you are late.

Maybe this is why "sold yesterday" feels so much worse than "sold three weeks ago."

Three weeks ago means you never had a chance.

Yesterday means you imagine one.

If only I had called.

If only I had seen it sooner.

If only.

The smaller the missed distance, the larger the counterfactual.

A flight missed by six hours is bad planning.

A flight missed by thirty seconds can haunt you.

Near misses are psychologically expensive.

They make alternate reality too easy to visualize.

Four cars, each sold one day earlier, is almost engineered to maximize that effect.

Close enough to imagine ownership.

Far enough to have none.

The five-month social absence works differently.

It is not a near miss repeated.

It is an opportunity vacuum.

But the emotional mechanism is similar.

You become conscious of wanting something only when access becomes uncertain.

This is perhaps why desire and scarcity are such reliable partners.

Availability can make us lazy.

Scarcity creates focus.

You may see someone often and postpone speaking because "there will be another time."

Then there is no next time for months.

Suddenly all the previous times become visible.

Why did I not do it then?

The future exposes the wasted past.

Again, no need for tragedy.

This is ordinary life.

But ordinary life is full of these small retrospective corrections.

We learn urgency after the opportunity has already moved.

The good news is that most lessons are reusable.

You miss one car and call faster next time.

You delay one conversation and become slightly braver later.

Maybe.

Humans also repeat mistakes enthusiastically.

The archive proves that too.

But repetition does not erase learning.

It only makes it nonlinear.

What I find most comforting now is the Ford Focus entry four days later.

It places a ceiling on the despair of November 9.

Not everything was being withheld.

Not every door was closing.

One door opened almost immediately.

That is the perspective the person inside the first entry could not have.

And this is why old writing can become a conversation between selves.

The younger one says:

Look at this pattern. Life is obviously messing with me.

The older one can answer:

You bought a car four days later.

The younger one says:

That is not the point.

The older one says:

It is a little bit the point.

Both are right.

Emotion does not need to be factually durable to have been real.

The frustration happened.

The pattern felt convincing.

Then reality changed.

That sequence is more useful than choosing one interpretation and deleting the other.

So I would keep the old accusation.

Not as theology.

As mood.

For a few weeks in 2009, it really did seem as though everything I reached for had been moved half a meter farther away.

Four cars.

One woman.

Bad timing everywhere.

Then I found a Focus.

And life continued.

Which is usually what happens after we announce that the universe has personally decided to ruin everything.

It continues.

Sometimes only four days later.
