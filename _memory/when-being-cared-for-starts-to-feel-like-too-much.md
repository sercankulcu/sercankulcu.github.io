---
title: "When Being Cared For Starts to Feel Like Too Much"
date: 2010-04-25
tags:

   - home

---

In April 2010, I wrote about what it feels like to live with your family again after being away for a long time.

The entry was very short.

My mother was constantly walking around with a plate in her hand.

Cake.

Pastry.

Fruit.

Something.

Always something.

My father had his own habits.

If I was lying on the couch, he would bring me the television remote.

If I fell asleep wearing my glasses, he would take them off.

He would cover me.

These are gestures that sound almost embarrassingly tender when listed years later.

At the time, however, my conclusion was not:

How lucky I am.

It was:

Why is this somehow nicer when I am away from them than when I am actually here?

I even wondered whether this was simply human ingratitude.

That may have been too harsh.

Perhaps it was not ingratitude.

Perhaps it was adulthood colliding with care.

There is a point in life when the things that once meant comfort can begin to feel like interference.

Not because they have become unkind.

Because we have changed.

A child receives care as the normal structure of the world.

Food appears.

A blanket appears.

Someone remembers whether you are cold.

Someone notices that you fell asleep with your glasses on.

You do not analyze this.

It is simply home.

Then you leave.

University.

Work.

Another city.

Your own place.

Whatever form independence takes.

And suddenly the invisible work behind comfort becomes visible.

Nobody brings the remote.

If you fall asleep with your glasses on, you wake up with your glasses on.

If you want fruit, you get up and find fruit.

If there is no fruit, there is no fruit.

No one appears with a plate.

At first, this independence can feel wonderful.

You decide.

You organize.

You eat when you want.

Sleep when you want.

Leave things where you want.

There is nobody asking whether you are hungry after you have already said no three times.

Freedom often begins with small absences.

No questions.

No reminders.

No unsolicited plate.

You do not realize that some of those absences are also care until much later.

Then you go back.

And the old system starts again.

Mother with food.

Father with the remote.

Blanket.

Glasses.

Small services that require no request.

The remarkable thing is that the same gesture can feel completely different depending on distance.

When you are away:

You remember it as warmth.

When you are there:

It can feel like too much attention.

This is one of the contradictions of family life.

We miss the pattern in memory and resist the pattern in practice.

Maybe because memory removes repetition.

It keeps only the meaning.

A mother bringing you fruit becomes:

My mother cared about me.

It does not preserve the fourth time she asked whether you wanted something that evening.

A father covering you with a blanket becomes:

My father looked after me.

It does not preserve the part where you might wake up and think:

Why is this blanket on me?

Memory edits irritation efficiently.

That is one of its kindnesses.

It is also one of its distortions.

Distance turns routine into symbol.

Home cooking stops being a sequence of ordinary meals and becomes "my mother's food."

A father's minor habits become "the way my father cared."

The irritating becomes precious because frequency disappears.

This is why returning can be strange.

The symbolic version of home meets the operational version.

You have spent months or years missing "home."

Then you arrive and discover that home is not an abstract warmth.

It has people in it.

People have habits.

They repeat themselves.

They enter rooms.

They ask questions.

They bring plates.

They watch whether you are sleeping.

Love is repetitive.

That is partly what makes it love.

It is also what can make it annoying.

There is a cultural tendency to idealize care only after it is gone.

We say we should appreciate these things while we have them.

True.

But that advice is easier to give than to live.

Appreciation does not erase the nervous system.

You can understand that someone is being kind and still want five minutes without being asked whether you want tea.

Both can be true.

Gratitude and irritation are not opposites.

They often coexist.

That may be the more honest interpretation of the old entry.

The younger version of me called it ingratitude.

The older interpretation is gentler.

People need autonomy.

Being cared for does not eliminate the need to feel capable of caring for yourself.

When those needs overlap, even affection can create friction.

The mother brings food because feeding is love.

The adult son may hear:

You cannot manage yourself.

She may not mean that at all.

He may not consciously think it either.

Still, adulthood is sensitive to reminders of dependency.

Once you have lived alone, small parental gestures can pull you backward in age for a moment.

You are an adult until your mother says:

Eat this.

Then you are suddenly twelve.

Not literally.

Emotionally.

Family has a strange ability to restore old roles faster than almost anything else.

You can be completely competent outside.

Handle work.

Money.

Travel.

Responsibilities.

Make decisions people rely on.

Then walk through your parents' door and become "the child" again.

Not because they deny your adulthood.

Because relationships have memory too.

Families store versions of us.

The person arriving home may be thirty.

The parent's care may still contain traces of ten, fifteen, twenty.

This is why family reunions can feel comforting and irritating in the same hour.

You are loved as someone continuous.

But you have changed.

You want the continuity and the change recognized at once.

That is difficult for everyone.

Parents also face a strange transition.

For years, caring is not optional.

A child genuinely needs food prepared.

Needs protection.

Needs reminders.

Needs someone to notice things.

Then the child becomes increasingly independent.

But habits formed through years of responsibility do not disappear immediately.

How does a parent stop being a parent at the right intensity?

There is no switch.

No official date.

No graduation ceremony where somebody announces:

From today onward, please reduce unsolicited fruit delivery by seventy percent.

So the gestures continue.

They become smaller.

Less necessary.

More symbolic.

A plate.

A blanket.

A remote control.

This is parental care after necessity has weakened but affection has not.

That makes the old entry more touching to me now.

My father bringing the remote is almost absurdly small.

I was capable of getting a remote.

Obviously.

That was not the point.

The act says:

Stay there.

I will get it.

Rest.

That sentence can be irritating when received too often.

Years later, it sounds beautiful.

This is the unfairness of hindsight.

The present self has unlimited patience for gestures the past self experienced in real time.

The past self had to live with the frequency.

The present self receives only the selected memory.

We should therefore be careful about judging our younger reactions.

It is easy to become sentimental and accuse the earlier self:

Why didn't you appreciate it?

Maybe he did.

Maybe appreciation was simply mixed with annoyance.

Real affection rarely arrives in pure emotional form.

We can love a person and want them to stop talking for a moment.

Miss a place and become bored after two days there.

Want family closeness and also want a locked door.

Human beings are not inconsistent because these things coexist.

They are layered.

Independence creates one of the deepest layers.

Once you learn your own routines, returning to someone else's household requires negotiation even if that household made you.

You have developed preferences nobody had to account for before.

When you sleep.

What you eat.

How much noise you tolerate.

When you want to talk.

How you arrange objects.

How long you stay alone.

These may seem trivial.

They are the architecture of adult life.

Living alone teaches the body that it controls the environment.

Living with family again reintroduces other wills.

Someone enters the kitchen while you are there.

Someone changes the television channel.

Someone asks where you are going.

Someone offers food.

Again, none of this is necessarily oppressive.

It can be ordinary family life.

But autonomy has made you more aware of it.

This is why leaving home changes home even if the house itself has not changed.

You return as a different user.

The old interface no longer behaves exactly as expected.

I should avoid turning this into a software metaphor, because family life deserves better than that.

The simpler truth is enough:

You have learned the shape of being alone.

Now closeness has edges.

There is another beautiful contradiction in the specific objects.

Food.

Remote.

Glasses.

Blanket.

None of these acts require conversation.

They are practical affection.

Some families speak love easily.

Others do things.

They cut fruit.

Fill a plate.

Bring an object from the other side of the room.

Turn off a light.

Cover someone sleeping.

This is a language.

The vocabulary is domestic.

The meaning is emotional.

The old entry does not tell me whether my parents were verbally expressive.

I should not invent that.

But the gestures themselves are undeniable.

Care was being communicated through action.

And action is sometimes easier to recognize later.

Words disappear.

Objects remain in memory.

I can picture the plate because the entry names it.

I can picture the glasses being removed.

The blanket being placed.

This is why small physical gestures are so important in autobiographical writing.

"Parents cared about me" is abstract.

"My father removed my glasses when I fell asleep" is a life.

Specificity carries emotion without demanding it.

The sentence does not say he loved me.

It does not need to.

There is also something almost comic in the idea that my father brought me the television remote while I was already lying on the couch.

It is such an unnecessary service.

That is why it feels affectionate.

Necessary care proves responsibility.

Unnecessary care reveals tenderness.

A parent must feed a small child.

A parent does not need to retrieve a remote for an adult who can stand up perfectly well.

Doing it anyway contains generosity.

Maybe excessive generosity.

Maybe annoying generosity.

Still generosity.

The mother's plate works the same way.

Food is one of the most persistent forms of parental love because it is measurable.

Did you eat?

Here.

Take this.

Eat some fruit.

There is comfort in having something concrete to do for someone you love.

Especially when that person no longer needs the larger forms of care.

You cannot solve their career.

You cannot manage their relationships.

You cannot protect them from every disappointment.

But you can cut fruit.

Perhaps this is why parents continue offering food long after the child is fully capable of feeding himself.

Food remains an available form of help.

Adulthood reduces the parent's control over outcomes.

The plate remains.

Seen from that angle, irritation softens.

Not disappears.

Softens.

Because the person holding the plate may also be adapting to loss.

The child gains independence.

The parent loses a role.

Nobody describes this as loss because independence is supposed to be good.

It is good.

But good transitions can contain grief.

The child leaves.

The house changes.

Daily usefulness changes.

Then the child returns temporarily and old habits wake up happily.

Eat.

Rest.

Here is the remote.

Give me those glasses.

You are home.

That may be what the gestures are saying.

The adult hears:

Too much.

The parent means:

You are home.

Both messages can occupy the same room.

This is why family relationships improve when we learn to translate behavior instead of only reacting to it.

Again, not every family gesture is harmless.

Care can become control.

Concern can become intrusion.

The word "family" does not magically make boundaries unnecessary.

But the source here gives no evidence of cruelty or control.

It gives plates, a remote, glasses, and a blanket.

So the interpretation should remain proportional.

This was affection that sometimes felt excessive.

That is enough.

The old line says these things seemed good when I was away but not so good when I was with them.

Distance changes appetite.

When hungry for family, every gesture sounds wonderful.

Once full, you need space again.

That is not moral failure.

It may simply be emotional homeostasis.

We move between closeness and independence.

Too much of either creates longing for the other.

Living alone can become lonely.

Living together can become crowded.

The fantasy is always located one step away from the current condition.

When alone:

I wish someone were here.

When surrounded:

I wish everyone would leave me alone.

Human beings are talented at wanting the missing half.

Perhaps maturity is not eliminating this contradiction.

It is noticing it sooner.

You can say:

I am irritated now, but I know I will miss this.

That does not require pretending not to be irritated.

It creates perspective.

Perspective is one of the few things age reliably improves, if we let it.

The 2010 entry already contained the beginning of that perspective.

Why does this feel good when I am away and bad when I am here?

That is a real question.

The answer may be that memory and presence obey different rules.

Presence has volume.

Memory has editing.

Presence repeats.

Memory selects.

Presence asks for response.

Memory asks for nothing.

This is why people become easier to love from a distance in certain moments.

Distance removes friction.

But friction is also evidence of relationship.

Only people close enough to affect your routine can annoy you in such specific ways.

The stranger cannot bring you fruit for the fourth time.

The stranger does not know you fell asleep with glasses on.

The stranger does not cover you.

Annoyance is sometimes the shadow cast by intimacy.

That does not make all annoyance meaningful.

But in this case, it probably is.

The detail I return to most is the glasses.

There is vulnerability in sleep.

You stop managing yourself.

Someone else notices.

They remove something that might be uncomfortable.

Cover you.

Do not wake you.

Care is often quietest when the recipient cannot acknowledge it.

That makes it unusually pure.

No thanks expected.

No conversation.

You may not even know it happened until later.

This is perhaps why sleeping children are such powerful symbols for parents.

When the child sleeps, resistance disappears.

No argument.

No "I'm not hungry."

No "leave it."

No claim to independence.

The parent can simply care.

An adult child asleep on the couch may briefly restore an older relationship.

Remove glasses.

Blanket.

Done.

Then morning comes and adulthood returns.

I do not know whether my father thought anything like this.

The source gives no access to his inner world.

So this is interpretation, not fact.

The fact remains beautifully small:

He took off my glasses and covered me.

That is enough to keep.

Years later, these details become more important because ordinary parental care is finite.

Not because love necessarily ends.

Because routines do.

People age.

Households change.

Distances change.

Health changes.

Eventually the person who always brought something may no longer be able to.

Or the child may simply live far enough away that the scene rarely occurs.

This gives domestic memories a delayed gravity.

At the time:

Please, I said I don't want anything.

Later:

I remember the plate.

This is one of life's crueler editing tricks.

We often understand the value of repetition after repetition ends.

The daily "good morning."

The same meal.

The same question.

The same phone call.

The same annoying reminder.

Once absent, repetition becomes rhythm.

We realize it had been marking time for us.

Family does this intensely.

Parents repeat themselves because love itself is repetitive.

Did you eat?

Drive carefully.

Call when you arrive.

Take this with you.

Wear something warm.

Again.

Again.

Again.

The content can become invisible.

The repetition is the message.

I am still thinking about you.

That is what remains when the literal instruction becomes unnecessary.

Perhaps the 2010 version of me was right to call attention to the contradiction, but wrong to call it only ingratitude.

It was adjustment.

The family was expressing continuity.

I was experiencing change.

Neither side was wrong.

I had learned to live without those gestures.

They had not learned to stop making them.

And maybe nobody needed to learn completely.

A little friction was a fair price for having a place where somebody still noticed whether my glasses were on when I fell asleep.

That sentence means more to me now than it probably could have in 2010.

This is what time does to ordinary care.

It converts service into evidence.

The remote is no longer a remote.

The fruit is no longer fruit.

The blanket is no longer a blanket.

They become proof that, for a while, there were people in the house whose attention extended all the way to small things.

And perhaps that is what home was.

Not the building.

Not even comfort.

Being observed with kindness.

Sometimes too closely.

Sometimes irritatingly.

Still kindly.

You leave because adulthood requires distance.

You return and discover that love has been waiting with a plate in its hand.

You complain.

You eat some anyway.

Then years pass.

And somehow the part you remember is the plate.
