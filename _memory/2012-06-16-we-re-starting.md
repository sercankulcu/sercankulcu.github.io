I did not like hospitals.

I especially did not like the moment when a medical decision stopped being a conversation and became something that was actually going to happen.

I was waiting for an eye procedure when the doctor entered the room.

He looked completely at home there.

Then he said something like, “Come on, kids. We're starting.”

That sentence changed the room.

Nothing about it was dramatic. He did not make a speech. He did not promise that everything would be perfect.

He simply sounded like someone who knew what came next.

I remember thinking that if he had said we were going to war, I might have stood up and followed him.

The thought was ridiculous.

The trust was real.

Before the procedure, I had carried the usual doubts. Was this really necessary? Was I being pushed into something? Was I making the right decision?

Those questions did not disappear because of one sentence.

But once the moment arrived, I needed something different from information.

I needed confidence that did not feel like salesmanship.

He had it.

The procedure that seemed large to me appeared ordinary to him. Not unimportant. Ordinary.

That was reassuring.

Experts sometimes help us most by making a difficult thing look manageable without pretending it is nothing.

Years later, I remember very little of the room.

I remember the sentence.

“We're starting.”

And I remember the strange relief of believing that the person saying it knew exactly what he was doing.
