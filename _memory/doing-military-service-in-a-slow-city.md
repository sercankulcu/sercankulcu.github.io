There are places that advertise themselves as peaceful.

Quiet.

Slow.

Relaxed.

This sounds wonderful when you are choosing somewhere to spend a weekend.

It sounds different when you are doing military service there.

In August 2010, I wrote a very short entry about Seferihisar.

My conclusion was immediate:

This was absolutely not a place where one should do military service.

Then I listed my reasons.

If you were serving there, you were probably in gendarmerie communications.

The town center was tiny.

There was barely anywhere to eat.

And, as if the universe had decided to complete the joke, Seferihisar had been recognized as a "slow city."

At the time, this did not sound like an achievement.

It sounded like a threat.

When you are a civilian, "slow" can mean peace.

When you are a soldier counting days, slow means something else.

A slow afternoon is not relaxing.

It is longer.

A quiet town is not charming.

It is a place where there is nowhere to go when you finally get permission to leave the barracks.

This is one of the strange things military service does to geography.

Places stop being places.

They become time.

How long until the next leave?

How far to the center?

How long can we stay outside?

Is there anywhere worth going?

What time do we need to return?

A civilian sees a coastal town.

A soldier sees a limited number of hours.

That difference changes everything.

I had already known before arriving that my short-term military service would intersect with an absurd collection of dates.

August 30.

Ramadan Bayram.

October 29.

November 10.

Kurban Bayram.

New Year's Eve.

Even my birthday.

Five months and five days does not sound very long when someone else says it.

It becomes much more impressive when you start placing holidays inside it.

Suddenly the calendar looks occupied.

The joke I made before going was that we would leave in 2010 and return in 2011, as if we were departing for a major expedition.

Technically true.

Emotionally exaggerated.

Exactly the kind of exaggeration that makes military-service humor work.

Time inside a barracks has its own mathematics.

A week outside is nothing.

A week inside can acquire structure.

Monday.

Tuesday.

Wednesday.

How many breakfasts?

How many nights?

How many inspections?

How many days until leave?

People who have never counted time that way may find it strange.

People who have immediately understand.

The calendar stops being abstract.

Dates become physical objects.

You can almost touch them.

And then there is the "çarşı."

Leave.

A few hours outside.

The civilian world temporarily returned to you.

Normal clothes around you.

Restaurants.

Shops.

People walking somewhere because they simply wanted to.

No formation.

No commands.

No schedule posted by someone else.

For a short-term soldier, even a mediocre town center could become important because it represented outside.

But Seferihisar had one problem.

It was very good at being small.

This was presumably part of its charm.

I did not appreciate the charm.

If you have spent days waiting to leave a military base, the phrase "small and peaceful town" can sound less like tourism and more like poor resource allocation.

We finally get out.

Where do we go?

There.

What else?

That's basically it.

Where do we eat?

Good question.

My old entry complained that there was hardly even a place to eat.

That was probably the frustration speaking.

I am sure there were places to eat.

The important truth is that, from a soldier's perspective, the available world felt extremely limited.

That feeling matters more than a restaurant count.

Military service reduces freedom so sharply that small freedoms become exaggerated.

A cigarette.

A phone call.

A tea.

A shower at the right time.

A decent meal.

A few hours in town.

The civilian version of you would barely notice these things.

The soldier version assigns them enormous value.

This is one reason military memories sound strange when told later.

The story may revolve around something objectively tiny.

A better breakfast.

A chair.

A socket for charging a phone.

A weekend leave.

A place selling edible food.

Outside, these are nothing.

Inside, they become events.

Context sets value.

Economists know this.

Soldiers know it without terminology.

Scarcity changes everything.

One of my entries from that period joked that our group, the 335th short-term intake, had managed to get more town leave in the first fifty-three days than soldiers serving fifteen months.

Oaths.

Holidays.

Weekends.

Distribution.

One reason after another.

Our leave books were filling up before Kurban Bayram even arrived.

There is pride in such absurd achievements.

Not military pride.

Administrative luck pride.

We had not conquered territory.

We had conquered the calendar.

This is another feature of compulsory environments.

People become experts in small loopholes of ordinary life.

Which day gives leave?

Who is on duty?

What event changes the schedule?

Who is being transferred?

When does the next group arrive?

The institution has official structures.

The people inside develop a parallel science.

How to make the day easier.

How to protect ten minutes.

How to find a cigarette.

How to avoid unnecessary trouble.

How to get through one more week.

In retrospect, that may be what my Seferihisar complaint really documented.

Not the town.

Adaptation.

I had entered a system in which I could not simply decide what to do with my day.

So naturally, I began measuring everything that remained selectable.

The town became important because it represented choice.

Where to sit.

What to eat.

Who to call.

Whether to walk left or right.

Civilian life is full of choices so ordinary that we do not recognize them as freedom.

Military service makes them visible.

Nobody tells you this before you go.

People tell you other things.

It is difficult.

It is boring.

It passes.

Do not argue.

Do what you are told.

Take this with you.

Do not take that.

Everybody has advice.

But one of the deepest changes is simpler:

You notice how much of normal life is self-direction.

Wake up when you want on Sunday.

Open the refrigerator.

Walk outside.

Drive somewhere.

Buy something.

Change your plan.

Stay somewhere longer.

Go home.

The acts are tiny.

Together, they form autonomy.

Remove them and suddenly autonomy becomes visible.

That is why even a short leave in a tiny town can matter.

You are choosing again.

Not much.

But enough to remember what choosing feels like.

The irony was that Seferihisar's public identity emphasized slowness.

Slow food.

Slow urban life.

A calmer pace.

In another period of my life, I might have appreciated that deeply.

The idea of rejecting constant acceleration is attractive.

A city that does not organize itself entirely around speed and consumption has something valuable to offer.

But military service had already slowed time for us.

We did not need help.

The clock was doing excellent work on its own.

A quiet town outside a quiet routine was not contrast.

It was continuity.

The civilian tourist arrives thinking:

Finally, I can slow down.

The soldier arrives thinking:

Please, for the love of God, something happen.

That contrast still makes me laugh.

The same place can be paradise or punishment depending on what you are escaping from.

A crowded city resident dreams of silence.

Someone living under imposed routine dreams of noise.

A person exhausted by choices wants simplicity.

A person with no choices wants options.

There is no universally relaxing environment.

Relief is directional.

It depends on what pressure you are moving away from.

Maybe that is why travel advice is often misleading.

"This place is peaceful."

Good for whom?

"This town is lively."

Good for whom?

"This job is flexible."

For whom?

"This life is simple."

Compared with what?

Descriptions need a reference point.

Without one, adjectives become advertising.

Seferihisar was slow.

That was true.

The mistake was assuming "slow" had one emotional meaning.

For a holiday, maybe wonderful.

For a soldier waiting for time to pass, less wonderful.

The communications unit added another layer of irony.

I had a technical background.

Communications sounded, from outside, almost appropriate.

Perhaps there would be interesting equipment.

Systems.

Radios.

Networking.

Something engineering-adjacent.

Then military reality arrived and reminded us that job titles inside institutions do not always mean what the civilian imagination expects.

One of my later jokes about the communications section was that it was the only part of the battalion without internet.

The shoemaker's children had no shoes.

The communications people had no internet.

That is the kind of organizational joke that writes itself.

Every institution has them.

The IT department with the worst printer.

The telecommunications office with no signal.

The process-improvement team drowning in forms.

The communications unit without internet.

Humor is one of the easiest ways people survive systems that contradict themselves.

You notice the absurdity.

You name it.

Now it belongs to you a little.

You cannot change the institution.

You can at least describe it accurately enough to make someone laugh.

This was especially useful in military service because complaint itself has limits.

Complaining all day makes the day worse.

Pretending everything is wonderful is impossible.

Humor sits between them.

Yes, this is ridiculous.

We know.

Continue.

That may be why so many military stories are funny years later even if they were not particularly funny at the time.

Distance edits discomfort.

Not completely.

But enough.

The boredom becomes structure.

The absurd rules become anecdotes.

The terrible day becomes the day something memorable happened.

The town that felt impossibly small becomes a story about serving in Turkey's first "slow city" and wishing it would speed up.

Memory performs its own demobilization.

It removes the command voice.

Keeps the punchline.

I do not want to romanticize military service.

Loss of autonomy is still loss of autonomy.

Boredom is still boredom.

The institution can be exhausting precisely because so much of your time is not yours.

But difficult periods often teach through contrast.

You understand choice after losing some of it.

You understand privacy after sharing space.

You understand silence differently after compulsory noise.

You understand home after being away.

And you understand the luxury of deciding where to eat after receiving permission to go into a town where, in your irritated opinion, there is nowhere to eat.

That is what remains for me in the Seferihisar entry.

A younger version of me had three reasons the place was unsuitable for military service.

Communications unit.

Tiny town center.

Slow city.

Short.

Angry.

Funny.

Underneath those three complaints was a larger discovery:

The quality of a place cannot be separated from the freedom with which you entered it.

A beautiful town can feel small when you cannot leave.

A quiet afternoon can feel endless when the schedule is not yours.

And a few ordinary hours outside can feel enormous when somebody else controls most of the week.

Years later, I can appreciate the joke more than the complaint.

Seferihisar had been designated a slow city.

We were soldiers.

We were already counting days.

Slow was the one thing we absolutely did not need.
