The first cup of tea without sugar tasted wrong.

It was not simply less sweet. It seemed to be a different drink, one that had been hiding underneath the tea I knew. The bitterness was sharper, the smell stronger, and every sip reminded me that something was missing.

For years, I had never thought of sugar as an addition. Tea arrived, sugar went in, and the two actions belonged together. My hand reached for the sugar almost before I had decided to drink.

That morning I left it alone.

I took another sip and wondered why anyone would choose to drink tea this way. People who already drank it without sugar had promised that I would eventually taste the tea itself. At the moment, I was not interested in the tea itself. I wanted the old cup back.

Still, I continued.

For several days, each glass began with the same small disappointment. Then something changed so slowly that I did not notice the exact moment. The bitterness became less aggressive. The missing sweetness stopped announcing itself.

One afternoon, someone gave me tea with sugar by mistake.

I drank it and was surprised.

It was too sweet.

The same taste I had once considered necessary now covered everything else. I looked at the glass as if someone had changed the recipe.

Nothing in the tea had changed. The difference was in me.

That small discovery stayed with me because habits rarely announce when they become habits. They enter quietly, repeat themselves, and eventually begin to feel natural. We notice their strength only when we remove them.

Tea made the process easy to see.

First the cup without sugar tasted incomplete. Then it became ordinary. Finally the old version became strange.

Years later, I do not remember every morning when I forced myself not to reach for sugar. I remember the first bitter cup and the unexpectedly sweet one that came afterward.

Between those two cups, without ceremony, normal had moved.
