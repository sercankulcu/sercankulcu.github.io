In December 2018, I argued that Turkish television series should be no longer than forty minutes.

My evidence was not a statistical study.

It was one scene.

A man enters a building.

It takes him three minutes to reach the second floor.

Then two people look at each other at the door for five minutes.

The door closes after another two minutes of near-silence.

The man takes five minutes to walk back downstairs and leave.

Do the same sequence twice, I wrote, and forty minutes is already gone.

That was the complaint.

Not that nothing happened.

That time itself had been inflated.

Television can stretch a glance until it becomes architecture.

A normal human action takes seconds.

Drama gives it music.

Reaction shots.

A close-up.

Another close-up.

Memory.

Silence.

Commercial break approaching.

Suddenly climbing two floors becomes an emotional journey.

Sometimes this is beautiful.

Cinema has always known how to slow time.

A face held for five seconds can contain more than a page of dialogue.

Silence can be story.

A hallway can create dread.

Pacing is not supposed to match real time.

The problem begins when slowness feels less like artistic choice and more like the obligation to fill duration.

That was what irritated me.

The scene did not feel deliberate.

It felt padded.

This distinction is subjective, but viewers recognize it instinctively.

Slow is not the same as empty.

A slow scene can be dense.

Tension.

Atmosphere.

Character.

Observation.

An empty scene is long because length is required.

The camera waits for something the story has not earned.

The audience begins noticing minutes rather than meaning.

Once that happens, the spell breaks.

You stop thinking:

What will he say?

You start thinking:

Why is he still on the stairs?

That is fatal for drama.

The original entry was written during an era when Turkish television dramas were famous for very long episodes.

The source itself does not provide industry data, so I will not turn this into a historical claim about exact average runtimes.

My complaint was simply that forty minutes would be enough if scenes were not stretched absurdly.

That observation reveals a larger storytelling principle:

constraints can improve work.

If you have forty minutes, every scene competes for space.

If you have two hours, weak material can survive longer before anyone is forced to cut it.

Editing is partly cruelty toward material you already made.

The actor performed it.

Crew lit it.

Camera recorded it.

Music was added.

Someone worked hard on every second.

Then the editor must say:

we do not need this.

That is difficult.

Long formats reduce the pressure to make those decisions.

Why cut?

We have time.

But “we have time” is not the same as “the audience should spend time here.”

That distinction matters in every form of communication.

Meetings.

Lectures.

Articles.

Presentations.

Emails.

Software interfaces.

People tend to fill the available container.

Give a student two pages, they write two.

Give them ten, somehow ten become necessary.

Schedule a one-hour meeting and the meeting develops a one-hour-shaped problem.

Give a television episode enormous runtime and stairs become plot.

This is Parkinson's law translated into storytelling:

content expands to fill the time available.

A hard limit can be creative mercy.

It forces hierarchy.

What matters?

Which look do we need?

Which line says the most?

Can the entrance happen in ten seconds?

The result is often not merely shorter.

It is clearer.

The old entry's imagined forty-minute limit was therefore not only about convenience.

It was about discipline.

If a story cannot survive cutting dead time, perhaps the dead time was never the story.

Of course there is another side.

Streaming eventually popularized flexible runtimes.

Episodes no longer needed to fit rigid broadcast slots.

Some stories benefited.

A chapter could be thirty-eight minutes.

Another sixty-two.

The container could follow content.

That sounds ideal.

But freedom introduces another risk:

without external limits, creators must supply internal discipline.

Nobody forces the cut.

Taste must.

The same is true in writing.

A platform with no word limit does not guarantee a better essay.

It guarantees the possibility of a longer one.

The writer still has to know when the thought is finished.

I am very aware of the irony while expanding a short Ekşi Sözlük entry into an essay.

Perhaps the 2018 version of me would tell me to cut this paragraph.

Fair.

The point survives.

Length should be earned.

That is a demanding standard because audiences do not experience production effort.

They experience time.

It may have taken three days to film a scene.

The viewer gives it three minutes.

Production cost does not create narrative value automatically.

This is another reason creators can become attached to material audiences find unnecessary.

They know how hard it was to make.

The viewer knows only whether it matters.

Editing requires forgetting sunk cost.

A scene can be expensive and still be removable.

A sentence can be beautiful and still not belong.

Writers sometimes call this killing your darlings.

Television occasionally needs to kill a staircase.

The five-minute stare in my old complaint is also interesting because staring is a classic dramatic device.

It lets the audience project emotion.

Love.

Anger.

Suspicion.

Shock.

The face becomes an open text.

But repetition weakens it.

One meaningful silence can be powerful.

Every confrontation conducted through minutes of staring turns the device into habit.

Techniques lose power when they become defaults.

Slow motion.

Flashbacks.

Music swells.

Reaction shots.

Cliffhangers.

Each works because it changes rhythm.

If everything is emphasized, emphasis disappears.

A story needs contrast.

Fast and slow.

Noise and silence.

Action and stillness.

The old entry's problem was not slowness alone.

It was monotony disguised as intensity.

This is why pacing is felt physically.

A well-paced story changes breathing without the viewer noticing.

A badly paced one makes the viewer check the remaining time.

That is perhaps the simplest test.

When the clock becomes more interesting than the scene, something failed.

Television habits also shape audience habits.

If viewers grow accustomed to stretched pacing, they learn to half-watch.

Phone in hand.

Conversation.

Kitchen trip.

The program assumes partial attention.

Then creators repeat information because viewers may have missed it.

Repetition makes full attention less rewarding.

The cycle reinforces itself.

I am not claiming this specific mechanism caused the scene in my source; the entry does not support that level of analysis.

But it is a useful general problem in long-form television.

Content and attention co-evolve.

Dense storytelling asks for focus.

Padded storytelling trains distraction.

The younger me wanted forty-minute episodes.

Today I would phrase the desire differently.

I want stories that know how long they are.

If the material needs twenty minutes, give it twenty.

If it needs ninety, give it ninety.

But do not make a man climb the same conceptual staircase for five extra minutes because the schedule needs feeding.

The audience's time is part of the production budget.

It should be spent intentionally.
