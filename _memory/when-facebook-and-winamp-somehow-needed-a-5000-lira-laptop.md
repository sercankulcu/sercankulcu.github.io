title: "When Facebook and Winamp Somehow Needed a 5000-Lira Laptop"
date: 2013-02-01
tags:

On 1 February 2013, I wrote down a small contradiction.

We were looking for a laptop for my girlfriend.

The requirements were not complicated.

It needed to open Facebook in Internet Explorer.

It needed to play music in Winamp.

That was essentially it.

And somehow we had reached a budget of 5000 lira.

The younger version of me found this so absurd that the conclusion came naturally:

May everyone who works at Apple be blessed with customers like this.

That was the joke.

But the contradiction behind it has aged extremely well.

We often do not buy according to what we need.

We buy according to what the object means.

This is especially obvious with technology.

Ask someone what they actually need from a laptop and the answer may be surprisingly modest.

Web browser.

Music.

Documents.

Video.

Email.

Maybe a few applications.

Then the shopping begins.

Processor.

Memory.

Storage.

Screen.

Graphics.

Build quality.

Brand.

Design.

Weight.

Battery.

Ports.

Operating system.

Future-proofing.

Resale value.

And suddenly a person whose original requirement was “Facebook should open” is comparing machines designed for workloads they may never perform.

The requirements have not changed.

The purchase has.

This is not necessarily irrational.

Objects are rarely purchased only for their minimum function.

A chair is for sitting, but nobody concludes that all chairs are therefore identical.

A car is for transportation, but people care about comfort, safety, appearance, reliability, prestige, and pleasure.

A watch tells time, yet that is often the least interesting thing about an expensive watch.

Technology is no different.

A laptop can be a tool.

It can also be an object you carry, look at, touch, open in public, live with for years, and quietly use to say something about yourself.

That is where the price begins escaping the requirements list.

The old entry used Apple as the punchline because Apple made this distinction particularly visible.

Again, the exact market conditions of 2013 are not the point here.

I am preserving the logic of the entry, not trying to reconstruct a historical price comparison.

The joke was simple:

If the computer only needs to browse Facebook and play music, why are we considering spending this much?

The implied answer was:

Because the purchase is not really about those two tasks.

That is still how consumer technology works.

There is always a gap between what a device must do and what we want owning the device to feel like.

The gap can be tiny.

It can also cost a lot of money.

People justify this gap in different ways.

“I'll use it for years.”

“I want something good.”

“I don't want it to become obsolete.”

“The screen is much better.”

“This one is lighter.”

“The cheap one feels terrible.”

“I may need the extra performance later.”

Some of these reasons are completely valid.

Some are stories we tell ourselves after already wanting the expensive object.

The difficult part is knowing which is which.

Human beings are excellent at reverse-engineering rational explanations for desire.

First:

I want this.

Then:

Here is why buying it is actually the responsible decision.

Technology provides unusually sophisticated vocabulary for this process.

You can hide desire inside specifications.

Not:

I like this laptop.

Instead:

The thermal design is better.

Not:

This one feels expensive and beautiful.

Instead:

The aluminum chassis should improve long-term durability.

Not:

I want the premium model.

Instead:

The additional memory offers better future scalability.

Sometimes these statements are true.

That is what makes them so useful.

A justification is strongest when it contains enough truth to sound like analysis.

The 2013 entry had no such patience.

Facebook.

Winamp.

5000 lira.

Case closed.

There is something refreshing about reducing the requirements to actual behavior.

What will the person do with the machine?

Not what could the machine theoretically do.

Not what benchmarks say.

Not what advertising shows.

What will happen on Tuesday evening?

Open browser.

Facebook.

Open Winamp.

Music.

Once you frame a purchase this way, a great deal of technological aspiration starts looking funny.

This principle is useful far beyond laptops.

People buy cameras and never leave automatic mode.

Buy powerful phones and use messaging, social media, and the camera.

Buy kitchen appliances with fifteen functions and use two.

Buy exercise equipment that becomes furniture.

Buy software subscriptions for workflows they never develop.

The unused capability is part of the purchase.

Sometimes we are buying our imagined future self.

The person who will edit video.

Learn photography.

Make music.

Start exercising.

Become organized.

Travel more.

Read more.

Cook elaborate meals.

The object arrives before the person.

And sometimes the person never arrives.

That does not mean the purchase was always a mistake.

Aspiration has value.

A good tool can invite new behavior.

But we should at least notice when we are paying for possibility rather than current need.

In the old entry, the distance between current need and purchase possibility was comic because the current need was so small.

Facebook and Winamp.

Two icons.

One very large budget.

The phrase “we had set aside 5000 lira” also says something about how couples and close relationships make purchases.

A personal buying decision becomes a shared project.

“We are looking.”

“We have a budget.”

One person may care about specifications.

The other about design.

One asks what it needs to do.

The other asks whether it feels right.

This can produce friction, but it can also reveal something useful:

people evaluate objects through different dimensions.

The engineer's mistake is often believing functional adequacy should end the discussion.

It does not.

A technically sufficient object can still be unpleasant to own.

Ugly.

Heavy.

Noisy.

Fragile-feeling.

Awkward.

Aesthetically wrong.

The opposite mistake is treating those feelings as though function does not matter.

Good purchasing lives somewhere between the two.

What does it need to do?

And what will make the owner actually enjoy using it?

The old joke takes the functional side because that side produces the comedy.

If you write down only the minimum requirements, the premium purchase looks absurd.

If you include every emotional requirement, almost anything can be justified.

That tension has no permanent solution.

It simply needs awareness.

One of the most expensive sentences in shopping is:

“Since we're buying one anyway…”

Since we're buying one anyway, let's get the better model.

Since we're already spending this much, the next version is only a little more.

Since we'll use it for years, better not compromise.

Step by step, the original budget disappears.

Nobody makes one irrational leap.

They make five reasonable small ones.

This is how a simple laptop turns into a premium laptop.

The process is so common that retailers organize product lines around it.

Base model.

Slightly better model.

Better screen.

More storage.

Premium finish.

One more upgrade.

Each individual step appears manageable.

The total becomes something the original requirements never demanded.

In 2013, my defense against this escalation was sarcasm.

Facebook.

Winamp.

Five thousand lira.

Congratulations to Apple.

Sarcasm is not a complete purchasing framework, but it is useful.

It forces the basic question back into the room.

What are we actually buying this for?

That question can save money.

It can also confirm that the expensive option is still worth it.

Maybe the answer is:

Yes, Facebook and Winamp are the main tasks, but I also want the machine that feels better every day for the next several years.

Fine.

At least now the premium has a name.

You are buying experience.

Design.

Preference.

Not pretending you require extraordinary computing power to open a social network.

There is dignity in admitting desire.

“I want the nicer one” is sometimes more honest than a spreadsheet.

We do not need to convert every preference into technical necessity.

The older I get, the more useful I find that distinction.

Need.

Preference.

Luxury.

Aspiration.

All legitimate categories.

Problems begin when we disguise one as another.

If something is a luxury and you can afford it and want it, that may be enough.

No benchmark required.

But if you are trying to make a rational budget decision, returning to actual use is powerful.

What do I do today?

What will this change?

Which feature will I actually notice?

Which one only looks good on the comparison page?

Technology moves quickly enough that this question has become even more important.

Premium specifications age.

Today's impressive number becomes ordinary.

The emotional pleasure of a well-made device may remain longer.

Or not.

That is why future-proofing is such a slippery justification.

You can spend heavily protecting yourself from a future that arrives in a different form.

Meanwhile the basic workload remains embarrassingly simple.

Browser.

Music.

Messages.

Documents.

The names of the applications change.

The pattern does not.

There is also something wonderfully dated about the exact pair in the entry.

Internet Explorer for Facebook.

Winamp for music.

Those two names place the memory immediately.

No long historical explanation is needed.

Software names can function like furniture in a photograph.

You see them and know the room belongs to another period.

Winamp especially belongs to a world where music collections often lived locally.

Files.

Folders.

MP3s.

Playlists built from what you possessed.

The software was not an infinite music service.

It was a player.

The distinction matters.

Likewise, “open Facebook in Internet Explorer” sounds almost archaeological now, but in the entry it was simply normal behavior.

This is why mundane technology ages so beautifully in personal archives.

The major technological milestones are documented everywhere.

Product launches.

Companies.

Specifications.

Market history.

What disappears are ordinary sentences.

“I just need it to open Facebook.”

Those are better time capsules.

They tell us what technology felt like before it became history.

The 5000-lira figure works the same way.

I do not need to translate it into today's money or claim what it represented in purchasing power.

That would require external historical analysis and would distract from the source.

What matters is that the younger me considered the amount obviously excessive relative to the requirements.

That is enough.

The joke depends on perceived mismatch, not inflation-adjusted economics.

This restraint is useful when reading old writing.

Not every number needs modernization.

Sometimes a historical number should remain exactly where it was.

It belongs to the emotional scale of that day.

Five thousand lira.

Enough to make me write the entry.

Therefore enough to matter.

The title of the original entry was itself a crude generalization about women and spending.

That framing belonged to the younger voice and to the style of the platform at the time.

I would not preserve the generalization as the thesis now.

One person's laptop purchase does not tell us how women shop.

It tells us how humans shop.

We want more than function.

Men do it with cars, computers, audio equipment, watches, tools, cameras, bicycles, and countless other things.

Women do it with whatever objects they happen to care about.

Gender is not the interesting mechanism.

Desire is.

The mature version of the story is therefore more universal than the old joke.

We laugh at someone spending heavily on a laptop for Facebook and music.

Then we look around our own homes.

There is probably an object somewhere whose capabilities far exceed our use.

A device.

A tool.

A feature.

Something we bought because we liked the idea of what it could do.

That is not necessarily hypocrisy.

It is just being human.

We do not buy only present utility.

We buy identity, possibility, comfort, beauty, and hope.

The useful part is knowing when we are doing it.

The best sentence I can take from the memory is not the Apple joke.

It is the implicit requirements document:

Must open Facebook.

Must play music.

Everything after that is negotiation.

Maybe that should be how more shopping begins.

Write the actual requirements first.

Then go to the store.

Not because the cheapest thing is always the correct choice.

Because otherwise the products will write the requirements for you.

A better screen becomes necessary because you saw it.

More storage becomes necessary because it is available.

A premium finish becomes necessary because the cheaper finish is now sitting beside it.

Desire expands to match the catalog.

Requirements rarely survive exposure to options.

That is what shopping is designed to do.

In 2013, I watched it happen in one laptop search.

I reduced the whole absurdity to three facts.

Facebook.

Winamp.

5000 lira.

It is still a very efficient consumer-technology lesson.
