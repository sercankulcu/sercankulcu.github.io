title: "Twenty Minutes Was Enough"
date: 2009-11-05

In November 2009, people were complaining that *How I Met Your Mother* was no longer giving them what they expected.

My response was simple.

What exactly were they expecting?

The show gave me at least twenty minutes after I came home in which the fatigue of the day disappeared.

It made me laugh two or three times.

It entertained me.

That was enough.

The old entry then became deliberately absurd.

Yes, the show did not provide lonely people with a girlfriend.

It did not give intelligence to stupid people.

It did not launch anyone into space.

But it was a television series.

And only about twenty minutes long.

That entry preserved an idea I appreciate much more now:

not everything has to transform you.

Some things are allowed to simply make twenty minutes better.

We often judge entertainment with inflated expectations.

Was it important?

Original?

Deep?

Life-changing?

Did the finale justify the investment?

Did the character development satisfy us?

Was the writing as strong as season two?

All legitimate questions if criticism is the goal.

But criticism and use are not always the same thing.

Sometimes the function of a sitcom is exactly what its name implies.

You sit.

Watch.

Laugh.

Feel slightly less tired.

Done.

There is dignity in modest utility.

A cup of tea does not need to change your worldview.

A short walk does not need to become a fitness journey.

A comedy does not need to redefine television.

If it gives you twenty good minutes at the end of a difficult day, it has done something real.

The phrase “after coming home” matters.

Entertainment changes according to the condition in which we receive it.

The same show watched on a lazy Sunday and after an exhausting workday can be completely different.

On Sunday, you may notice repetition.

Weak writing.

Predictable jokes.

After work, you may be grateful that the characters are familiar and the structure predictable.

Complexity is not always a benefit.

Sometimes fatigue wants recognition without effort.

Familiar characters.

Short duration.

A few laughs.

No need to learn a new fictional universe.

The brain has already made too many decisions.

This is one reason comfort shows exist.

People rewatch series whose plots they already know.

From a pure information perspective, this makes no sense.

No surprise.

No new content.

Yet surprise is not always the goal.

Predictability can be soothing.

You know the emotional range.

You know nobody will demand too much.

The episode arrives with known boundaries.

Twenty minutes later, it leaves.

This is almost the opposite of prestige television, where each episode may demand full attention, memory, interpretation, and emotional stamina.

There is room for both.

The mistake is asking one to be the other.

The old complaint “it no longer gives what is expected” contains a hidden problem.

Expected by whom?

Expectations accumulate around successful works.

The first season surprises.

Later seasons must compete with memory of surprise.

That is impossible forever.

Once a style becomes familiar, part of its original pleasure disappears because novelty disappears.

Fans often interpret that loss as decline.

Sometimes the work truly declines.

Sometimes the audience has changed.

The first hundred jokes taught you the grammar.

The next joke arrives already partly predicted.

This is why long-running comedy has an unfair task.

It must remain itself enough to satisfy familiarity and become different enough to avoid repetition.

Too different:

they ruined it.

Too similar:

it is stale.

The audience wants the feeling of the beginning without being the person who watched the beginning.

No show can fully provide that.

The 2009 version of me escaped this problem by lowering the contract.

Do not give me transcendence.

Give me twenty minutes.

Make me laugh twice.

Very reasonable.

I think adults become better at happiness when they learn to value things at the scale they actually operate.

A friend does not need to solve your life to make an evening good.

A holiday does not need to heal burnout forever.

A book can contain one useful chapter.

A movie can contain one scene you remember.

A meal can just taste good.

Meaning does not have to be maximal to be valid.

Modern review culture often pushes us toward total judgments.

Masterpiece.

Trash.

Worth it.

Waste of time.

Everything becomes a verdict.

Ordinary enjoyment disappears between five stars and one star.

But most of life lives around three and a half.

Pretty good.

I enjoyed it.

Would watch again.

That is enough.

The source itself is casual.

It does not say which episode I watched, what work I had done that day, where I was living, or what specifically made me tired.

Those details are absent.

The memory does not need them.

Its value is precisely the generic ritual.

Come home.

Watch.

Laugh.

Reset slightly.

Thousands of evenings are built like that.

They do not become major memories.

They become life.

There is another reason twenty-minute comedy can matter.

Short entertainment has a clear exit.

A feature film may ask for two hours.

A modern drama episode perhaps an hour.

A sitcom asks for one small block.

You can say yes without reorganizing the evening.

Low commitment makes recovery easier to access.

This is why small rituals survive.

They fit around life rather than requiring life to fit around them.

The old entry's sarcasm also protects entertainment from impossible demands.

A show is not responsible for loneliness.

Not responsible for intelligence.

Not responsible for giving your life adventure.

It can accompany you.

It cannot live for you.

This distinction became even more important as media became more personalized and abundant.

People sometimes expect content to solve boredom permanently.

There is always another episode.

Another video.

Another recommendation.

Yet infinite supply does not create infinite satisfaction.

Entertainment works best when it has boundaries.

Twenty minutes.

Laugh.

Stop.

The satisfaction comes partly because the need was modest.

I do not want to romanticize passive distraction.

There are nights when entertainment becomes avoidance.

Hours disappear.

Sleep gets delayed.

Problems remain.

The source does not suggest that here.

It describes a short reset.

That is different.

Rest is not the enemy of seriousness.

People need decompression.

A mind held at maximum intensity all day eventually stops being useful.

Pleasure can be maintenance.

The younger me did not write any of this.

He simply told critics to stop expecting miracles.

The show made him laugh.

That was enough.

Sixteen years later, I think the simplicity of that standard is worth keeping.

Not everything needs to be unforgettable.

Some things earn their place by helping us forget the day for twenty minutes.
