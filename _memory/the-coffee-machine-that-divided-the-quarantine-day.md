By September 2020, I had apparently reorganized part of my day around a filter coffee machine.

The original entry is not dramatic.

There is no crisis in it.

No argument.

No journey.

No great decision.

Just a machine on the counter and a new habit.

After buying the filter coffee machine, I stopped drinking the other kinds of coffee I had been making, even though I already had machines for some of them too.

Turkish coffee no longer felt strong or satisfying enough for me.

Instant coffee had become something I looked at with suspicion.

Filter coffee won.

During those quarantine days, I was drinking it two or three times a day.

That was enough to write down.

Looking back, I think the frequency matters more than the coffee.

Two or three times a day means the drink had become punctuation.

Morning.

Somewhere around the middle.

Maybe later.

The archive does not preserve exact hours, so I should not invent them.

But repetition itself tells us something.

The machine was not an occasional object.

It had entered the rhythm of the house.

Pandemic memories are often described through large things.

Restrictions.

Statistics.

Masks.

Schools.

Hospitals.

Remote work.

Empty streets.

But most of daily life was still lived through very small repetitions.

Wake up.

Make coffee.

Sit down.

Read something.

Work.

Make another coffee.

Walk from one room to another.

Check the news.

Make coffee again.

Days that were globally historic could feel locally repetitive.

That contrast is one of the strangest things about 2020.

Outside:

history.

Inside:

the same cup again.

A machine can become important in that kind of life not because it changes anything major, but because it creates a reliable sequence.

Water.

Coffee.

Switch.

Wait.

Pour.

Sit.

The ritual does not solve the uncertainty outside.

It simply creates ten predictable minutes inside it.

That may be enough.

Humans are good at using rituals this way.

Tea.

Coffee.

Cigarettes.

Cooking.

Walking.

A television episode.

Anything that divides a shapeless day into smaller pieces.

When normal schedules disappear, repeated acts become homemade clocks.

You begin knowing the day by what you have already done.

First coffee.

Second coffee.

Evening.

That is not a formal timetable, but it is structure.

And structure becomes valuable when days begin resembling one another.

The old entry is funny because I wrote about the coffee itself as though I had finally found the correct answer.

Other methods existed.

I had used them.

Some even had their own machines.

Then filter coffee arrived and apparently ended the competition.

This happens with small domestic technologies.

You buy something expecting another option.

Instead it replaces a whole category.

A coffee machine.

An air fryer.

A particular pan.

A good knife.

A streaming device.

Something becomes the default.

The other objects remain physically present but socially retired.

They have not broken.

You simply stop reaching for them.

That is a quiet kind of technological succession.

No announcement.

No formal retirement.

One day you realize:

I have not used that in months.

The new habit won.

Filter coffee had apparently done that to my other coffee routines.

The entry also contains a very specific little market report.

At the time, I wrote that half-kilo packages around 40 lira were perfectly good.

I warned against being too stingy and buying the 20-lira ones.

And, in parentheses, I recorded the dollar exchange rate as 7.43.

That parenthesis is probably my favorite part now.

Not because of economics.

Because it timestamps the cup.

Personal archives become more vivid when they accidentally store prices.

A haircut.

A nightclub entrance fee.

A bottle.

A car.

Coffee.

Money tells you the scale of ordinary decisions at that moment.

I am not going to translate the 40 lira into today's purchasing power.

That would require a separate historical calculation and would not improve the memory.

The important fact is simpler:

in September 2020, I thought 40 lira for half a kilo was a reasonable point for good coffee, and 20 lira was cheap enough to deserve suspicion.

That was my consumer threshold that day.

The exchange-rate note shows I was already mentally connecting everyday purchases with the wider economy.

Dollar: 7.43.

Half kilo: 40 lira.

Coffee remains coffee, but the background enters the cup.

This is how inflation and exchange rates become personal.

Not through charts first.

Through shelves.

You remember when something used to cost this.

You notice the same object becoming that.

Economic history arrives disguised as grocery memory.

The parenthesis does not explain any of that.

It simply preserves the number.

That is what makes it useful.

Archives should keep numbers even when they look unnecessary.

Years later, they become scenery.

The entry also tells me I had become opinionated about coffee rather quickly.

This one is good.

That one is weak.

Instant coffee is questionable.

Do not buy the cheapest beans.

Every habit eventually grows standards.

At first:

coffee.

Later:

which coffee?

At first:

whatever works.

Later:

not that one.

Familiarity produces discrimination.

Not always expert discrimination.

Sometimes just preference with confidence.

I should be careful not to rewrite the 2020 version of myself as a coffee specialist.

The entry does not support that.

I was simply a person who had bought a filter coffee machine, liked the result, and formed strong enough opinions to post them.

That is more ordinary and more believable.

There is also something very domestic in the phrase “even though I have machines for the others.”

A kitchen slowly fills with solutions to previous preferences.

Then preferences change.

The objects stay.

Households are museums of former intentions.

The sandwich maker.

The blender.

The coffee machine.

The second coffee machine.

The device bought during one enthusiastic month.

Every cupboard contains small abandoned futures.

I will use this every day.

I will make this at home now.

I will stop buying that outside.

Sometimes the promise survives.

Sometimes it does not.

Filter coffee apparently survived long enough to take over.

Two or three times a day during quarantine is not a trial period.

That is adoption.

The date matters.

4 September 2020.

By then the pandemic was no longer a strange event from a few weeks earlier.

It had become duration.

That may be why small routines mattered.

Emergency mode cannot remain emotionally intense forever.

Eventually even extraordinary circumstances become daily life.

People adapt.

Not necessarily because conditions become acceptable.

Because the nervous system cannot treat every day as the first day of a crisis.

You begin doing ordinary things inside the abnormal thing.

Buying coffee.

Comparing prices.

Developing preferences.

Complaining about quality.

This is one of the deepest forms of adaptation.

History says:

Pandemic.

The person says:

The 40-lira coffee is better than the 20-lira one.

Both are true at exactly the same time.

I like memories that preserve this scale difference.

They resist the temptation to make every historical period feel dramatic every minute.

Nobody experiences history continuously as history.

Most of the time, people are trying to decide what to eat.

What to watch.

What to drink.

Whether the coffee is worth the money.

That does not make the larger context unimportant.

It makes the human experience more accurate.

The coffee machine did not define 2020.

But it defined some minutes of some days in 2020.

That is enough for a personal archive.

There is another small shift visible in the entry.

Before the machine, different coffee types competed.

After the machine, one method became habitual.

Tools shape taste.

We often imagine preference coming first.

I like this, therefore I buy the tool.

Sometimes the order reverses.

You buy the tool.

Because the tool is there, you use it more.

Because you use it more, the taste becomes familiar.

Because it becomes familiar, other versions start feeling wrong.

Then you say:

I prefer this.

Maybe you always did.

Maybe the machine helped create the preference.

Daily life is full of these feedback loops.

Convenience becomes habit.

Habit becomes taste.

Taste justifies the original purchase.

A very neat system.

The same thing happens with software.

Cars.

Phones.

Kitchen equipment.

Once a workflow becomes frictionless, alternatives begin feeling unnecessarily difficult.

Filter coffee had the advantage of repeatability.

Put things in.

Make enough.

Drink.

Again later.

The exact preparation method is not described in the source, so I will not decorate it with invented measurements or aromas.

The important thing is frequency.

Two or three times.

Every day.

That repetition creates the memory.

There was also probably caffeine involved in the enthusiasm, but that conclusion hardly requires literary analysis.

What interests me more is how a cup can make staying home feel slightly less shapeless.

Coffee gives you a reason to stand up.

Go to the kitchen.

Do something with your hands.

Wait.

Return.

That small interruption matters when work, leisure, news, conversation, and boredom are all happening within the same walls.

One of the problems of staying home for long periods is that activities lose spatial boundaries.

The same chair becomes office.

Cinema.

Café.

Reading room.

Internet terminal.

The coffee trip at least creates movement.

Again, I am interpreting the general function of such routines, not claiming the source says I consciously used coffee this way.

The source only records the habit.

But the habit fits the texture of those months.

There is also a comic kind of seriousness in advising strangers not to be cheap about coffee.

Spend 40.

Avoid 20.

As though society needed guidance urgently.

Perhaps that is exactly what online writing is for.

Not everything has to deserve publication in a traditional sense.

A forum or dictionary archive can record tiny convictions.

This coffee is worth it.

That bar is good.

This teacher was funny.

This laptop is malfunctioning.

This mall smells too clean.

These details eventually form a life more effectively than carefully selected milestones do.

A CV cannot tell you what you drank three times a day during quarantine.

The archive can.

That is why I keep returning to mundane entries.

They restore ordinary time.

Big memories survive on their own.

Graduation.

Military service.

Jobs.

Moving.

Marriage.

Children.

The brain knows those are important.

It keeps them.

But who preserves the coffee price?

Who preserves the exact software you used?

The shampoo brand?

The broken speaker?

The weird breakfast?

Those vanish first.

And yet those details are often what make the past feel inhabitable.

You do not return to 2020 by remembering the word “pandemic.”

You return through a room.

A cup.

A price.

A routine.

A machine.

The exchange rate in parentheses.

That is enough to open a small door.

I also like that the entry does not complain about being stuck at home.

It simply says that during these quarantine days, filter coffee goes very well two or three times a day.

That understated tone may be more revealing than a dramatic diary.

Humans normalize quickly.

Even confinement acquires beverage recommendations.

Perhaps this is resilience.

Perhaps it is just habit.

Probably both.

We cannot remain solemn forever.

At some point someone asks:

What coffee are you drinking?

And life continues.

The mature version of this memory does not need to claim that the coffee machine saved quarantine.

It did not.

It was a coffee machine.

That modesty is important.

Small comforts do not have to become salvation narratives to matter.

A good cup can simply be a good cup.

A routine can make one hour nicer.

That is sufficient.

Not every object has to transform a life.

Sometimes it just improves the afternoon.

And perhaps the problem with modern storytelling is that we keep demanding transformation.

The trip changed me.

The book changed me.

The person changed me.

The crisis changed me.

Most life does not arrive with that kind of force.

Most life accumulates.

A machine gets used every day.

A taste changes.

A price gets remembered.

Months pass.

Years later, the small entry reveals the atmosphere more clearly than the grand statement would have.

September 2020.

At home.

Filter coffee.

Two or three times a day.

Half a kilo around 40 lira.

Dollar at 7.43.

Do not buy the very cheap stuff.

That is not a history of the pandemic.

It is something narrower.

A history of one cupboard during the pandemic.

Sometimes that is exactly the scale memory needs.
