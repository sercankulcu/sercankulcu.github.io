The first password I ever used was:

**15211**

It sounds almost too simple now.

Today, a password like that would probably be rejected before I even finished typing it.

Too short.

Only numbers.

No uppercase letters.

No lowercase letters.

No symbols.

No special characters.

No warning telling me that my entire digital life was about to collapse.

Back then, none of that seemed important.

We were setting up a computer.

Windows 98.

I was still young enough to think that the person who knew how to assemble a computer also knew everything else about computers.

So when he asked:

"What should your password be?"

I gave the most reasonable answer I could think of.

"You know better than me. Make it something good."

He thought for a moment.

Then he said:

"From now on, your password is 15211."

That was it.

No explanation.

No password manager.

No recovery email.

No two-factor authentication.

Just five digits.

And somehow those five digits became mine.

I do not even remember why he chose them.

Maybe they meant something to him.

Maybe they were random.

Maybe he simply looked at something nearby and typed the first number that came to mind.

It did not matter.

Once somebody tells you, at the right age, that something is yours, you tend to believe them.

15211 became my password.

Later, when websites started asking for more complicated passwords, I did what many people probably did.

I did not really create a new system.

I decorated the old one.

Add a few letters.

Put something at the beginning.

Something at the end.

Make it look more complicated.

The original number remained hidden inside.

That is funny to think about now.

Passwords are supposed to be secret and temporary.

They are supposed to change.

They are supposed to protect things.

But some of them become memories.

A password can remind you of a computer.

A room.

A person.

A period of your life.

The sound of a CRT monitor turning on.

The Windows startup screen.

The strange excitement of having a machine in front of you that seemed capable of almost anything.

At the time, I did not understand how much computers would become part of my life.

I only knew that this machine was different from most things I owned.

You could change it.

Install things.

Delete things.

Break things.

Fix things.

Learn things.

You could make it behave differently simply by understanding it a little better.

That was fascinating.

And somewhere inside all of that was a small gate.

A password.

15211.

Passwords used to feel much more personal because we created so few of them.

Now almost every service asks for one.

Email.

Banking.

Shopping.

Social media.

Cloud storage.

Work systems.

Streaming services.

Phones.

Routers.

Applications we barely remember signing up for.

A password has become infrastructure.

Back then, one password could feel like part of the identity of the computer itself.

You turned the machine on.

Windows appeared.

Then the password.

It was one of the first things the computer asked you.

Not because it really needed serious protection.

Mostly because computers were teaching us that personal machines could have personal spaces.

Your files.

Your settings.

Your desktop.

Your password.

It felt important.

Even when the password was 15211.

There is something else I like about remembering it.

People often imagine that their relationship with technology began with some important event.

The first program they wrote.

The first computer science course.

The first operating system they installed.

The first serious project.

In reality, the beginning is usually made of smaller things.

Wondering what a button does.

Changing a wallpaper.

Installing a game.

Copying a music folder.

Watching someone assemble a computer.

Being asked what your password should be.

These moments do not feel important while they are happening.

They become important later because so many other things grow out of them.

I certainly did not sit in front of Windows 98 thinking:

This machine will influence my education, my career, my hobbies and the way I think.

I was probably thinking something much simpler.

I have a password now.

That was enough.

Technology has changed enormously since then.

Passwords became longer.

Security became more serious.

Machines became faster.

Screens became thinner.

The Internet became part of almost everything.

But memory does not update at the same speed.

Somewhere in my head, a computer still turns on.

Windows 98 appears.

Someone asks me what password I want.

And because I have no idea what makes a good password, I give the responsibility to somebody who seems to know better.

He gives me five digits.

15211.

It was not a good password.

But it was a very good first one.
