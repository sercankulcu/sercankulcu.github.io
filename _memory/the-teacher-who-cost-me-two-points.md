---
title: "The Teacher Who Cost Me Two Points"
date: 2010-06-24
tags:
   - school
   - teachers
   - mathematics
   - memories
   - humor
---

Some teachers are remembered because they gave you a good grade.

Some because they changed the direction of your life.

Some because, decades later, you can still hear the exact strange sentence they used in class.

İsmail belonged to the third group.

We called him Köse Dayı.

He was my mathematics teacher.

And according to the entry I wrote in June 2010, he had managed, all by himself, to make my secondary-school achievement score come two points lower than it otherwise would have.

That sounds like a perfectly respectable reason to dislike a teacher.

Yet the same entry immediately says that I liked him.

I liked his outlook on life.

I liked his jokes.

And I still remembered two of his sayings well enough to write them down years later:

“I’ll drag you into a dark room and embarrass you.”

And:

“I’ll throw you out of the window and blood will come from your mouth.”

They sound alarming when removed from context.

That is partly why they are funny.

A mathematics teacher should, in theory, be remembered through mathematics.

Equations.

Functions.

Geometry.

Exams.

Instead, memory performs its own selection.

Years pass and some of the mathematics fades while the teacher’s absurd language remains.

This is one of the mysteries of education.

Teachers spend enormous effort deciding what students must remember.

Students remember something else.

The teacher writes a theorem on the board.

The student remembers the joke made before it.

The curriculum defines the important content.

Memory ignores the curriculum.

That does not mean the lesson failed.

It means education is larger than content.

A teacher enters the room with mathematics and also brings a personality.

Tone.

Timing.

Humor.

Temper.

Phrases.

Ways of reacting.

Ways of seeing students.

Those things become part of the education whether anyone planned them or not.

The old entry contains a contradiction that I now find more interesting than the jokes.

This man cost me two points.

I liked him anyway.

When we are young, grades can feel like the whole relationship with a teacher.

A high grade means good teacher.

A low grade means bad teacher.

At least emotionally, that is often the first model.

But it does not always survive contact with actual people.

A teacher can be strict and memorable.

Demanding and funny.

Unhelpful in one sense and valuable in another.

You can resent the result and still like the person.

That complexity is a sign that school was never only a transaction.

If the only purpose had been maximizing marks, then the calculation would be simple.

Two points lost.

Negative value.

Case closed.

But I remembered his outlook on life.

That means something else had happened in the classroom.

The source does not tell me exactly what that outlook was.

I should not reconstruct a philosophy from two jokes.

I do not know whether he was optimistic, cynical, relaxed, demanding, eccentric, or something else entirely.

The entry simply says I liked the way he looked at life.

That sentence is enough.

It tells me that the teacher had escaped the boundaries of his subject.

He had become a person worth observing.

This is something students notice more than teachers sometimes realize.

A teacher is formally there to teach mathematics.

But students are also watching how an adult behaves.

How he responds when something goes wrong.

How seriously he takes himself.

Whether he can laugh.

Whether he humiliates people.

Whether he is fair.

Whether he enjoys what he does.

Whether life seems to have crushed him.

Young people study adults even when no exam follows.

Teachers become accidental models of adulthood.

Some show what students want to become.

Some show what they desperately do not want to become.

Some simply show that adulthood can contain humor.

Perhaps that was part of Köse Dayı’s appeal.

Again, the archive does not let me say exactly.

But it does let me say this:

His jokes survived.

That matters.

There are teachers whose entire presence disappears after the final exam.

You remember taking the course.

You may remember the grade.

The person becomes blank.

Then there are teachers who remain through one sentence.

A gesture.

A nickname.

A ridiculous threat nobody could mistake for formal pedagogical language.

“Karanlık odaya çekerim, rencide ederim.”

It has rhythm.

That is why it survives.

People remember language that has shape.

Ordinary instructions disappear.

“Please be quiet.”

Forgotten.

“Study for the exam.”

Forgotten.

But an absurd sentence can become permanent.

The brain rewards surprise.

This is why good lecturers use stories, analogies, jokes, and strange examples.

Not merely to entertain.

Surprise gives memory a hook.

Of course, humor can also distract.

A student may remember the joke and forget the theorem.

That is possible.

But education without personality has its own failure mode.

Everything becomes technically correct and emotionally invisible.

Students need reasons to remain mentally present.

A memorable teacher creates presence.

Even irritation can create presence.

This is where the two-point loss becomes interesting.

Marks and memory obey different accounting systems.

The official system says:

Two points less.

The personal archive says:

Still liked him.

Officially, the score matters.

Emotionally, perhaps the person mattered more.

This does not mean grades are unimportant.

For a student, two points can matter very much.

Admissions, rankings, opportunities — numerical differences can produce real consequences.

It would be dishonest to romanticize that away.

A charming teacher should not be excused automatically for unfair evaluation.

The source, however, does not say he was unfair.

It only says his effect on my achievement score was negative.

Those are not the same statement.

Maybe I earned the grade.

Maybe the course was difficult.

Maybe something else happened.

The archive does not explain.

So the honest version leaves that unresolved.

What remains is the emotional contradiction:

He lowered the number.

I liked the man.

That is a mature kind of memory even though I wrote it casually.

It separates evaluation from affection.

Life requires this skill constantly.

Someone can disappoint you and still be good.

Someone can help you and still be unpleasant.

A person can be right about something you wish they were wrong about.

A person can be wrong and still worth loving.

School is one of the first places we encounter these contradictions outside the family.

Teachers have authority over us.

That makes our judgments about them intense.

A teacher controls marks, discipline, access, approval.

Students talk about teachers constantly because power makes personality important.

One teacher gives easy grades.

One never smiles.

One tells the same joke.

One arrives late.

One makes everyone nervous.

One seems to hate the entire class.

One somehow makes a difficult subject bearable.

These reputations spread faster than official course descriptions.

By the time a student enters the classroom, the teacher may already be a character.

Nicknames are part of that culture.

“Köse Dayı.”

A nickname immediately tells us that the formal identity — İsmail, mathematics teacher — was not the only identity students used.

Schools generate unofficial language because institutions are too formal for lived experience.

The timetable has surnames.

Students have nicknames.

The school has departments.

Students have stories.

Official documents preserve employment.

Memory preserves character.

That is why old nicknames can unlock whole periods.

You hear one and suddenly the corridor returns.

Not necessarily in precise visual detail.

But the social world returns.

Who people feared.

Who they liked.

Who they imitated.

Which phrases circulated.

Nicknames belong to oral history.

They are rarely archived properly.

Unless someone writes them down.

In 2010, I did.

I doubt I was thinking about preservation.

I was probably simply describing a teacher I remembered.

But now the entry functions differently.

It preserves not only a person but a style of school memory.

The teacher was not introduced through credentials.

No university degree.

No years of service.

No pedagogical method.

Instead:

Köse Dayı.

Mathematics teacher.

Cost me two points.

Still liked him.

Here are two things he used to say.

That is a student’s biography of a teacher.

And perhaps it is more revealing than the official one.

Students evaluate teachers through experience rather than résumé.

Did the room become interesting when this person entered?

Did you dread the hour?

Did you laugh?

Did you learn?

Did the teacher become a story?

Those questions determine who survives in memory.

There is another reason I like the two absurd sayings.

They belong to a style of humor that depends almost entirely on exaggeration.

The threat is too theatrical to function as literal language.

Dark room.

Embarrassment.

Thrown from a window.

Blood from the mouth.

The excess is the joke.

This kind of classroom language creates a shared code when the relationship allows it.

The same sentence from the wrong teacher could feel cruel.

From another, everyone laughs.

Context creates meaning.

That is why quoted speech is dangerous when detached from atmosphere.

Words do not carry tone perfectly into the future.

An archive preserves the sentence but not the voice.

I know I listed the phrases under “wise sayings” as jokes.

That framing matters.

The entry says I liked him because of his humor.

So I can safely preserve them as part of that humorous memory rather than reinterpret them as literal threats.

Still, changing educational norms make old classroom humor interesting to revisit.

Things teachers said casually in one era might sound bizarre in another.

Students change.

Institutions change.

Expectations about authority change.

Language that once produced laughter may later produce complaints.

Sometimes that is progress.

Sometimes something informal is lost.

Usually both are true.

I do not need to decide which category Köse Dayı belongs to.

The memory belongs to its time.

The point is not to recommend the phrases as modern classroom practice.

The point is that they were his phrases.

They made him memorable.

This distinction matters when writing honestly about the past.

We do not need to modernize every old scene.

We can preserve what happened and still recognize that context changes.

Memory should not become public-relations editing.

If a younger version of me found something funny, that belongs in the record.

If an older version sees additional complexity, that can belong beside it.

Both selves are allowed to speak.

This is what makes revisiting an archive more interesting than simply republishing it.

The old entry gives the raw reaction.

The present can ask why it lasted.

Why do I remember this teacher?

Not because of the two points alone.

If I had truly disliked him, the entry would probably have centered on resentment.

Instead, the loss is almost the setup for affection.

“He cost me two points, but…”

That “but” is the entire story.

It tells us that human judgment refused to behave like arithmetic.

Mathematics teacher.

Very appropriate.

In mathematics:

minus two is minus two.

In memory:

minus two plus humor plus affection does not produce a simple answer.

Perhaps teachers should be pleased by that.

Education is full of numbers.

Exam scores.

Averages.

Attendance percentages.

Rankings.

Credits.

Hours.

But the final effect of a teacher cannot be reduced to them.

A student may leave with a mediocre grade and a lasting respect for the person.

Another may earn the highest grade and forget the class entirely.

The measurable outcome and the remembered outcome diverge.

Institutions need measurement.

People need meaning.

A good education somehow has to survive both.

As someone who later became a teacher myself, this old entry has another layer now.

Students may remember almost none of what you expect.

That is humbling.

You can prepare slides for hours.

Choose examples carefully.

Explain a concept three different ways.

Years later, a former student may approach you and say:

Hocam, do you remember that one thing you said?

And it will be the least important sentence of the semester.

Perhaps something you said while the projector was failing.

Perhaps a joke.

Perhaps a complaint.

Perhaps advice you barely remember giving.

That can feel unfair.

But it is also beautiful.

Teaching leaves traces outside the syllabus.

We do not control which ones remain.

This should make teachers careful.

And perhaps a little less self-important.

The lecture we consider brilliant may disappear.

The small kindness may remain.

The irritated remark may remain too.

Students archive us selectively.

Sometimes brutally.

Köse Dayı’s archive, at least in my writing, consists of two points and two jokes.

That is not much.

Yet it is enough for a person to reappear.

I can no longer reconstruct every mathematics lesson.

I do not need to.

The nickname does some of the work.

The strange phrases do the rest.

There he is.

Not complete.

No memory gives us a complete person.

But recognizable.

That is what small written records can do.

They rescue people from becoming generic.

Without the entry, I might someday remember only:

I had a mathematics teacher I liked.

That sentence has almost no life in it.

With the entry:

His name was İsmail.

We called him Köse Dayı.

He cost me two points.

I still liked him.

And he had a habit of producing threats so exaggerated that they became jokes.

Now there is somebody there.

Specificity restores the person.

It also restores the younger version of me.

A student still keeping score.

Still aware of two lost points.

But already able to say:

I like this teacher anyway.

That may be the part I value most now.

Young people are often accused of reducing teachers to grades.

Sometimes fairly.

But students are capable of more complicated judgments than adults give them credit for.

The number matters.

The person matters separately.

Maybe the teacher hurt your average and improved the texture of school.

Maybe both truths survive.

Years later, which one weighs more?

The fact that I wrote about him at all gives the answer.

The two points became trivia.

Köse Dayı became memory.
