There was a rule on an online community I used: if a title was written as a question, you were not supposed to write an entry that merely answered it.

The principle was easy enough to understand. If the title asked what someone would say and the entry only supplied the answer, the result added almost nothing. I had already been punished once under this rule, and I accepted that my own entry had not been especially valuable.

Then I noticed the boundary.

One version of an entry could be unacceptable because it ended with the words “would say.” Remove those words, and nearly the same entry could survive. The meaning remained almost unchanged; only the grammatical surface had moved to the safe side of the rule.

That was when the rule became interesting.

Once people discover that punishment depends on wording rather than purpose, they stop asking what the community wants and begin asking what exact sentence will survive moderation. The user becomes a small lawyer. One word disappears, one phrase changes, and technically the rule has been obeyed.

Online communities often begin with simple expectations. Do not spam. Do not insult people. Write something useful. Then real users arrive, edge cases multiply, and eventually the community needs an unofficial legal tradition.

I had become part of that tradition.

The strange part was that the original intention still made sense to me. I was not arguing that every bad entry deserved protection. I only wanted the rule to admit what language makes difficult: two sentences can be grammatically different and functionally identical.

A single word stood between punishment and permission.

Naturally, I began paying very close attention to that word.
