title: "The Middle-School Crush Who Kept Reappearing Before Weddings"
date: 2020-09-02

In September 2020, I recorded a pattern that was almost certainly too small to deserve a theory.

Which is exactly why it was funny.

My middle-school crush had added me on Facebook one week before getting married.

One month later, she was divorced.

Years later, before a second marriage, something else happened.

About a week before the wedding, her Instagram account became public.

I noticed.

Two weeks passed.

I waited.

That was the entire entry.

The younger logic was obvious:

Here we go again.

The mature logic should be more careful.

A Facebook add does not cause a divorce.

An Instagram privacy change does not predict a marriage outcome.

There is no evidence in the source that either action had anything to do with me.

There is not even evidence that the timing carried special meaning for her.

The comedy belongs entirely to the observer.

I had seen two events close together once.

Years later, another pre-wedding social-media event appeared.

The brain immediately began building mythology.

This is one of its favorite hobbies.

Humans are pattern engines.

Two points become a line.

Three become destiny.

A coincidence becomes “this always happens.”

Especially when the pattern touches old emotion.

Middle-school crushes are perfect material because they occupy a strange category.

Too early to belong to adult romantic history.

Too emotionally vivid to disappear completely.

You may have had no meaningful relationship.

Perhaps barely any interaction.

The source does not say.

Yet the label survives:

my middle-school crush.

That is enough to reactivate an old version of yourself.

A name appears online.

For a second, the school corridor returns.

Not literally.

Emotionally.

Early crushes often survive not because the person remained important in adult life but because they were attached to the first discovery of wanting to be noticed.

Before relationships became complicated, attraction was often mostly imagination.

See person.

Think about person.

Maybe speak.

Maybe not.

Construct entire internal universe.

Then life moves.

Schools end.

Cities change.

People marry.

Divorce.

Remarry.

The old crush becomes a historical figure inside your own biography.

Then social media does something unprecedented:

it keeps historical figures searchable.

Before Facebook and Instagram, a middle-school crush could genuinely disappear.

You might hear something through mutual friends.

Run into them by chance.

See a wedding announcement.

Otherwise, childhood and adolescence had natural endings.

People left the frame.

Social media weakened those endings.

Now the past can re-enter through a notification.

Friend request.

Follow.

Profile becomes public.

Suggested contact.

A photograph appears because someone else liked it.

The person did nothing specifically toward you, yet the algorithm puts them back in your present.

This changed memory.

Old relationships and old crushes became less buried.

Not necessarily more emotionally important.

More accessible.

That is different.

Accessibility can imitate significance.

If you see someone's name every few months, the brain keeps the file warm.

A person who might have become a faded school memory remains digitally legible.

Married.

Moved.

Child.

Job.

Vacation.

Divorced.

Remarried.

You can know milestones without participating in the life.

That is a strange social position.

Too informed to be a stranger.

Too distant to be a friend.

The internet created millions of these ghost relationships.

People we know about but do not know.

The 2020 entry lives exactly there.

I knew enough to notice wedding timing.

Not enough to know what the timing meant.

So the mind filled the gap with humor.

One week before first marriage:

Facebook add.

One month later:

divorce.

Before second marriage:

Instagram becomes public.

I wait.

The joke is prediction.

But good retelling requires refusing the temptation to imply causality.

We are excellent at rewriting sequences into stories.

A happened.

Then B.

Therefore A meant something about B.

This is how superstition begins.

Sports fans wear the same shirt.

Investors see patterns in charts.

People interpret dreams.

Old crush changes profile visibility before wedding.

Hmm.

The brain hates randomness because randomness gives us no role.

A pattern lets us stand inside the event.

Maybe this means something.

Maybe I am a signal.

Maybe the universe has a sense of humor.

Probably the universe was busy elsewhere.

That does not make the observation less funny.

Coincidences do not need supernatural meaning to be delightful.

In fact, they are often better without explanation.

The first marriage detail is especially sharp.

Added one week before.

Divorced one month later.

Those two facts sit close enough to invite a completely irresponsible narrative.

The younger me leaned into that invitation.

“Again she did not learn.”

That line is comic arrogance.

As if opening Facebook before marriage were a known risk factor.

As if I were an omen.

As if there were a previous lesson available.

This is the kind of humor personal archives preserve well because it depends on the narrator's inflated role.

Reality:

someone from middle school made a social-media action.

Narrator:

History is repeating itself.

Excellent.

We all do this in smaller ways.

A person messages after years.

Why now?

An ex views a story.

What does it mean?

Someone follows, unfollows, likes an old photo.

Digital actions are tiny but ambiguous.

Ambiguity produces interpretation.

The less direct the signal, the more room the imagination gets.

A message saying “I want to talk” is clear.

A profile becoming public is almost content-free.

Therefore it can mean anything.

Social media created a whole vocabulary of weak signals.

Seen.

Liked.

Followed.

Viewed.

Online.

Typing.

Deleted message.

Profile photo changed.

People build emotional narratives from interface states.

The interface was not designed to carry all that meaning.

Users assigned it.

This is one reason digital relationships can be exhausting.

A person may spend more time interpreting metadata than words.

When did they see it?

Why did they not reply?

Why did they like that but not this?

Why public now?

The old entry captures this with unusual efficiency.

Instagram became public.

I noticed timing.

That was enough to start the countdown.

Two weeks.

Still waiting.

Waiting for what exactly?

The source does not say.

Presumably to see whether history would repeat in some comic sense.

That makes the narrator almost like someone watching a sequel.

The first film established a rule.

The second film is testing it.

This is storytelling imposed on life.

Humans do this constantly because stories are easier to remember than independent facts.

First marriage.

Facebook.

Divorce.

Second marriage.

Instagram.

Question mark.

There is structure.

Once structure appears, we want resolution.

The responsible memoirist has to resist inventing one.

The archive stops at two weeks.

So the story stops there.

No claim about what happened afterward.

No conclusion about the marriage.

No suggestion that the social-media action meant regret, curiosity, or interest.

The uncertainty is the correct ending.

That incompleteness actually improves the piece.

We are left with the younger narrator checking the pattern and waiting to see if reality will cooperate.

There is another layer.

The phrase “middle-school crush” compresses decades.

In adulthood, the person may be almost entirely different from the child you remember.

So are you.

Yet the label remains attached.

This can be unfair.

We preserve people in roles they stopped occupying long ago.

First love.

School bully.

Funny kid.

Quiet girl.

Best student.

Crush.

Then we encounter the adult and experience surprise because the old category no longer fits.

Social media complicates this further by showing transformation gradually.

You may see a person age through photographs without ever meeting them again.

The face changes.

Jobs change.

Partners.

Cities.

Children.

But in your private index, the file name remains:

middle-school crush.

Memory is terrible at version control.

This is why old feelings can be amusing rather than threatening.

The adult self looks at the old category with distance.

Not:

I am still in love.

More:

Ah yes, the person who once occupied enormous mental space for reasons I can barely reconstruct.

Then they appear in a feed.

The past waves.

The present scrolls.

The story also says something about how weddings make old connections visible.

Marriage announcements gather timelines.

People search names.

Old classmates react.

Family photos spread.

Profiles become more active.

Dormant networks briefly wake.

A wedding is not only a relationship event.

Online, it is a visibility event.

That may explain some of the timing without any personal meaning at all.

People change privacy settings.

Update photos.

Reconnect with old contacts.

Again, possible.

Not proven.

The source supports observation, not motive.

This distinction is worth emphasizing because nostalgia easily becomes self-centered.

We see an old person's action and assume our shared past is active in their mind too.

Maybe.

Maybe not.

A good memory can admit asymmetry.

The event mattered to me because I noticed it.

It may have been completely ordinary for her.

That is fine.

Personal writing does not require reciprocal significance.

You can tell the truth about your own reaction without claiming access to someone else's.

That is the mature version of this story.

The younger version says:

Look, she is doing it again.

The older version says:

Look how quickly my brain turned two coincidences into a private mythology.

Both are funny.

The second is kinder.

And probably more accurate.

I still love the final image:

Instagram open.

Two weeks passed.

Narrator waiting.

Not for a message.

Not for a meeting.

For the universe to complete a joke.

Sometimes that is enough entertainment.
