One evening in July 2010, my father and I were watching television together.

TRT1 was showing *Gölgeler*.

At some point, the program played short pieces from very old Sezen Aksu clips.

Old enough, apparently, that I did not recognize her immediately.

I asked:

“Who is that?”

My father answered without waiting even a second:

“Sezen Aksu.”

I was genuinely surprised.

“How did you know that, Dad?”

His answer was immediate too:

“Are you supposed to know everything?”

The original sentence included the kind of affectionate verbal decoration fathers sometimes add when they want to make sure the point lands properly.

And that was it.

A few seconds of television.

A name.

A father answering faster than his son.

A son briefly discovering that his father had existed before becoming “Dad.”

That last part is what I see in the memory now.

Children, even adult children, have a strange habit of imagining parents as people whose lives began roughly when ours did.

We know intellectually that this is nonsense.

Of course they had childhoods.

Friends.

Music.

Movies.

Fashions.

Arguments.

Private jokes.

People they admired.

Places they went.

Things they knew long before us.

But emotionally, parents often arrive in memory already cast in the parental role.

Mother.

Father.

Not young man.

Not young woman.

Not someone who once sat in front of a television or radio and learned the faces and voices of a generation.

Then a tiny moment breaks the illusion.

An old singer appears.

You do not know.

Your father does.

Immediately.

Suddenly the timeline expands backward.

That is what I like about this scene.

Nothing sentimental is said.

Nobody tells a childhood story.

My father does not explain when he first heard Sezen Aksu.

He does not say which songs he liked.

He does not describe a concert, a cassette, a radio program, or a year.

The source gives none of those details, so they should remain unknown.

He simply recognizes her.

That instant recognition is enough.

Knowledge has history.

You do not recognize someone that quickly by accident.

At some earlier point, the face became familiar.

The younger version of me was impressed mostly because I had not expected my father to know.

That expectation is funny now.

Why shouldn't he know?

Sezen Aksu had a career before I had much of a memory.

My father had lived through years I knew mainly as dates.

Of course he had cultural references I did not.

The surprising thing was not his knowledge.

It was my surprise.

That is often how generational arrogance works.

We mistake newer knowledge for broader knowledge.

The younger person knows computers.

New music.

Current slang.

New devices.

Internet culture.

Whatever belongs to the present.

That can create the quiet assumption that the older person is always catching up.

Then the television shows something from an earlier layer of culture.

The roles reverse instantly.

Who is that?

Sezen Aksu.

No hesitation.

The student changes.

The teacher does not even need to try.

My father's response makes the scene better because he did not answer modestly.

He could have said:

“Oh, I remember her.”

Or:

“She was famous when I was younger.”

Instead:

“Are you supposed to know everything?”

Perfect.

Parents have a talent for converting a factual victory into a correction of character.

I asked for a name.

I received a lesson in epistemic humility.

That is efficient parenting.

The answer also tells me something about the way we spoke.

There was enough ease for surprise to become teasing immediately.

I could say:

“How did you know?”

He could hear the hidden implication:

How could *you* know something I don't?

Then return it.

Who exactly do you think you are?

This kind of exchange is difficult to preserve through formal descriptions of family relationships.

You can write:

“My father and I were close.”

Maybe true.

“My father had a good sense of humor.”

Maybe true.

But those sentences are flat.

Dialogue does better.

Who is that?

Sezen Aksu.

How did you know?

Are you supposed to know everything?

Now there are two people in the room.

You can feel the relationship.

This is why I value tiny dialogues in an archive.

Long essays preserve beliefs.

Dialogue preserves timing.

The fact that he answered without waiting even a second matters.

If he had stared at the screen, thought for a while, and then guessed, there would be no story.

The speed made it authority.

I did not know.

He knew.

End of debate.

There is another thing old television clips do.

They collapse age.

A famous person appears decades younger than the version you know.

Sometimes recognition becomes difficult because our mental image has frozen at a later point.

A singer's hair is different.

Clothes belong to another era.

Image quality is different.

Face younger.

Movement different.

The entire visual language of television has changed around them.

You know the name but fail to connect the younger face to it.

Someone who watched the person at the time does not have the same problem.

They are not comparing the clip with a later image.

They remember the image itself.

Perhaps that is what happened.

The source only tells me that the clips were very, very old and that I failed to identify Sezen Aksu before my father did.

That is enough to establish the generational asymmetry.

I was looking backward.

He may simply have been recognizing something from his own lived past.

There is a major difference between history and memory.

History says:

This happened before.

Memory says:

I was there when it was happening.

Even popular culture changes depending on which side you occupy.

To one generation, an old song is retro.

To another, it was once new.

To one generation, an old television clip is archival footage.

To another, it is a face returning.

This is why nostalgia cannot be inherited completely.

You can love music from before your time.

Watch old films.

Learn the references.

Collect records.

But you cannot recreate the original newness.

You know the future of the thing.

The people who encountered it then did not.

They heard the song before it became a classic.

Saw the singer before biography settled around her.

The culture was still present tense.

My father carried some of that present tense with him.

I did not.

Maybe that is part of what surprised me.

Parents contain inaccessible decades.

We know pieces because they tell stories.

But much remains stored in ordinary recognition.

A song starts and they know the next line.

An actor appears and they know the name.

A street comes up and they remember what used to stand there.

Someone mentions a brand and they know the old package.

A politician's name produces an immediate reaction.

A household object brings back a whole vanished routine.

These are not stories yet.

They are compressed archives.

Sometimes a question opens one.

Sometimes it does not.

That night, mine did not open very far.

I did not ask:

When did you first hear her?

What did you listen to then?

What were you doing in those years?

Maybe I should have.

But I do not want to rewrite the younger self into a more thoughtful interviewer.

He was watching television.

He asked who the woman was.

His father beat him to the answer.

He laughed.

That is honest.

We often realize too late which questions we could have asked our parents.

Not necessarily because they are gone.

Even while they are present, time passes.

Memory changes.

Details disappear.

We become interested in family history at a different age from the one when those stories were casually available.

As children, we want parents to explain the world.

Later, we want them to explain themselves.

Those are not the same questions.

Who were you before me?

What did you listen to?

What did you fear?

What did you want?

Who were your friends?

What did a normal Friday night look like?

What made you laugh?

Which singer could you identify in one second?

The last question, at least, has an answer.

Sezen Aksu.

It is tempting to turn this tiny moment into a larger portrait of my father.

I should resist.

The source does not give enough.

It gives one television program.

One singer.

One exchange.

That smallness is not a weakness.

A person's presence can survive through one line.

Some family memories work almost like photographs.

They do not explain the entire relationship.

They simply preserve a pose.

This one is auditory.

My father's voice saying, in effect:

Do you think you're the only one who knows things?

There he is.

I also like the mild generational revenge inside the scene.

Younger people frequently become technical support for older relatives.

How do I open this?

Where did the file go?

Why is the phone doing that?

What password?

Which button?

The young person becomes expert by default because the device belongs to the young person's era.

This can become dangerous for the ego.

Then an old clip appears.

The father identifies the singer instantly.

Balance restored.

Every generation has its native interface.

His was not necessarily a device.

It was time.

There are things you know because you studied them.

Things you know because someone taught you.

And things you know because you were alive in the correct decade.

That last category has no substitute.

I can search old television archives now.

I can read dates.

I can know discographies more accurately than someone who lived through them.

But factual completeness is different from recognition.

Recognition contains repetition.

Context.

Emotion.

Background exposure.

The face appearing again and again through years.

A culture slowly embedding itself.

That is what makes certain answers immediate.

No search.

No reasoning.

Sezen Aksu.

The conversation also reminds me how fathers sometimes refuse to explain their authority.

You expect evidence.

They give attitude.

How do you know?

Because I know.

Why are you surprised?

Move on.

There is something satisfying about that confidence when the fact is correct.

He had earned the one-second victory.

My response in the entry was basically punctuation and laughter.

That seems right.

No comeback available.

The father had won the round.

Family life contains thousands of such miniature contests.

Who remembers the road?

Who knows the actor?

Who can fix the thing?

Who was right about the weather?

Who predicted the result?

None matter.

All matter temporarily.

They create the rhythm of familiarity.

People who live together accumulate harmless competitions because shared life needs play.

Teasing is one form.

Correcting each other another.

Remembering differently another.

“I told you.”

“No, you didn't.”

“Yes, I did.”

“Who is that?”

“Sezen Aksu.”

“How did you know?”

That is family texture.

Not the major holidays.

Not the formal photographs.

The small verbal collisions between people who know each other well enough to be slightly rude without creating distance.

I would rather preserve those than polish everything into sentimental dialogue.

Real affection often has elbows.

It interrupts.

Mocks.

Corrects.

Says the thing slightly harder than necessary because the relationship can absorb it.

That is not universal, and not every harsh exchange is affectionate.

But in this source, the closing smile makes the tone clear enough.

I was amused.

The line landed.

Years later, I am still amused.

The television program itself is almost incidental now.

*Gölgeler* provided the trigger.

The old Sezen Aksu clip provided the test.

The actual subject became knowledge between generations.

Who owns the past?

The person who recognizes it.

For one second, my father had an entire world behind his answer that I did not.

That world is what I notice now.

As children, parents appear old.

As adults, we begin doing arithmetic.

How old was he when this happened?

How old was she when I was born?

What year was that photograph?

Suddenly parents become younger in retrospect.

You look at an old picture and realize:

He was younger than I am now.

That realization can rearrange family memory.

Father stops being a permanent category.

He becomes a man moving through time.

The same is true here, though there is no photograph.

A very old music clip points backward toward years in which my father was simply younger.

Before some of the roles I associate with him hardened.

Before I knew him in the way a son knows a father.

The archive does not tell me what those years were like.

It only opens the door by one centimeter.

That is enough to make me curious.

Perhaps that is the best function of small memories.

They do not answer.

They make the missing world visible.

I also see something about myself in the exchange.

The young version of me clearly enjoyed knowing things.

That should surprise nobody reading the archive.

There are technical definitions.

Historical facts.

Aviation facts.

Programming notes.

Random trivia.

Names.

Dates.

Statistics.

Knowing was part of the fun.

Then my father knew something first.

The shock escaped before diplomacy:

How did you know?

Not:

Oh, you know her.

How?

As though knowledge required authorization.

His answer was exactly the medicine required.

Everything has to be known by you, does it?

No.

Apparently not.

That lesson has aged well.

The older I get, the more obvious the size of what I do not know becomes.

Every person carries domains where they can answer before I finish the question.

A mechanic hears a sound.

A musician recognizes three notes.

A farmer looks at a plant.

A parent recognizes an old singer.

Expertise is not always credentialed.

Sometimes it is simply accumulated life.

One danger of education is believing formal knowledge is the main form of knowledge.

It is not.

Lived familiarity can be extraordinarily precise.

Someone may not explain why they know.

They just know.

Pattern recognition built over years.

That is true in engineering too.

Experienced people sometimes diagnose a problem from a symptom that seems insignificant to everyone else.

You ask:

How did you know?

They may give a technical explanation.

Or they may say:

I've seen it before.

Parents have their own version.

I've lived it before.

The old clip belonged to that category.

Seen before.

Known before.

Remembered before I could recognize it.

There is a final reason I like the story.

Nothing happens after the punchline.

That restraint feels almost cinematic.

Father answers.

Son is surprised.

Father returns the challenge.

Cut.

A longer source might have weakened it.

Memory often improves through compression.

We do not need the rest of the evening.

What we ate.

What happened after the program.

Whether another singer appeared.

The television was on.

The past appeared briefly on screen.

My father recognized it.

I did not.

He enjoyed the victory.

I wrote it down.

And sixteen years later, the joke has changed slightly.

Back then, the funny part was:

My father knew Sezen Aksu from an ancient clip.

Now the funny part is:

Why did I ever think he wouldn't?
