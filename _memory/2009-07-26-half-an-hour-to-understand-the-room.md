We entered a large nightclub in Montreal and spent the first half hour trying to understand what kind of place we had entered.

The building seemed to change character from one level to the next. One floor had the familiar things: a bar, a crowd, music, a wide area where people danced without caring much about the official boundaries. Another level felt more private. At the top, people sat down, smoked, rested, and looked as if the night had already used most of their energy.

Before going inside, we had even tried to explain something to the doorman that he did not seem to understand. We were a group of men and assumed that might matter. His confusion told us more than an explanation could have: apparently we had brought a rule with us that did not belong there.

Inside, the same thing kept happening. People stood closer than I expected, dressed differently from what I was used to seeing, and treated certain kinds of interaction as completely ordinary. Nobody explained anything because nobody else seemed to need an explanation.

So we watched.

Travel often works this way. The obvious differences are easy because you expect them: language, streets, money, food. The difficult differences are the invisible ones, the rules everybody follows without knowing they are rules.

After a while, the place became easier to read. The building had not changed. We had simply stopped comparing every movement with the version of nightlife we already knew.

The entrance had cost fifteen dollars. That was the only part of the evening that arrived with a clear number.

Everything else took about half an hour.
