title: "He Stayed"
date: 2020-07-22

I met him on a diving boat.

We had been talking casually when he told me that years earlier he had come to the coast for a one-month holiday. He was around forty then. At the end of the month, he did not go back.

He stayed.

The sentence was so simple that it made the decision sound simple too. He had arrived with one life and, somewhere between the sea, the boats, and the ordinary days of that month, decided to build another one.

I looked toward the shore while he spoke. From the water, the town seemed to offer exactly the kind of lie a tired person wants to believe: that another life is waiting somewhere beautiful, and all you need to do is remain there.

I returned many times after that.

Each visit produced the same thought. Perhaps I could stay too. I could wake early, swim, know the people in the small shops, own fewer things, and somehow become a calmer version of myself.

Then the holiday ended and I went home.

The following year, I returned and imagined it again.

He had done what I only rehearsed in my mind. That difference interested me more than the town itself.

A person can spend years wanting another life without wanting the cost of changing the current one. Moving is not only choosing a new place. It means leaving routines, work, people, certainty, and the comfortable knowledge of what tomorrow will probably look like.

The man on the boat had crossed that line. I did not know how difficult it had been for him, and I did not ask enough questions to find out. I only heard the finished version: I came for a month and stayed.

Perhaps all successful changes sound simple afterward.

I kept visiting without staying, and eventually I stopped treating that as failure. Not every place we love needs to become home. Some places have another purpose.

For me, that town became proof that a life could turn in another direction.

I continued on my own road, but after meeting him I could no longer pretend it was the only road available.
