In February 2010, I wrote a long, irritated entry about men, women, proposals, confidence, and rejection.

Most of the generalizations have not aged well.

One small story inside it has.

I had been friends with a woman for three and a half years.

During that friendship, according to the old entry, she sometimes complained about her appearance.

She criticized several features.

She liked her eyes.

I agreed about the eyes.

After three and a half years, I finally told her I wanted something more than friendship.

Her immediate response was:

“You fell for my looks.”

That answer completely disrupted the script in my head.

The response I thought of but did not say was brutally sarcastic.

I mentally listed the very physical features she herself had complained about and wondered how appearance could possibly be the explanation she chose.

I did not say it.

Probably wise.

The younger me treated the scene as evidence in a theory about women becoming more confident the moment someone asks them out.

The older version interests me for another reason.

We had known each other for three and a half years.

I believed that duration should prove something about motive.

She apparently interpreted the proposal through a completely different frame.

This is another example of how two people can live in the same relationship and possess different stories about it.

Friendship is especially vulnerable to this.

One person may experience years of closeness as evidence that romance has slowly formed.

The other may experience the same years as evidence that the relationship is safely non-romantic.

Same history.

Opposite conclusion.

That is why “we have known each other for years” does not predict what happens when one person changes the category.

The category change itself can rewrite the past.

Suddenly old conversations are reinterpreted.

Was that flirting?

Was that why he called?

Did she know?

Was I obvious?

Did I misunderstand everything?

The proposal is not only about the future.

It reaches backward and destabilizes the archive.

This is what makes confessing feelings to a friend frightening.

You are not merely asking:

Would you like to date?

You may also be asking the friendship to survive new information.

Afterward, the other person knows the previous relationship contained something they may not have recognized.

That can create pressure even if nobody intended harm.

The 2010 version of me focused on the humiliation of asking.

“Your charisma gets destroyed,” roughly speaking.

That was young masculine pride talking.

Rejection felt like public loss even when the conversation was private.

The more mature way to see it is that vulnerability is not loss of status.

It is exposure to uncertainty.

You want an answer you cannot control.

That is uncomfortable.

People often protect themselves with sarcasm before and after such moments.

If she says no, make fun of the answer.

If he hesitates, call him cowardly.

If she asks for time, complain about games.

Humor can protect dignity.

It can also prevent us from hearing what the other person is actually saying.

Her answer — “you fell for my looks” — may have had meanings I did not understand.

Maybe it was disbelief.

Maybe insecurity.

Maybe deflection.

Maybe humor.

Maybe a test.

Maybe simply her honest interpretation.

The source does not tell us.

I cannot reconstruct her mind.

What I can preserve is my surprise.

I had expected the three-and-a-half-year friendship to make my reasons obvious.

It did not.

That is the useful lesson.

No amount of private reasoning becomes shared reasoning automatically.

We believe people know why we love them because we have been loving them internally for a long time.

They do not have access to that history.

They see behavior.

Often ambiguous behavior.

If you never say the important sentence, you cannot later complain that the other person failed to infer it correctly.

The old entry also argues that people become increasingly hesitant after bad experiences.

That part remains recognizable.

Every new attempt carries ghosts from previous ones.

You remember the time you misread interest.

The time you waited too long.

The time you spoke too soon.

The awkward answer.

The silence.

The friendship that changed.

Experience can improve judgment.

It can also overfit the past.

One bad result becomes a universal rule.

Never confess first.

Never date a friend.

If they need time, it means no.

If they say X, they really mean Y.

These rules feel protective because they reduce uncertainty.

They also reduce reality.

Different people are different.

Obvious statement.

Difficult practice.

The younger me wanted a formula.

Men answer directly.

Women make you wait.

Men become hesitant because of accumulated damage.

Women want certainty.

The entry tries to build a system out of disappointment.

This is what humans do when emotional events feel unpredictable.

We theorize.

If I can identify the rule, perhaps next time I can avoid humiliation.

Romance resists this.

The sample size is too small.

Variables are uncontrolled.

Everyone lies occasionally, including to themselves.

Feelings change during the experiment.

Terrible science.

Still, we keep producing theories.

Perhaps because uncertainty is harder to tolerate than being wrong.

A false rule gives confidence.

“No rule” gives only judgment.

The story's strongest image appears near the end of the old entry.

I compared a hesitant man to someone trapped in an elevator between two floors.

He cannot move up.

Cannot escape downward.

A woman looking from the upper floor would need to reach out.

That image is melodramatic.

It is also better than the gender theory around it.

After enough rejection or confusion, people can become emotionally stuck between desire and action.

Wanting connection.

Unable to risk asking.

That state is not specifically male.

People of any gender can live there.

The elevator is built from memory.

Every failed attempt adds a cable.

Every embarrassment adds a warning sign.

Eventually the person believes caution is maturity.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes it is fear with good vocabulary.

Three and a half years is a long time to wait before changing a friendship.

The source does not say whether I had wanted romance for all three and a half years.

So I will not claim that.

It says only that after being normal friends for that period, I made the proposal.

Perhaps feelings emerged late.

Perhaps not.

That uncertainty matters.

But the long friendship clearly raised the emotional stakes.

This was not a stranger.

No clean exit.

History existed before the question.

That may explain why the response stayed memorable enough to write about years later.

The younger me wanted to answer:

Looks? Which looks?

The older me is glad he apparently did not.

Some thoughts should remain drafts.

A sarcastic response might have won the moment and damaged the person.

Embarrassment often produces the desire to transfer pain.

You hurt my pride.

I will hurt yours.

Not acting on that impulse is one small form of adulthood, even if the restraint happened accidentally.

What would have been the better response?

The source does not preserve what I actually said, so I will not rewrite the scene with an ideal line.

That is the point.

Memory gives us the answer we received and the answer we swallowed.

Between those two is character.

Years later, I no longer need the event to prove anything about women or proposals.

One person said something unexpected after three and a half years of friendship.

I was stunned.

I thought of a cruel joke.

I did not say it.

That is enough story.

Perhaps more than enough.

The old entry tried to turn rejection into theory.

The memory works better without the theory.

Two people can know each other for years and still misunderstand what they mean to each other.

No formula required.
