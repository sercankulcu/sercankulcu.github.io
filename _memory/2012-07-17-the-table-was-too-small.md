I had a simple theory about okey: the game needed more than tiles and four players.

It needed a large table. It needed tea in the familiar glass and saucer. In my memory, it also came with the smoky air of the places where I had learned to play it, even if that part is better left in the past.

This created a problem when someone suggested playing in a modern coffee shop.

The table was too small.

That was enough to make the whole idea feel wrong. Four racks, discarded tiles, cups, hands, elbows, and conversation all needed territory. A small café table turned a relaxed game into a negotiation over square centimeters.

The rules of okey did not forbid the place. Nothing in the game demanded a particular chair, tea glass, or room. Yet the setting had become part of the game through repetition.

The click of tiles belonged with certain sounds.

The tea belonged with certain objects.

The table belonged with enough space to forget the table existed.

A game can travel, of course. Traditions survive because people carry them into new places and allow some things to change.

But geometry is less flexible than culture.

Four people still need room.
