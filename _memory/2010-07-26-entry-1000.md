When my thousandth entry arrived, I could have written about the number.

Nine hundred and ninety-nine entries were behind it, which seemed large enough to justify a speech. I could have looked back, counted years, or congratulated myself for reaching a clean decimal milestone.

Instead, I wrote about someone.

The person was still new to the online community at the time, and I wished that they would soon be fully welcomed. I made a playful blessing from the meaning of their nickname, then wrote the sentence that gave the entry its real purpose: this person had made number one thousand meaningful.

The nickname does not need to survive here.

The feeling does.

Online communities were full of numbers: entry counts, generations, votes, dates, statuses. They made participation measurable. A round number could suddenly feel important even though nothing magical happened between 999 and 1000.

The number only gave me a place to stop.

When I stopped, I thought of a person.

That seems more important now than the milestone itself. Numbers are good at showing accumulation. They are much worse at explaining why any of it mattered.

I do not know what happened to that relationship afterward, and the old entry does not provide an ending. It does not need one.

At that moment, someone had become part of the meaning of the place.

Entry 1000 did not become memorable because it was the thousandth.

It became memorable because I gave it away.
