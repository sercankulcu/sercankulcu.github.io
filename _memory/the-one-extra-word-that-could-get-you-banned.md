In July 2010, I wrote about one of the strangest kinds of rule:

the rule that makes sense until you look at it too closely.

The setting was Ekşi Sözlük.

The issue was simple enough.

There were question-shaped titles.

And there was a rule against writing an entry that merely answered the question in the title.

At first glance, fair enough.

A title says:

What would Adnan say to Bihter?

Then someone writes:

He would say X.

That is not much of an entry.

It is simply completing the sentence.

The rule was apparently meant to stop that kind of thing.

The problem was that the boundary could become absurdly thin.

I had already been “lanetlenmiş” once because of this rule.

The exact old platform terminology matters because it carries the atmosphere better than a modern translation would.

There were consequences.

You could be removed.

You could be punished.

You could discover that one apparently harmless grammatical choice had crossed a line you did not realize was there.

So I wrote an example.

The title asked what someone would say.

The wrong version, according to my understanding of the rule, was essentially:

“See: [answer] — he would say.”

The supposedly correct version was simply:

“See: [answer].”

One extra word.

“Would say.”

That was enough to transform the entry from acceptable into unacceptable.

At least that was how I understood the rule.

And I found this ridiculous.

Not because I believed my own bad example deserved protection.

I explicitly admitted that the entry which had got me punished deserved what happened.

My complaint was different.

If the logic of the rule is that answering the question in the title is forbidden, then removing the words “would say” does not magically stop the remaining entry from being an answer.

The meaning is still sitting there.

Only the grammar has changed.

That is the part that interested me.

You could obey the surface of a rule while violating its intention.

Or violate the surface while doing almost exactly the same thing as the permitted version.

This is where rules become comedy.

A good rule tries to separate useful behavior from useless behavior.

A bad implementation often separates one wording from another.

The person subject to the rule quickly learns the difference.

Not:

What is the community trying to encourage?

But:

What exact string gets me in trouble?

This is how people become lawyers.

Not necessarily because they love law.

Because vague rules create amateur legal scholars.

Anyone who has spent enough time inside an online community knows this process.

At first, you behave naturally.

Then something is deleted.

You ask why.

Someone points to a rule.

You read it.

You think:

That cannot possibly mean what they say it means.

Then someone gives you an example.

Now you know.

Not the principle.

The boundary.

The next time you approach it, you change one word.

One punctuation mark.

One formulation.

Technically compliant.

Spiritually identical.

Welcome to governance.

The old internet was full of small communities with enormous constitutions.

Forums had rules.

IRC channels had rules.

Message boards had rules.

File-sharing communities had ratio rules.

Gaming servers had rules.

Wikis had rules.

Ekşi Sözlük had rules.

Every community that survived long enough eventually discovered that people could turn almost any social interaction into a procedural dispute.

Someone would post something annoying.

A moderator would prohibit it.

Someone else would imitate the annoying thing in a slightly different form.

The rule would expand.

Then someone would find another edge case.

Soon what began as:

Please do not be annoying

became:

Article 14, subsection C, exception 2.

This is not because moderators are uniquely bureaucratic.

It is because human beings are extremely creative when asked not to do something.

Tell people:

Do not answer question titles directly.

Someone will ask:

What counts as “directly”?

Does a link count?

Does a reference count?

What if I remove the verb?

What if the title contains the answer already?

What if the entry is ironic?

What if the answer is also a standalone definition?

What if I technically do not use a sentence?

A simple principle meets language.

Language wins.

This was particularly visible in Ekşi Sözlük because the platform was built from language itself.

Everything depended on titles and entries.

The difference between a good entry and a useless one was often not mechanical.

It was editorial.

Contextual.

Social.

Tone mattered.

Intent mattered.

Whether something added information mattered.

But those are difficult things to enforce consistently.

Machines like exact rules.

Communities often pretend they can have them.

Then humans arrive.

The result is usually a collection of edge cases.

My 2010 complaint lives inside one of those edge cases.

I was not making a grand argument about censorship.

That would be too much.

The source is much smaller.

I had been punished once.

I understood that my own entry deserved criticism.

But I looked at the accepted formulation and thought:

Come on.

These two things are functionally the same.

That reaction is familiar far beyond online communities.

It happens in universities.

A form is rejected because one box is empty even though the information exists elsewhere.

A document is unacceptable because the date is written in the wrong format.

A request must be submitted before 17:00, but 17:01 belongs to another universe.

A file must be named exactly this way.

A signature must be blue.

A sentence must not contain the extra word.

At some point, procedure separates from purpose.

Then the person following the procedure begins optimizing for the procedure rather than the purpose.

This is one of bureaucracy's oldest tricks.

And online communities recreated it almost instantly.

We like to imagine digital communities as flexible alternatives to institutions.

Then we build institutions inside them.

Moderators become administrators.

Rules become policy.

Users learn loopholes.

Old users explain unwritten customs to new ones.

Appeals happen.

Punishments get names.

People argue about precedent.

Civilization speedruns itself.

The interesting thing is that such systems are not necessarily bad.

Rules are often what allow large communities to remain readable.

Without moderation, a discussion space can quickly become unusable.

Spam.

Repetition.

Personal attacks.

Low-effort posts.

Noise.

A platform like Ekşi Sözlük depended on at least some shared expectation about what counted as an entry worth keeping.

So the mature version of the story is not:

Rules are stupid.

It is:

Rules become fragile when they try to encode judgment too precisely.

The original intention may have been sound.

Do not produce entries that merely complete question titles.

Good.

But meaning does not live in one word.

Remove “would say,” and the answer can remain an answer.

The rule may now be technically satisfied while the original problem survives.

That is the difference between syntax and semantics.

I did not use those terms in the old entry.

But the distinction is there.

Syntax asks:

What words are present?

Semantics asks:

What is this doing?

Communities frequently regulate syntax because semantics is harder.

It is easier to say:

Do not write this phrase.

Harder to say:

Do not write something that contributes nothing beyond mechanically answering the title.

The second requires judgment.

Judgment creates inconsistency.

Inconsistency creates complaints.

So we reach for rules.

Rules create loopholes.

Loopholes create more rules.

And now everyone is tired.

There is a software analogy available here, but I do not want to turn every memory into one.

The social version is already clear enough.

People want fairness.

Fairness often feels like consistency.

Consistency encourages formal rules.

Formal rules eventually encounter situations they were not designed for.

Then someone either uses judgment or obeys the letter.

Neither choice satisfies everyone.

That is the real tension.

My younger self was mostly interested in the absurdity.

One extra word.

Really?

I had enough awareness to admit:

Fine, my own bad entry deserved to go.

That admission makes the complaint stronger.

I was not asking for my mistake to be declared brilliant.

I was asking the rule to make conceptual sense.

If this is forbidden because it answers the title, why is the nearly identical answer permitted once one word disappears?

That is a reasonable question.

The old entry ends with something close to:

This is what I understand, anyway.

That ending matters.

I was not claiming official authority.

I was reconstructing policy from experience.

This is another classic feature of community rules.

There is the written rule.

Then there is what users believe the rule means.

Then there is what moderators actually do.

These three things may overlap.

They may also be three different countries.

Experienced users learn policy through incidents.

This got deleted.

That did not.

This person was banned.

That person was warned.

Therefore the actual rule must be somewhere between the examples.

It is common law created from moderation outcomes.

New users read documentation.

Old users say:

No, no. Here is how it really works.

Every mature community develops this oral layer.

The archive preserves me participating in it.

Here is the rule.

Here is my example.

Here is what happened to me.

Be careful.

Think about every word.

That last part is very Ekşi.

Think about every word.

It sounds almost literary.

In context, it meant:

A single extra word may get you thrown out.

Writing under rules changes how you write.

You begin editing not only for clarity but for survivability.

Does this count as a definition?

Is this too personal?

Is this a direct answer?

Can this be interpreted as chat?

Should I remove the final phrase?

Does the reference make it acceptable?

The platform enters the sentence.

This is true of every platform.

Twitter's character limit changed writing.

SMS changed writing.

Forums changed writing.

Search engines changed headlines.

SEO changed vocabulary.

Social-media moderation changes phrasing.

Algorithmic ranking changes what people choose to say.

The container is never neutral.

Writers learn its shape.

In 2010, I was learning the shape of Ekşi Sözlük one deleted or punished edge case at a time.

There is a reason old communities create such intense attachment.

You do not merely use them.

You learn them.

Their interface.

Their language.

Their taboos.

Their legendary incidents.

Their moderation habits.

Their in-jokes.

Eventually competence itself becomes a form of belonging.

A newcomer says something obviously forbidden.

Old users immediately recognize it.

Not because they are smarter.

Because they have scars.

I had one.

I had been lanetlenmiş once.

Therefore I had knowledge.

Pain is an efficient onboarding system.

This sounds dramatic for a website.

That is part of the humor.

Online communities repeatedly turn tiny events into real emotion.

An entry is deleted.

You are annoyed for an hour.

Someone upvotes something.

You are pleased.

A moderator warns you.

You feel misunderstood.

Your account status changes.

You shout at work.

The stakes are invented, but the feelings are not.

Humans are good at making symbolic systems matter.

That is why communities work at all.

The rules matter because belonging matters.

If you did not care about the place, being punished there would be irrelevant.

You would close the tab.

Instead, you learn the rule.

Complain about it.

Adjust.

Stay.

That is membership.

The funny part is that every online generation believes its moderation disputes are unprecedented.

They are not.

Change the interface and vocabulary, and the argument remains.

Why was my post removed?

Why is this allowed but mine is not?

What exactly does this rule mean?

Why did that person get away with it?

Is context considered?

Is the moderation automated?

Can I appeal?

People were asking versions of these questions long before modern social media.

They will probably ask them after today's platforms are gone.

Governance is unavoidable anywhere humans gather.

So are complaints about governance.

The 2010 entry is a small fossil from that cycle.

It also captures a web culture in which being removed from a community could feel more final.

Accounts and identities were more local.

If you lost standing in one place, you could not necessarily recreate the exact social world somewhere else.

The audience.

History.

Nickname.

References.

Reputation.

All were tied to that community.

This encouraged people to learn the local rules carefully.

Today identity is still platform-specific, but users are often spread across many spaces at once.

Then, one community could occupy a larger share of your online social life.

That made its peculiar procedures matter.

Still, I do not want to romanticize bad rules simply because they are old.

Nostalgia should not turn irritation into wisdom automatically.

Some rules were genuinely silly.

Some enforcement was inconsistent.

Some community customs were exclusionary.

Some punishments were disproportionate.

Old internet culture was not better merely because it was smaller.

It was simply different.

More local.

More idiosyncratic.

Sometimes more human.

Sometimes more arbitrary.

Often both at once.

That duality is what this memory preserves.

The rule had a sensible intention.

The implementation could look absurd.

My own punished example was bad.

The accepted alternative was not obviously better.

All those things can be true simultaneously.

Maturity is often learning to resist the desire for one clean verdict.

Was the moderator right?

Maybe.

Was the rule coherent?

Not entirely.

Was I annoyed?

Clearly.

Did I stay?

Obviously.

Case closed.

Or perhaps not.

The most interesting part now is how much energy once went into distinctions that have completely disappeared from my daily life.

I no longer wake up worried about whether one word will make an Ekşi entry count as answering a question title.

That concern belonged to a particular ecosystem.

This is exactly why it is worth preserving.

Personal history is not only major events.

It is also obsolete anxieties.

Things that once occupied mental space and now require explanation.

A person can reconstruct an era by asking:

What were people worried about that nobody worries about now?

Will my modem disconnect?

Is the CD scratched?

Do I have enough SMS messages left?

Will this entry get me lanetlenmiş?

Technology changes.

The old problems become charming.

Not because they were pleasant.

Because distance reduces their power.

I can now look at the 2010 version of myself carefully comparing two almost identical entries and smile.

He had a point.

He was also spending a lot of intellectual energy on one extra word.

Both facts are worth keeping.

There is a broader writing lesson hidden here too.

Rules can make writers pay attention.

Even foolish constraints can sharpen awareness.

If one word matters procedurally, you begin seeing one word.

The habit is transferable.

Remove what is unnecessary.

Ask what the sentence is actually doing.

Notice when wording changes form but not meaning.

The platform may have taught this accidentally.

That does not justify the rule.

It merely shows that people extract lessons from strange places.

A moderation dispute can become editing practice.

An annoyance can become memory.

A one-word difference can survive sixteen years.

That may be the greatest irony.

The word that supposedly did not belong in the entry became the reason the entry was worth remembering.

Without it, nothing happens.

With it, punishment.

Then complaint.

Then archive.

Then, years later, this story.

Apparently one extra word can do quite a lot.
