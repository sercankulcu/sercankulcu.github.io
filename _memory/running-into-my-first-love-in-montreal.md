title: "Running Into My First Love in Montreal"
date: 2009-07-30
tags:

   - memory

---

There are some people you expect to meet again.

Old classmates.

Former colleagues.

Friends of friends.

People who still live in the same city.

Their reappearance may surprise you, but it does not feel impossible.

Then there are the people who belong to a sealed part of your life.

You do not necessarily think they are gone forever.

You simply stop expecting the world to put them in front of you.

In July 2009, I wrote a very short entry about meeting my first love again.

The place made the encounter almost absurd.

A strip club.

I was in Montreal.

I was only there for about a week.

And somehow, in a city far from home, inside a place where I would never have expected the past to be waiting for me, there she was.

Dancing.

The old entry does not explain our childhood or youth together.

It does not say when we had known each other, how long the relationship had lasted, or how it ended.

It does not tell me how many years had passed.

So I will not invent any of that now.

The archive gives me only the collision.

First love.

Unexpected city.

Unexpected place.

A conversation.

Her life story.

And the feeling that the more I heard, the more difficult it became to leave the encounter behind.

That is enough.

Sometimes the most powerful memories are powerful because they arrive without preparation.

If you plan to meet someone from your past, memory has time to organize itself.

You imagine the conversation.

You wonder how they look now.

You decide what to say about your own life.

You prepare a version of yourself.

Unexpected meetings remove that protection.

The past simply appears.

No warning.

No rehearsal.

You see a face and several versions of time occupy the same room.

Who the person was.

Who you were.

Who both of you have become.

The mind is asked to process all of it immediately.

That can be disorienting anywhere.

In a strip club in Montreal, it becomes almost unreal.

Travel already creates a temporary identity.

When you are away from home for a short period, especially only a week, the city does not yet feel like part of your normal life.

You are a visitor.

You walk differently.

Look at streets more carefully.

Notice signs.

Prices.

People.

You enter places partly because you may never return.

The ordinary rules of home loosen slightly.

The city feels separate from the biography you left behind.

Then biography walks into the room.

That is what makes the encounter so striking to me now.

Montreal should have been outside the map of that old relationship.

Instead, the map folded.

A person from an earlier life appeared inside a temporary one.

Distance failed.

This happens occasionally and it always feels larger than probability.

You travel hundreds or thousands of kilometers and see someone connected to home.

You choose one street.

One building.

One hour.

And there they are.

The brain immediately asks:

What are the odds?

Usually, the actual odds are less mystical than the feeling suggests.

Cities concentrate people.

Social and professional routes overlap.

Chance does strange things simply because life gives it many opportunities.

But probability is not the point when you are the person inside the event.

Emotion does not calculate sample size.

It says:

Of all places.

Of all nights.

Here.

That sentence is the beginning of many memorable stories.

Of all places.

The setting also complicates the encounter because it forces two images of a person together.

First love is rarely remembered neutrally.

Even when the relationship is long gone, "first" gives it a protected status.

First love often belongs to an age before we understood relationships well.

That can make the memory purer than reality ever was.

Not necessarily happier.

Purer.

The person becomes attached to discovery itself.

The first time affection felt different.

The first time jealousy hurt.

The first time somebody's attention altered the entire day.

The first time a separation felt impossible.

Later relationships may be deeper, healthier, more adult, more important.

But first experiences occupy a different category because there was no previous template.

You did not know what would happen next.

That ignorance gives memory unusual brightness.

Years later, the actual person and the remembered person can be almost two different people.

That is why unexpected reunions can hurt.

Reality is asked to meet memory.

Memory has had years to edit.

Reality has been living without asking permission.

The person in front of you is not obliged to resemble the person you carried.

Neither are you.

The old entry says that listening to her life story made the encounter harder.

That detail matters more than the location.

Had I only seen her across the room, perhaps the memory would have remained shocking but shallow.

Recognition.

Disbelief.

Then departure.

Conversation changes that.

A life fills the distance.

Years that had been blank suddenly contain events.

Choices.

Difficulties.

Turns you did not witness.

You understand that while someone existed as a frozen memory inside you, they continued living in full detail elsewhere.

This is one of the strangest things about people from our past.

In memory, they stop moving.

In reality, they do not.

We preserve a person at twenty.

They become twenty-five.

Thirty.

Forty.

They go places.

Meet people.

Lose people.

Work.

Fail.

Recover.

Change.

None of this appears in our internal version until, sometimes, we meet again.

Then the frozen image is forced to absorb elapsed time all at once.

The old entry says my heart broke more as I listened.

I should be careful with that phrase.

The archive does not tell me exactly what happened to her.

It does not tell me what parts of her story affected me.

It would be wrong to turn the absence of detail into a dramatic biography.

What is supported is the response.

I found the story painful.

That pain grew as I thought about it.

And I believed the encounter would be difficult to forget.

This is important because compassion toward someone from the past can be emotionally confusing.

You may no longer be in love with them in any present-tense sense.

You may not want a relationship.

You may barely know the adult they have become.

Still, hearing that life has been difficult for them can hurt with surprising force.

Why?

Because old affection does not always vanish cleanly.

Sometimes it changes function.

It stops asking for anything.

It becomes a small permanent wish:

I hope you are all right.

You may not think this every day.

You may go years without thinking of the person at all.

Then you see them struggling and discover the wish had remained.

That is different from wanting them back.

The distinction matters.

Nostalgia often tries to confuse care with desire.

You remember how important someone once was and assume the importance must still be romantic.

Not necessarily.

Human attachment leaves residue.

Someone can stop being part of your life without becoming emotionally meaningless.

This is especially true of first love.

They occupy part of the autobiography even after leaving the current story.

Their well-being can still touch something old.

That does not mean the old story should restart.

It means it existed.

One of the difficulties of reunion is knowing what exactly you are feeling.

Love?

Pity?

Shock?

Nostalgia?

Protectiveness?

Regret?

Grief for the person they used to be?

Grief for the people both of you used to be?

All of these can arrive together.

The mind wants one label.

The body often provides a mixture.

That may be why the 2009 entry ends so dramatically.

The more I thought about it, I wrote, the larger it became.

I said it would be impossible to forget for the rest of my life.

I even wrote that life lost its meaning.

That is the language of immediate emotional impact.

I would not repeat the last claim literally today.

Life did not permanently lose meaning because of one encounter.

The sentence records scale, not philosophy.

At that moment, the event felt too large to fit comfortably inside an ordinary night.

Young writing often speaks this way.

Everything is forever.

Nothing will ever be the same.

I will never forget.

Life has no meaning.

Later, we learn that even enormous emotions become integrated.

They do not always disappear.

They become less physically dominant.

The event finds a shelf.

You can take it down without the whole room collapsing.

That is one of time's quieter mercies.

But it would be dishonest to rewrite the old entry as calm reflection.

I was not calm.

Something had hit me.

The exaggeration proves it.

There is another dimension to the setting that deserves care.

A strip club carries assumptions.

People may look at a dancer and instantly imagine a biography.

That is unfair.

Work does not tell us the whole person.

Neither does one room.

Neither does one night.

The old entry itself suggests this because the emotional turning point was not seeing her dance.

It was hearing her story.

The human being replaced the category.

That matters.

From a distance, people become roles.

Dancer.

Engineer.

Waiter.

Soldier.

Professor.

Driver.

Customer.

Once they speak, roles become inadequate.

The person acquires history.

This is especially powerful when you already knew an earlier version of them.

You cannot reduce someone to the job in front of you because memory keeps supplying contradiction.

You knew a different context.

A different age.

A different possibility.

That can make the present harder to accept, but it can also make judgment more difficult.

And that is good.

Judgment should become difficult when a human being becomes specific.

It is easy to have opinions about categories.

Specific lives ruin clean opinions.

I think this may be one of the lasting effects of encounters like this.

They interfere with abstraction.

Afterward, you know that any person you see inside a role has a before.

A childhood.

A family.

A first love.

Somebody who remembers them from another world.

We all do.

That realization is simple and easy to forget.

Travel sometimes restores it because unfamiliar settings make us observe people more closely.

Then, in this case, travel went further.

It placed someone familiar inside the unfamiliar.

The contrast became impossible to ignore.

The source around this entry places the night within a broader Montreal nightlife period.

I had written about Club Chez Paree on Saint Catherine Street a few days earlier.

I described prices, the crowd, the commercial mechanics of the place, and the slightly foolish confidence of being a visitor exploring somewhere new.

That earlier tone is almost entirely playful.

Then the "first love" entry appears.

The atmosphere changes instantly.

Same city.

Same general nightlife world.

Completely different emotional register.

This is how travel actually works.

We like to summarize trips.

Montreal was great.

The food was good.

We visited this place.

We went there.

But a trip can contain unrelated emotional climates.

A funny afternoon.

A boring meeting.

A lonely hotel room.

A surprising conversation.

One evening can rewrite the emotional memory of the whole journey.

You return home carrying one scene more heavily than all the sightseeing.

The archive suggests that this encounter was one of those scenes.

I had only a week in the city.

A week should be small.

Yet one evening inside that week stretched far beyond it.

This is another way time behaves badly.

Duration and importance are not correlated.

You can know someone for ten years and remember only fragments.

You can speak to someone for an hour and remember the room decades later.

You can live in a city for years and never write about it.

Spend seven days somewhere else and carry one night forever.

Memory has no respect for proportionality.

It chooses.

The phrase "first love" itself also deserves examination.

When we call someone that, we are doing more than ordering relationships chronologically.

We are giving them symbolic ownership of a beginning.

That can become unfair to both sides.

The person is asked to represent an entire emotional era.

Who they really are may become secondary.

This is why reunions can feel almost violent.

The symbolic person and the actual person occupy the same face.

One belongs to your biography.

The other belongs to themselves.

Maturity means allowing the second to win.

You can preserve what someone meant to you without demanding that they remain that person.

This is difficult because memory is possessive.

It says:

You are the person from my story.

Reality says:

I have my own story.

The conversation in Montreal seems to have forced that reality on me.

Her life had continued without my witnessing it.

Whatever I had remembered was incomplete.

That incompleteness hurt.

But it was also truth.

Perhaps all reunions with old loves contain some version of this.

Even happy ones.

You see someone.

The old face flashes first.

Then the present arrives.

There may be grey hair.

Different clothes.

A different voice.

Different confidence.

A different sadness.

You are reminded that time happened equally to both of you, even if memory had preserved only one side.

And then the other person is looking at you.

You are also their changed person.

This is easy to forget.

We experience our own aging continuously, so we seem normal to ourselves.

To someone who has not seen us in years, we may be the shock.

They also have a preserved version.

Reunion is reciprocal disorientation.

The old entry focuses entirely on my response.

That is natural.

It was my archive.

But today I would leave more space for the unknown on her side.

What did she think when she saw me?

Did she recognize me immediately?

Was it uncomfortable?

Ordinary?

Funny?

Painful?

The source does not say.

So the only honest answer is:

I do not know.

This is an important discipline in autobiographical writing.

Our memories are first-person documents.

They do not give us access to other minds.

The temptation is to complete the scene.

She must have felt this.

She probably thought that.

No.

Perhaps.

Maybe.

But unless the archive records it, certainty would be fiction.

Leaving another person's inner life unknown is not a weakness.

It is respect.

There is enough story on my side.

The shock.

The conversation.

The emotional aftermath.

The inability to stop thinking about it.

That is what belongs to me.

There is also a larger question hiding here:

What do we owe people we once loved?

Sometimes nothing.

Sometimes kindness.

Sometimes distance.

Sometimes a conversation.

Sometimes only the refusal to turn them into a story that serves us.

That last one matters especially when revisiting old writing.

The younger version of me wrote immediately from emotion.

The older version has a responsibility not to exploit the missing details.

A dramatic life story could make a dramatic essay.

But it would not be mine to invent.

So the encounter remains partly obscured.

I think that makes it stronger.

A first love.

Montreal.

A strip club.

A week-long visit.

A life story that hurt to hear.

No invented explanation.

The blanks stay blank.

Real memory is full of blanks.

Good writing does not always fill them.

Sometimes it frames them.

There is an old habit in romantic storytelling of treating chance encounters as destiny.

If two people meet again in an unlikely place, fiction usually wants something to happen.

They reconnect.

They realize the love never died.

They rescue each other.

They begin again.

Life is less obedient.

A coincidence can remain only a coincidence.

Its importance does not require a sequel.

The meeting itself can be enough.

This is perhaps the mature reading I would add to the 2009 entry.

At the time, the encounter felt as though it had to mean something because it hurt so much.

Now I think pain does not automatically imply instruction.

Some moments are significant simply because they reopen a sealed room.

You look inside.

You remember.

You feel more than expected.

Then you close the door again.

Not because the room was unimportant.

Because it belongs to the house you already lived through.

That is different from denial.

It is integration.

The past does not need to become the present to remain real.

First love is especially vulnerable to this confusion.

We sometimes honor it by wanting to recreate it.

But recreation is impossible.

Even if the two people reunite, they are not returning.

They are beginning something new with old knowledge.

The original thing belongs to its time.

That is why preserving it accurately matters.

Not better than it was.

Not more tragic.

Not more romantic.

Just true enough.

The source says meeting her put me in a difficult emotional position.

Her story affected me deeply.

The more I thought about it, the larger the feeling became.

I believed I would never forget it.

That is enough truth.

And in one sense, the old entry was correct.

The encounter did survive.

Not necessarily every detail.

Not the exact conversation.

Not the sequence of the night.

But the collision itself.

That is what memory kept.

Of all places, Montreal.

Of all places in Montreal, there.

Of all people, someone who belonged to the beginning of my emotional life.

Sometimes chance is not a message.

It is simply a very good archivist.

It places two distant parts of your life in the same room and leaves you to understand that they were always part of the same story.
