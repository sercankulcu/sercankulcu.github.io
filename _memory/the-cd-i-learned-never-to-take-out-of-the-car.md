date: 2011-10-07

For about a month in 2011, I accidentally ran a small social experiment from the driver's seat of my car.

The equipment was simple.

One car.

One Emre Aydın CD.

A changing set of passengers.

No survey forms.

No control group.

No statistical software.

Just a question:

“Do you want me to turn on the radio?”

The response kept arriving at remarkable speed.

“No, no, no. This is my favorite song.”

It did not seem to matter who had got into the car.

Someone I knew.

A friend.

An acquaintance.

Even a hitchhiker.

The reaction was similar often enough that I finally wrote it down.

My conclusion was practical:

I did not know about Emre Aydın himself, but it was probably a good idea not to take his CD out of the car.

That is the whole source memory.

What I like now is how perfectly it belongs to a period when the music inside a car still had a physical center.

A CD could live there.

Not merely be played there.

Live there.

You inserted it and then forgot about it.

It remained in the player for days.

Weeks.

Sometimes months.

The first track became the soundtrack of starting the engine.

Other tracks belonged to particular roads because the sequence kept meeting the same commute at approximately the same point.

You learned the order without trying.

The car and the album slowly became associated with each other.

There were no personalized recommendations deciding what came next.

No algorithm quietly replacing one song with another.

No infinite library unless you physically carried one.

There was the CD.

The radio.

Maybe several more CDs somewhere in the car.

That was enough.

Physical scarcity gave music repetition.

And repetition gave music memory.

Today almost any song can be summoned instantly.

That is an extraordinary luxury.

But abundance changes listening.

When everything is available, very little has to stay.

A CD in a car had to stay unless someone made the deliberate decision to remove it.

That small inconvenience mattered.

It allowed an album to outlast the mood in which it was first chosen.

On Monday you might actively want to hear it.

By Thursday it was simply what was there.

By the following week, the songs had become part of the car.

Then somebody else got in.

That is where the social experiment began.

Music inside a car is never entirely private once there is a passenger.

The driver controls the machine, but the cabin becomes shared territory.

Temperature.

Windows.

Conversation.

Smoking, in that period.

Route.

And music.

You can ignore another person's taste in your own room.

In a car, the speakers are communal.

That creates a little ritual of negotiation.

Do you like this?

Should I change it?

Want the radio?

Put something else on?

The question is partly courtesy.

It says:

You are now inside my small moving room. I am willing to adjust it.

What surprised me was how often adjustment was unnecessary.

The CD had already solved the problem.

The passenger recognized the song.

Liked it.

Wanted it to continue.

The answer came before I could reach for the radio.

That makes music one of the fastest ways strangers can discover common ground.

Conversation sometimes requires effort.

Where are you from?

What do you do?

How do you know so-and-so?

Music can skip all that.

A song begins.

Recognition appears.

“Oh, I love this.”

Now two people who may have had almost nothing to say thirty seconds earlier possess a shared object.

The song does not belong to either person.

That is what makes it useful.

Neither has to reveal much.

You can agree on music before agreeing on almost anything else.

This is especially noticeable with hitchhikers.

The source says that even people I had picked up while hitchhiking sometimes reacted the same way.

I should not invent where I picked them up, where they were going, who they were, or what happened during those rides.

None of that survives.

But the social geometry is clear.

A stranger enters the car.

The driver and passenger do not yet have shared history.

Then the passenger recognizes the music.

For a moment, the song does some of the social work.

It reduces distance.

Not dramatically.

Nobody becomes a lifelong friend because of one chorus.

But the cabin becomes less anonymous.

That is enough.

Music often performs exactly this small task.

It makes silence less awkward.

It gives conversation somewhere to begin.

It lets two people experience the same few minutes without requiring them to manufacture intimacy.

And sometimes it does even more:

it reveals that a song you thought belonged to one kind of person actually belongs to everyone.

That was the funny part of my month-long observation.

I apparently expected more variation.

Surely somebody would dislike the CD.

Surely someone would prefer the radio.

Surely taste would divide the passengers.

Instead, the same fast response kept arriving.

No, leave it.

Favorite song.

After enough repetitions, the CD stopped being merely music and became a reliable social instrument.

There is a temptation to overstate what this says about Emre Aydın's popularity in 2011.

The archive is not a proper survey.

The sample was whoever happened to get into my car.

The wording “there is no girl who dislikes this guy,” from the original entry, was obviously comic exaggeration based on my own encounters, not demographic research.

That distinction matters.

Personal archives capture perception, not population statistics.

What I can say safely is simpler:

For about a month, among the women who happened to ride in my car, I repeatedly saw very enthusiastic reactions to the Emre Aydın CD.

Repeated enough to become a joke.

Repeated enough to become advice:

keep the CD nearby.

That phrasing also exposes the younger voice.

There is a little bit of tactical thinking in it.

If this music works so reliably with passengers, why remove it?

The car stereo becomes social equipment.

Not sophisticated seduction.

Nothing that grand.

More like having tea in the cupboard that everyone seems to like.

Useful.

Safe.

Proven.

This makes me smile because people often build tiny theories from daily repetition.

A certain café always seems crowded, therefore it must be good.

A certain route was faster three times, therefore it is the best route forever.

Every passenger likes this CD, therefore the CD has universal appeal.

Humans are informal statisticians with dangerously small datasets.

But daily life does not need peer review.

If something works five times, we use it again.

The car CD had become one of those things.

There is another piece of 2011 hidden in the source: the offer to switch to the radio.

Radio was still a real alternative.

Not background infrastructure.

A separate mode of listening.

CD meant:

I chose this.

Radio meant:

let us see what is happening out there.

One was controlled repetition.

The other was shared broadcast time.

The passenger could choose between your private selection and whatever station you found.

Today the equivalent choice might be between a playlist, streaming service, podcast, Bluetooth connection, or letting the passenger take control from a phone.

The social question remains.

What should we listen to?

The interface changes.

The negotiation survives.

Cars are excellent containers for musical memory because listening there is attached to movement.

A song in a room can blend into the room.

A song in a car collects geography.

Traffic lights.

Roads.

Weather.

Night drives.

People getting in and out.

Even when exact locations disappear, the body remembers movement.

This is probably why old songs can create a sudden illusion of travel.

You hear the opening seconds and feel as if you should be holding a steering wheel.

The archive does not tell me which Emre Aydın song was playing during each reaction.

It includes quoted lyrics nearby, but reproducing them is unnecessary and would distract from the memory.

The important object is the CD as a whole.

A disc that stayed in the car long enough to become predictable.

Predictability is underrated in music.

We often celebrate discovery.

New artist.

New album.

New song.

But much of our deepest relationship with music comes from repetition.

The track you no longer consciously choose.

The album that has become furniture.

The song everyone in the car knows.

Music becomes meaningful partly by losing novelty.

At first you listen to it.

Later you inhabit it.

That may be why passengers responded so quickly.

Recognition is pleasurable.

The first notes arrive and the brain already knows the territory.

No effort.

You are home inside the song before the car has moved very far.

This effect is stronger when music is tied to a period of life.

The same song can later reopen a whole year.

Not because its lyrics describe the year accurately.

Because it was physically present while the year happened.

That is one reason personal soundtracks are so arbitrary.

A song becomes associated with a breakup even if it is not about breakups.

With a city even if the artist never saw the city.

With military service.

With university.

With a particular office.

With driving.

Meaning attaches through repetition, not only through intention.

The Emre Aydın CD in my car was doing that in real time.

I could not know which associations would survive.

I only noticed that everybody seemed to want it left alone.

There is also something pleasingly analog about carrying your social advantage as a disc.

The modern equivalent would be invisible.

A playlist.

A recommendation history.

A streaming queue.

The CD was an object.

You could remove it.

Scratch it.

Lose it.

Put it in another car.

Lend it.

Its presence had a location.

That matters because objects become anchors more easily than services.

A streaming subscription does not sit in your hand years later.

A CD can.

Its case may be cracked.

The booklet may be worn.

The surface may carry scratches.

Physical media age with us.

They become evidence.

I do not know whether that particular CD still exists.

The source does not say, and I should not pretend otherwise.

But in October 2011, it had a clear job.

Stay in the car.

Do not interfere with success.

That last part is probably the most faithful way to preserve the humor.

People often make life worse by changing something that is working perfectly well.

We become bored.

Want novelty.

Try to optimize.

The younger me had discovered the opposite principle.

This CD keeps receiving enthusiastic passenger approval.

Therefore:

Hands off.

No upgrade needed.

There is practical wisdom in that.

Not only for music.

If a simple thing reliably makes an environment better, perhaps it does not need improvement.

A familiar coffee mug.

A chair.

A route.

A restaurant.

A playlist.

A tradition among friends.

We are surrounded by pressure to refresh, replace, update, and discover.

Sometimes stability is the feature.

The CD had become part of the hospitality of the car.

Get in.

Sit down.

Music already handled.

I also enjoy the way the entry separates the artist from the artifact.

“I don't know about him personally, but don't leave the CD behind.”

That distinction is healthier than we often allow ourselves.

You do not need to become interested in every aspect of an artist to value the work.

The work can function independently.

A song does not ask whether you approve of the singer's personality before helping a quiet drive.

The CD's effectiveness in my car did not require biography.

It required sound and recognition.

That was enough.

Looking back, the memory is not really about Emre Aydın.

It is about a small social environment built around music.

A car.

A driver.

A passenger.

A polite offer to change what is playing.

A fast refusal.

Then continuing down the road with one less decision to make.

That is a very modest kind of harmony.

Maybe that is why it stayed in the archive.

Most journeys do not become stories.

Nothing happens.

You go somewhere.

Talk.

Arrive.

But repeated tiny moments can become a pattern.

One passenger says it.

Then another.

Then another.

Eventually the pattern itself becomes the event.

I watched it for about a month.

Then I wrote the conclusion.

Keep the CD in the car.

It is difficult to imagine a more 2011 piece of advice.
