The man assembling the computer asked me what password I wanted.

I had no idea.

At that age, I assumed that anyone who knew how to open a computer case, connect cables, and make Windows appear on the screen also knew the correct answer to every other computer question.

“You choose,” I told him. “Make it a good one.”

He thought for a moment and gave me five digits.

That was my password.

There was no discussion about security. No capital letters, symbols, recovery codes, password managers, or second devices. The password was short enough to remember immediately, and that seemed to be its main purpose.

The computer ran Windows 98. I remember the thick monitor, the startup screens, and the feeling that the machine contained more possibilities than I could understand. I could install a game, change something, break something, and sometimes learn enough to fix it again.

The password stood at the entrance to that world.

Later, websites began demanding stronger passwords. Five digits were no longer enough. A password needed letters, numbers, symbols, and rules that changed from one site to another.

Like many people, I did not immediately become wise. I simply began modifying familiar patterns.

But the first password remained different in memory. It belonged to a time when there were very few passwords to remember and when having one at all felt strangely important.

Today, passwords are mostly infrastructure. They protect email, banking, phones, work accounts, shopping, cloud storage, and services I barely remember joining. A password is something to manage rather than something to admire.

The first one was not like that.

It was part of the computer.

I can still imagine the machine starting, the screen appearing, and someone beside me giving me those five digits as if he were handing over a small key.

By modern standards, it was a terrible password.

As a beginning, it was perfect.
