Every hometown has things that outsiders know about better than the people who actually grew up there.

This becomes especially funny when those things are supposedly "famous."

In July 2010, I wrote a short entry about Konya and its sweets.

My memory was simple.

Years earlier, when I was younger, there had been one sweet that seemed genuinely famous:

Mevlana candy.

That was the one.

You knew it.

People knew it.

It belonged to the city.

Then, as I remembered it, another "famous" sweet appeared:

akide candy.

And I had to admit something inconvenient.

I liked it more.

Especially the hazelnut version.

Then, some years later, yet another supposedly famous sweet joined the list:

hurma candy.

At that point I became suspicious.

How many famous sweets can one city manufacture before fame itself becomes part of the production process?

My younger conclusion was that Konya had become very good at continuously developing its portfolio of famous sweets.

The city was improving itself in the strategic field of making people eat sugar.

That was funny enough.

But the real problem came when I was in Ankara.

People there knew these "famous Konya sweets."

Sometimes better than I did.

This put me in a difficult position.

I was from Konya.

Therefore, in their minds, I should know.

Not only know.

I should probably provide.

Apparently hometown identity includes an informal supply-chain obligation.

Someone hears you are from Konya and suddenly you become regional procurement.

"Bring that famous candy next time."

Which famous candy?

You are from there.

You should know.

This is how local identity becomes administrative work.

It is easy to claim pride in your hometown when the subject is architecture, history, or culture.

It becomes more complicated when your colleagues expect kilograms of confectionery.

At that point patriotism has a weight.

And a price per kilo.

That was one of my complaints in the old entry.

Nobody asking for famous sweets seemed interested in what those sweets cost when purchased by the kilogram.

From their perspective, the city produced them.

Therefore I had access.

Possibly for free.

This is a common misunderstanding about hometowns.

If you come from a place associated with a product, outsiders imagine some privileged relationship between you and the product.

People from tea-growing regions must have unlimited tea.

People from places famous for hazelnuts presumably own hazelnuts personally.

If your hometown is known for a dessert, you must have a family member who can produce industrial quantities.

Geography becomes inventory.

Of course, this is mostly affectionate.

People like attaching food to places.

Food makes geography easy to remember.

Say Gaziantep and someone thinks of baklava.

Say Kayseri and mantı appears.

Say Adana and kebab arrives immediately.

Say Konya and food associations begin too.

This is not bad.

Regional food is one of the friendliest forms of identity.

You can argue about politics forever.

Food allows a city to introduce itself without asking for ideological agreement.

Try this.

Eat.

That is a much easier conversation.

But food identity has an interesting problem.

Tradition is rarely as stable as we imagine.

Things we call traditional were invented at some point.

Some are ancient.

Some are surprisingly recent.

Some become "famous" because generations actually make them.

Others become famous because enough shops, signs, brochures, relatives, bus companies, tourism offices, and gift boxes repeat the claim.

Eventually repetition produces truth.

Maybe not historical truth.

Social truth.

If every shop says something is the famous local sweet, eventually visitors arrive expecting it.

Then locals produce more of it because visitors expect it.

Now it really is famous.

This loop is fascinating.

Fame creates demand.

Demand creates supply.

Supply makes the fame visible.

The visible fame creates more fame.

A city can, in a sense, manufacture tradition.

My 2010 entry used a jokingly suspicious tone toward this.

I imagined some capitalist mechanism continuously adding new famous sweets to Konya.

First one.

Then another.

Then another.

As if somebody in a meeting had said:

We need growth.

Mevlana candy has reached market saturation.

Launch a second heritage product.

Then a third.

The joke works because "traditional" and "new product launch" are supposed to belong to different worlds.

But in reality, they often meet.

A local product can be old and marketed aggressively.

A newer product can acquire tradition quickly.

A business can turn nostalgia into packaging.

None of this automatically makes the product fake.

Commercialization does not erase cultural meaning.

But it does change the way meaning spreads.

The hazelnut akide candy in my memory is a good example.

I may have mocked the expanding list of famous sweets.

I still admitted that I liked one of the newer additions more than the older Mevlana candy.

Taste had no loyalty to heritage rankings.

This is another useful reminder.

People often feel pressure to prefer the most authentic version of something.

The oldest.

The local original.

The culturally approved choice.

But the mouth is a poor nationalist.

It likes what it likes.

You can respect the historically important product and still prefer the newer one.

No tribunal is required.

Food should be one of the places where people are allowed to be uncomplicated.

Eat the thing.

Do you like it?

Good.

The older I get, the more suspicious I become of performances of taste.

Not taste itself.

The performance around it.

You must drink coffee this way.

You must eat this dish only there.

That restaurant is for tourists.

Real locals never order that.

This version is authentic.

That one is commercial.

Sometimes these distinctions contain useful knowledge.

Local people often do know better.

Sometimes they are simply another way to construct status.

I know the correct thing.

You do not.

My old Konya entry accidentally reverses this.

I was the local.

People in Ankara seemed to know the expanding catalog of famous Konya sweets better than I did.

This is embarrassing only if local identity requires expertise.

It does not.

Being from somewhere does not make you an encyclopedia of it.

You can live in a city for years and never visit its most famous museum.

You can grow up near a landmark tourists travel thousands of kilometers to see and barely notice it.

Familiarity changes attention.

Visitors ask:

What is special here?

Residents ask:

What do I need today?

The tourist collects symbols.

The local collects routines.

This explains why outsiders sometimes know the official identity of a city better than residents.

They studied it.

The local simply lived there.

A visitor may know the famous foods, historic buildings, and recommended restaurants.

A resident knows which road is annoying at 5:30.

Where the pharmacy is.

Which bakery is open early.

Which neighborhood changed.

Which shortcut stopped working.

These are different kinds of knowledge.

Neither is automatically superior.

One describes the city as representation.

The other describes the city as lived system.

My Ankara friends knew the representation:

Konya has these famous sweets.

I knew something more practical:

If you want me to bring kilos of them, somebody has to pay.

That is local realism.

There is a broader lesson here about identity.

We often expect people to perform where they come from.

If someone is from a city, region, or country, we ask them to explain it.

What do people there eat?

What do they think?

Is this true?

Do you know that person?

Have you visited that place?

The individual becomes a customer-service desk for geography.

Usually harmless.

Sometimes tiring.

No person contains an entire city.

I can be from Konya and not know every sweet marketed with its name.

A New Yorker does not know every restaurant.

A Parisian has not visited every museum.

Someone from Istanbul may never have taken the tourist route outsiders assume defines the city.

Home is not a travel guide.

That sentence feels obvious now.

When I was younger, I experienced the mismatch mostly as comedy.

How could people in Ankara know my city's famous candy better than me?

Worse, how could this somehow make me look either ignorant or stingy?

Ignorant if I did not know the sweet.

Stingy if I knew it but did not bring enough.

A no-win system.

The funniest social expectations are like this.

A simple fact about you creates obligations nobody formally negotiated.

You own a car?

You can drive us.

You know computers?

Fix mine.

You are a doctor?

Look at this thing on my arm.

You are from Konya?

Bring candy.

Every identity comes with unsolicited support tickets.

Computer engineers understand this especially well.

The moment people learn you work with computers, all electronic devices become your jurisdiction.

Printer.

Phone.

Wi-Fi.

Television.

Possibly microwave oven.

The logic is not technical.

It is categorical.

Electricity exists.

You are the computer person.

Proceed.

Hometown food requests use the same system.

The product is from your city.

You are from your city.

Therefore logistics have been solved.

There is almost something affectionate in being assigned this role.

People ask because they associate you with the place.

The request says:

You are our connection to there.

That can be nice.

It makes geography social.

A box of sweets traveling from one city to another carries more than sugar.

It carries the joke.

The visit.

The expectation.

Someone remembering where you are from.

Small gifts create networks between places.

This is why regional foods travel so much in Turkey.

People return from hometown visits carrying bags.

Boxes.

Jars.

Packages wrapped much more carefully than their market value seems to justify.

Someone's mother sent this.

An aunt made that.

This is from our town.

Try it.

Food becomes portable home.

It moves belonging around.

A person may live in Ankara for years and still arrive from Konya with something expected.

The physical distance between cities is reduced by repetition.

Going home.

Coming back.

Bringing something.

This routine belongs especially strongly to student and early working life.

When you live away from your hometown, return trips are not simply travel.

You cross between versions of yourself.

At home, you become the person your family knows.

Back in the city where you study or work, you become the independent version.

Objects cross with you.

Food is one of the most common.

You leave with a bag fuller than the one you arrived with.

Parents are suspicious of empty luggage.

There is always something to take.

Food.

A container.

Something someone asked for.

Something nobody asked for but your mother is certain you need.

The bag becomes a supply chain between family and adult life.

My old sweet complaint belongs somewhere in that atmosphere, even though the entry itself does not specify a particular journey or family handoff.

I should not invent one.

But the social expectation is recognizable.

People know you are from somewhere.

Products begin moving through you.

The phrase "famous local product" becomes a threat to luggage capacity.

There is another interesting part of the entry:

my memory of the timeline.

About ten years earlier, I wrote, there was basically one famous candy.

Then another appeared.

Then around five years earlier, another.

These were rough recollections, not a researched history.

That distinction matters.

The entry records how the product landscape felt to me, not an authoritative history of confectionery in Konya.

Personal archives should be read that way.

They are evidence of perception.

Not always fact.

This is especially important when revisiting old writing.

The temptation is to convert memory into certainty.

Don't.

If younger me said "about ten years ago," that means exactly that:

this was how I remembered the change in 2010.

The value is in the perception.

From inside the city, fame seemed to be multiplying.

That is the story.

Maybe market historians could trace exact origins.

Not necessary.

The subjective change is enough.

The city's edible identity felt like it was expanding faster than I could track it.

That happens to cities in many ways.

A neighborhood becomes famous after you leave.

A restaurant becomes an institution you barely remember.

A street changes its identity.

A local product becomes nationally known.

Then outsiders ask you about it because you are "from there."

You realize the place continued evolving without consulting your memory.

This is a subtle experience.

We often freeze hometowns at the age we knew them best.

The city keeps changing.

Our internal version does not.

Then the two meet.

You return.

There is a new road.

A building is gone.

A shop has moved.

Something everybody calls traditional did not exist in your version.

You say:

Since when is this famous?

Someone younger looks at you as if you have failed citizenship.

This is not only about Konya.

It is about how memory timestamps place.

There is no single hometown.

There is the city itself, continuously changing.

And there is the version carried by each person who left.

My Konya was partly frozen at earlier stages.

The sweet catalog had apparently continued receiving updates.

Ankara had the latest release.

I was running an older version.

That is probably the best way to describe the situation.

The people around me had read the release notes.

I had not.

This is another reason hometown conversations become funny after moving away.

People expect current local expertise.

You may have historical expertise instead.

You know what was there.

They know what is famous now.

Both talk about the same city.

Different versions.

Food exposes this gently.

Unlike politics or urban development, sweets are low stakes.

Nobody gets too angry.

At worst, someone does not receive the promised kilogram.

Still, the dynamic is the same.

Places evolve.

Identity lags.

Maybe that is why I like the old entry.

It looks like a complaint about candy.

It is actually a small document about distance from home.

Not emotional distance in a dramatic sense.

Informational distance.

The city keeps producing things without you.

Other people tell you what your city is known for.

You become both insider and outsider.

That is a common condition for anyone who moves away from where they grew up.

You have legitimacy without currency.

History without updates.

You can say:

I'm from there.

Then immediately:

Wait, what is that?

This is healthy.

It prevents hometown identity from becoming too self-serious.

No one gets to own a city completely.

Not even locals.

Cities are too large, too dynamic, too contradictory.

You know your path through them.

That is enough.

So I no longer find it strange that Ankara friends could know some famous Konya sweet better than I did.

They knew the product.

I knew the joke.

And I knew the price problem.

The older me would probably respond more generously now.

You want the famous candy?

Fine.

Tell me which one.

Apparently there are updates.
