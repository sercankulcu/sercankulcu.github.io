date: 2012-07-07

There are small acts of urban competence that nobody teaches.

You learn them by watching.

How to stand on an escalator without blocking everyone.

How to walk through a crowded station without constantly changing speed.

How to position yourself before the doors open.

How to get off a bus without beginning the process five seconds too late.

And, for a certain kind of unnecessarily ambitious commuter:

how to enter a metro car at the exact moment the doors are closing.

In July 2012, I wrote a short entry about being able to ride the metro without holding on.

I decided that this was probably not the hardest balancing act available.

The more difficult achievement was this:

run toward a metro as the doors are closing,

fly through the gap at the last possible second,

stop exactly where you land without crashing into another passenger,

and then survive the sideways glances until your stop.

That was the challenge.

The actual journey was secondary.

The first problem was physics.

The second was dignity.

Cities are full of these moments.

You are late enough to run, but not late enough to give up.

The train is still there.

The doors are open.

Technically.

The warning sound has begun.

Your brain performs an instant calculation.

Distance to train.

Closing speed of doors.

Your own running speed.

Number of people standing inside.

Probability that the next train will arrive soon enough for you to regret this decision.

No equation is written.

The answer appears immediately.

Run.

This is not careful engineering.

This is real-time systems work under questionable requirements.

Once the decision is made, commitment is total.

You cannot begin running toward a closing metro and then become philosophical halfway there.

The people inside are already watching.

The people on the platform are watching.

The doors are closing.

Your body has become a public demonstration.

You accelerate.

At that moment, reaching the train feels important.

Not because the next train is necessarily far away.

Often it is not.

But once you start running, missing the train becomes emotionally unacceptable.

You are no longer trying to save time.

You are defending the decision to run.

This is a common human error.

We begin an action for one reason.

Then the reason disappears.

But stopping would make the original action look foolish.

So we continue.

The cost is already paid.

The sprint has begun.

Now we must justify the sprint.

There is probably a behavioral-economics term hiding somewhere in this.

At the time, I was mainly concerned with not hitting anyone.

Getting through the doors is only phase one.

You arrive inside the carriage with momentum.

The passengers inside have not agreed to participate in your experiment.

Somebody is standing near the door.

Someone has a bag.

Someone is looking at a phone.

Someone has correctly assumed that the closing-door warning means no new human body will suddenly enter at speed.

Then you appear.

A successful arrival requires deceleration.

Fast.

Ideally without grabbing a stranger.

Without stepping on a foot.

Without colliding with a pole.

Without creating a chain reaction.

This is where the entry became funny to me.

Standing in a moving metro without holding on seems like a balance skill.

But stopping from a run inside a crowded carriage without touching anybody?

That is the advanced course.

If you manage it, there is a very brief private feeling of victory.

Made it.

Then you realize everyone saw you.

Urban public transport has a strange social rule.

People generally pretend not to observe each other.

We are very close physically.

Socially, we maintain distance.

Eyes go to phones.

Advertisements.

Windows.

The floor.

Anywhere that communicates:

I am not involved in your life.

Then someone does something noticeable.

Runs through closing doors.

Drops something.

Trips.

Argues loudly.

Misses a step.

Now the system changes.

People still pretend not to look.

They simply look indirectly.

That was the second part of my old entry:

surviving the stolen glances until getting off.

Nobody needs to say anything.

The looks are enough.

A person across the carriage briefly raises their eyes.

Another looks and then immediately returns to a phone.

Someone near the door has the expression of a person who had already predicted this would happen.

You become aware of your breathing.

Running makes the situation worse.

You are trying to look as if nothing unusual occurred while your lungs are presenting evidence to the contrary.

There is no elegant way to do this.

You stand there.

Maybe straighten your clothes.

Maybe look at the route map with exaggerated seriousness.

Yes.

Of course.

I always enter trains like that.

Completely normal.

The metro begins moving.

Now comes the original skill:

standing without holding on.

This is another tiny urban game.

The experienced passenger does not fight the movement.

You adjust before the train fully accelerates.

Feet apart.

Weight distributed.

A slight movement of the body.

The beginner reacts after the force arrives.

Too late.

Suddenly a harmless acceleration becomes a public balance test.

This is learned physically.

Nobody explains it.

Your body develops a model.

You begin anticipating starts and stops.

You learn where to stand.

You notice the driver.

The track.

The familiar curve before a station.

Public transport creates embodied knowledge.

People who use the same route for years carry invisible maps in their muscles.

They know where the stairs will be when the train stops.

Which carriage puts them closest to the exit.

Which door opens on which side.

Where the crowded transfers happen.

What time the platform becomes unbearable.

This knowledge is rarely written anywhere.

It is local optimization.

Cities reward repetition.

The tourist sees a network.

The commuter sees a sequence of tactical decisions.

Stand here.

Enter there.

Avoid that exit.

Use the other staircase.

The second carriage is better in the morning.

Do not transfer from that end.

Every repeated journey becomes slightly more efficient.

Most of these improvements save seconds.

Humans still care.

There is something funny and beautiful about that.

We will spend months learning how to save thirty seconds from a daily route.

Then lose twenty minutes scrolling on a phone.

Efficiency is not always rational.

Sometimes it is simply satisfying.

Catching the closing train belongs to that satisfaction.

The actual time saved might be small.

But the feeling is larger.

I beat the doors.

This is perhaps the urban equivalent of catching a falling object before it hits the floor.

No major consequence.

Still satisfying.

The danger is when the game becomes more important than common sense.

Closing metro doors are not an invitation to prove athletic ability.

Transit systems use warning signals for a reason.

The old entry was a joke about a familiar scene, not advice.

Missing a train is better than getting caught in a door or causing someone else to fall.

This is one of those places where older me would add a sentence that younger me did not bother adding:

The next train exists.

Usually.

That realization is more useful than it sounds.

When we are younger, many small delays feel unacceptable.

Miss the train.

Miss the bus.

Wait ten minutes.

Arrive later.

The impulse is:

No.

I can still make it.

There is energy in that.

Also unnecessary stress.

With age, one becomes slightly more willing to accept the next train.

Not always.

But more often.

There is a broader lesson in that transition.

Not every closing door needs to be defeated.

Some are simply closed.

Another opportunity comes.

This sounds suspiciously metaphorical for a story about public transport.

But cities produce metaphors whether we ask for them or not.

The door closes.

You run.

Sometimes you make it.

Sometimes you do not.

If you make it, you spend the next five minutes pretending you did not just sprint like an idiot.

If you miss it, you stand on the platform and discover that the world continues.

Both outcomes are survivable.

That is perhaps the part we forget in small moments of urgency.

The mind converts preference into necessity.

I want this train.

Therefore I need this train.

The difference between those sentences is enormous.

Much of everyday stress lives there.

The metro example is harmless.

Other versions are not.

I need this job.

I need this person to answer.

I need this plan to work.

I need this exact opportunity.

Sometimes the thing really is important.

Often we are standing on a platform with another train arriving later.

Knowing which situation you are in is a useful skill.

Still, I do not want to remove all the foolishness from the old scene.

There is something energetic in it.

The doors are closing.

You see the gap.

You run.

For a few seconds, the ordinary commute becomes an action sequence.

No soundtrack.

No hero.

Just a person trying not to be ten minutes later.

You cross the threshold.

Now the real challenge begins.

Stop.

Do not hit anyone.

Recover balance.

Pretend your heart rate is normal.

Avoid eye contact.

That final part is important.

Public embarrassment is amplified by eye contact.

If nobody looks directly at anyone, perhaps the incident did not officially happen.

This is one of the unwritten treaties of city life.

We give strangers the dignity of pretending not to notice.

Someone stumbles.

Look away.

Someone runs for the door.

Look briefly, then look away.

Someone is having an awkward phone conversation.

Become extremely interested in the route map.

This selective inattention allows millions of people to share small spaces without constantly becoming part of one another's lives.

It is a kind of courtesy.

Not warmth.

Not friendship.

Privacy created through mutual pretending.

Crowded cities require this.

You cannot emotionally process every stranger near you.

So we develop boundaries made from gaze.

The metro carriage is a temporary society.

Nobody chose the members.

Everyone has a destination.

The rules are simple.

Do not block the door.

Do not fall on people.

Do not play sound loudly.

Let passengers exit first.

Do not stare.

And if someone enters at the last second with more velocity than dignity,

give them a few discreet glances,

then allow them to pretend it never happened.

That is enough.

My 2012 entry lasted only a couple of sentences.

I was comparing two balance skills.

Standing in the metro without holding on.

And entering at the last instant without crashing into anyone.

The second seemed harder.

I still think it was.

But what I like now is everything around that tiny observation.

The rush.

The calculation.

The body learning the city.

The passengers looking without looking.

The strange importance of catching one particular train.

The effort to appear normal immediately after doing something visibly ridiculous.

These are the details that disappear first when we remember a period of life only through big events.

Jobs.

Relationships.

Graduations.

Moves.

Trips.

But daily life was mostly not big events.

It was this.

A platform.

Closing doors.

A short run.

A successful stop.

A few strangers pretending not to watch.

Then the train moved on.
