There are conversations that fail because the subject is difficult.

Politics.

Money.

Relationships.

Family.

Work.

Then there are conversations that fail before the subject even arrives.

In May 2010, my phone rang.

I answered.

“Hello?”

The caller asked:

“Who are you?”

This is already a poor beginning.

There is a basic rule of telephone civilization that most people understand without needing formal instruction:

If you call someone, you should have at least some idea who you are trying to reach.

Maybe you dialed the wrong number.

Fine.

That happens.

But then the burden of explanation is generally yours.

“Hello, is this Ahmet?”

“Sorry, I may have the wrong number.”

“May I speak to…”

Something.

Anything.

Instead, this caller opened with the confidence of a police interrogation.

Who are you?

So I answered with the only question that seemed reasonable:

“Who were you trying to call?”

The caller replied:

“Who are you?”

I tried again.

“Who were you trying to call?”

“Who are you?”

At this point we were no longer having a telephone conversation.

We had built a loop.

Two human beings connected through a telecommunications network, using all the technology required to carry voices across distance, and yet unable to advance beyond two sentences.

Who are you?

Who were you trying to call?

Who are you?

Who were you trying to call?

Somewhere, communication engineering was being wasted.

Eventually the caller answered my question.

“My father.”

That was enough for me.

I hung up.

There are moments when continuing a conversation becomes an act of charity, and I was apparently not feeling charitable.

The story should have ended there.

It did not.

The same voice called again.

I answered again.

“Hello?”

“Who are you?”

This time I hung up immediately.

Some problems do not improve with additional data.

I still like this little memory because it contains a complete model of bad communication.

Both parties possess information.

Neither is giving the information the other needs.

The caller presumably knows who his father is.

I know who I am.

Neither fact is useful until connected to the actual question:

Which number were you trying to reach?

That is the entire problem.

But everyday communication often fails in exactly this way.

People answer the question they want to answer.

Or ask for information without providing context.

Or assume the other person already knows what they mean.

Then irritation begins.

A surprising amount of human conflict is not disagreement.

It is missing protocol.

Telephone calls make this especially visible because the opening seconds require coordination.

One person initiates.

The other person receives.

The caller usually has more context.

The receiver has almost none.

That creates a simple responsibility:

The caller should explain.

You know why you called.

I do not.

This is why “Who are you?” sounds so absurd when spoken by the caller.

The question reverses the information flow.

It asks the person with less context to repair the situation created by the person with more context.

It is the communication equivalent of walking into someone’s house and asking:

“What are you doing here?”

Still, the story belongs to a different telephone era.

In 2010, caller identification existed, of course, but unknown numbers still produced more genuine uncertainty than they often do now.

There were fewer messaging layers around calls.

People called.

Sometimes from numbers you did not recognize.

Sometimes from someone else’s phone.

Sometimes numbers changed.

Sometimes you answered without knowing whether the next voice belonged to a friend, a business, a relative, a wrong number, or somebody trying to sell you something.

The telephone demanded immediate participation.

A message can wait.

A call arrives and says:

Now.

This is part of why strange calls become memorable.

They interrupt whatever reality existed before the ringtone.

For thirty seconds, a stranger can become the most important person in the room simply because they have occupied your ear.

Then the call ends and they disappear completely.

Wrong-number calls are especially strange because they create temporary relationships that should never have existed.

Two people meet because of one incorrect digit.

Most of these encounters last seconds.

“Sorry, wrong number.”

“No problem.”

Finished.

The network closes the accidental door.

But occasionally one person refuses to accept the normal script.

Then you get:

Who are you?

The phrase itself is interesting because outside the context of a phone call, it can be profound.

Who are you?

Philosophers can spend books on it.

People spend years trying to answer it.

Identity.

Selfhood.

Memory.

Social roles.

Consciousness.

At 8 p.m. on a wrong-number call, however, the philosophical answer is unnecessary.

You called me.

You tell me.

That is enough metaphysics for tonight.

The funniest part of the original exchange is probably “my father.”

I asked who he was trying to call.

He finally answered.

His father.

Technically, progress.

Practically, almost no progress at all.

There are millions of fathers.

The answer contains relational information without identifying information.

It is like asking:

“Which restaurant are you looking for?”

“The one where I eat.”

Excellent.

We are moving rapidly.

This kind of answer appears often when people speak entirely from their own point of view.

The caller knows exactly who “my father” means.

Therefore the phrase feels specific to him.

To me, it means almost nothing.

This is one of the deepest ordinary mistakes in communication:

Confusing what is obvious to me with what is available to you.

Teachers see this constantly.

You explain something using a term you have used for years.

To you, the term is transparent.

To the student, it is another unknown inside the explanation.

Engineers do it.

Doctors do it.

Managers do it.

Families do it.

Friends do it.

“We talked about that.”

Which that?

“The usual place.”

Usual to whom?

“He said it was ready.”

Who is he?

What is it?

Context is invisible to the person who already has it.

Good communication requires temporarily forgetting what you know and imagining what the other person does not.

That is harder than it sounds.

The caller could not do it.

Neither, after a point, was I interested in teaching him.

So I hung up.

This is an important part of the story too.

Not every failure of communication deserves repair.

We often talk about communication as though persistence is automatically virtuous.

Explain more.

Ask again.

Try to understand.

Be patient.

Usually good advice.

But conversations also have costs.

Time.

Attention.

Energy.

If somebody calls twice and begins both calls by demanding to know who you are, ending the interaction may be the most efficient available form of communication.

Click.

Message delivered.

There is something satisfying about the physical finality of old phone etiquette.

Hanging up used to be a stronger gesture.

Modern text conversations can drag indefinitely.

No formal end.

One person stops replying.

Then maybe replies two hours later.

Then the conversation resumes tomorrow.

Digital communication often dissolves boundaries.

Telephone calls had a clear binary state.

Connected.

Disconnected.

And hanging up could function as punctuation.

Full stop.

In this case, twice.

The second call is what turns the incident from irritation into comedy.

If somebody behaves strangely once, maybe confusion.

Maybe stress.

Maybe a misunderstanding.

If they immediately call again and repeat the exact same opening, the behavior becomes character.

He had apparently learned nothing from the first attempt.

Perhaps from his perspective, I was the difficult one.

That possibility makes the whole thing better.

He may have put down the phone thinking:

What a rude person. I only asked who he was.

Then called again to solve the mystery.

And met the same resistance.

This is one of the pleasures of small conflicts with strangers:

Both people can leave completely convinced that the other person was the idiot.

There is no referee.

No final judgment.

The universe simply separates them.

Personal archives are useful because they preserve which side of the absurdity we occupied.

Naturally, the archive is biased.

I wrote the entry.

I get to sound reasonable.

The caller does not have an entry beside mine explaining:

I was trying to call my father and some lunatic kept interrogating me.

Maybe somewhere he told the story exactly that way.

That is possible.

Memory is rarely a neutral camera.

Still, the dialogue itself is enough.

You can read it and decide.

I remain comfortable with my position.

If you place the call, opening with “Who are you?” is not strong telephone work.

There is also a broader difference between communication and information exchange.

Technically, our conversation contained words.

Questions.

Answers.

Turns.

The line worked.

The voices arrived.

So the communication infrastructure succeeded.

The human communication failed.

This distinction matters.

Technology can transmit perfectly and still connect two people who do not understand each other.

Higher bandwidth does not fix bad questions.

Better audio quality does not provide context.

Faster networks cannot force relevance.

We often imagine communication problems as technical because technical problems are easier to diagnose.

Can you hear me?

Yes.

Then everything important becomes human.

Did you understand me?

Did I tell you enough?

Did I ask the right question?

Did I listen to the answer?

The 2010 call passed the first test.

We could hear each other perfectly.

Unfortunately.

It failed the rest.

I also like how little the story needs.

No names.

No location.

No backstory.

No explanation of what I was doing before the call.

The scene is almost entirely dialogue.

That makes it feel like a tiny stage play.

Two voices.

One telephone.

No progress.

Sometimes preserving a memory means resisting the temptation to decorate it.

I do not know who the caller was.

I do not know whether he eventually found his father.

I do not know which number he intended to dial.

The archive does not say.

Those missing details are not weaknesses.

They are the shape of the event.

He arrived as a voice and left as a voice.

That is exactly how wrong-number calls work.

Anonymous entrance.

Anonymous exit.

The only thing he left behind was a question.

Who are you?

Years later, the answer is still:

The person you called.

That should have been enough.
