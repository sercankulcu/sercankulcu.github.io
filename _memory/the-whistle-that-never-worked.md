date: 2012-01-18

In January 2012, I wrote about one of the strangest objects I had kept as a souvenir.

A whistle.

It had been distributed to everyone during military service.

I had never actually used it there.

Not once.

Later, long after the period it belonged to had ended, I found it again.

I blew into it.

Nothing.

The whistle did not work.

My immediate thought was ridiculous:

I should have tested this when they gave it to me.

Maybe I could have returned it as defective.

Then I put it away again.

I could not bring myself to throw it out.

That is the whole original entry.

A broken whistle.

No important monetary value.

No craftsmanship.

No family history.

No practical use.

A tool that had failed at the one job it was made to do.

And I kept it.

There is probably no better definition of a souvenir.

A useful object has to justify the space it occupies.

A souvenir is exempt.

Its job is not to work.

Its job is to remember.

That changes the normal rules of ownership.

If I bought a whistle because I needed a whistle and discovered that it produced no sound, the decision would be easy.

Garbage.

Replacement.

Refund.

End.

But once an object becomes attached to a period of life, usefulness becomes almost irrelevant.

The broken whistle had already succeeded at a second function.

It carried time.

I could pick it up and know where it came from.

That was enough.

We keep many objects for this reason while pretending there must be another.

An old ticket.

A key that opens nothing we still own.

A restaurant receipt.

A student card.

A badge.

A hotel card.

A small stone from somewhere visited once.

A dead phone nobody will ever turn on again.

The practical mind asks:

Why do you still have this?

The answer often sounds weak when spoken aloud.

Because.

Because it was there.

Because I was there.

Because if I throw it away, something feels more finished than I want it to feel.

Objects are good at carrying endings.

This is why throwing them away can feel disproportionately serious.

You are not throwing away the event.

You know that.

Memory does not physically live inside plastic.

But the hand is not always convinced by philosophy.

It holds the object and feels continuity.

Then the bin appears.

Suddenly disposal looks like a declaration.

This is over.

Of course it was already over.

Military service had ended before I found the whistle again.

The object did not keep the period alive in any literal sense.

Still, keeping it allowed one physical survivor from that world to remain.

That can matter.

We underestimate how much memory depends on contact.

A photograph gives sight.

A recording gives sound.

An object gives weight.

Temperature.

Texture.

Scale.

You hold the thing and the past occupies the hand.

That experience is different from remembering intellectually.

You may know you once lived somewhere.

Then you find an old key from the apartment and suddenly the door returns.

Not visually in perfect detail.

But bodily.

The metal belonged to a routine your hand once knew.

The whistle worked the same way even though, amusingly, it did not work as a whistle.

Its failure may actually have improved the souvenir.

A functioning whistle would be merely old equipment.

A whistle that survived an entire military service without being used and later turned out not to work has a story.

Objects become memorable through imperfection.

A scratch.

A missing button.

A crack.

A strange repair.

The flaw differentiates this object from every identical one that once came from the same box.

Military-issued objects are especially anonymous at first.

Everyone receives the same thing.

Same function.

Same general appearance.

The system does not care which individual object becomes yours.

Then time changes that.

This one was in my pocket.

This one stayed in my bag.

This one came home.

This one ended up in a drawer.

Mass-produced object becomes personal artifact.

Not because someone engraved a name on it.

Because biography touched it.

There is something funny about military equipment becoming sentimental.

Military service is structured around function.

Uniforms have functions.

Commands have functions.

Schedules.

Equipment.

Everything is meant to belong to a practical order.

Then someone leaves, takes a useless whistle home, and turns it into nostalgia.

The institution says:

equipment.

The former soldier says:

memory.

Institutions cannot control what their objects become later.

A standard item can become evidence of youth.

A joke.

A story for children.

A thing found during cleaning that delays the entire cleaning process for twenty minutes.

Because that is what old objects do.

You open a drawer with a clear goal.

Throw away unnecessary things.

Excellent.

Very adult.

Very efficient.

Then you find something from ten years earlier.

Efficiency ends.

You sit down.

Where did this come from?

Oh.

Right.

Memory has defeated housekeeping again.

The old entry does not say where I found the whistle.

Only that I saw it again and tried it.

That gap should remain.

The point is not the drawer or box.

The point is rediscovery.

Rediscovery is one of the pleasures of forgetting.

If you remember every object you own, finding it produces nothing.

But forgotten objects re-enter life almost like visitors.

You recognize them one second before you remember the story.

First:

What is this?

Then:

Ah.

That "ah" contains the return.

The whistle must have produced exactly that.

Military service.

A whole period condensed into a tiny object.

Then curiosity.

Does this thing actually work?

Blow.

Nothing.

And suddenly another absurd thought:

So it may have been defective the entire time.

This is wonderful because the defect had no consequences.

I had never needed to use it.

The whistle could have been broken from the day it was issued.

Nobody knew.

Not even me.

A tool can fail silently for years if nobody asks it to perform.

There is a small technical lesson there.

Untested systems are only assumed to work.

But I will resist turning the whistle into an engineering lecture.

The funnier truth is enough.

The military gave me a whistle.

I completed military service.

I came home.

Later I discovered the whistle may never have been capable of whistling.

Excellent.

This is the sort of administrative absurdity that feels completely plausible in retrospect.

Not scandalous.

Not important.

Just slightly perfect.

The imagined product return makes the story even better.

I wrote that I should have blown it when they first handed it to me, so perhaps I could have returned it.

I enjoy the image of trying to perform consumer rights inside military bureaucracy.

Excuse me.

This whistle appears defective.

Do you have another one?

Can I see the manager?

It is obviously a joke.

The object was issued, not purchased in a shop.

The idea of treating it like a retail transaction is what creates the humor.

But the joke also reveals how ownership changes once an issued object comes home.

During service, it belongs to the system.

Afterward, sitting in your house, it becomes just another thing.

You can evaluate it.

Mock it.

Keep it.

Throw it away.

The authority around it disappears.

That transformation happens to many objects associated with structured periods.

A school uniform after graduation.

An employee badge after resignation.

A conference lanyard after the event.

A hospital wristband after recovery.

While the period is active, the object has institutional meaning.

Afterward, it becomes personal.

Sometimes its emotional importance increases exactly when its official importance becomes zero.

The whistle was completely unnecessary in civilian life.

That made it pure memory.

Useful objects remain in circulation and slowly detach from their origins.

A mug from an old office may simply become a mug.

A jacket from university may continue being worn until it is only clothing.

But a useless item cannot hide behind continued function.

Every time you see it, the question is:

Why is this still here?

And every time, the answer is the past.

That is why strange souvenirs can be more powerful than useful ones.

They have no excuse except memory.

I think this also explains why throwing them out becomes difficult.

If an object is useful, you can say:

I need it.

If it is beautiful:

I like looking at it.

If it is valuable:

It may be worth something.

A broken military whistle offers none of these defenses.

Keeping it requires admitting sentimentality.

That can be uncomfortable for people who like to imagine themselves practical.

I kept it anyway.

Good.

Practicality should not win every argument.

A home containing only useful objects would be efficient and strangely empty.

Human beings decorate space with surplus meaning.

Photographs.

Books already read.

Gifts never used.

Objects inherited from people who are gone.

Cheap souvenirs from expensive trips.

The value is not in function.

It is in connection.

Modern minimalism sometimes treats unused objects as moral failures.

If you have not used it in a year, throw it away.

Maybe.

That can be helpful.

Clutter can become exhausting.

But no rule can distinguish perfectly between clutter and artifact.

The difference is private.

One broken whistle may be meaningless plastic to everyone else and perfectly legitimate memory to the owner.

The important question is not:

Does this object still work?

It is:

What happens inside me when I hold it?

Sometimes the answer is nothing.

Then discard it.

Sometimes an entire year comes back.

Then maybe the drawer can spare a few centimeters.

We are not museums.

But we are also not warehouses optimized for turnover.

A life needs a little inefficient storage.

The digital world has made this question stranger.

We can now preserve enormous amounts of memory without physical space.

Thousands of photographs.

Messages.

Emails.

Documents.

Videos.

Cloud archives.

Yet physical objects still behave differently.

A photograph of the whistle would preserve its appearance.

It would not preserve the act of finding it unexpectedly while looking for something else.

Digital archives are searchable.

Physical archives ambush you.

That is one of their charms.

You cannot keyword-search a drawer.

You open it.

Things appear in the wrong order.

Memory benefits from this disorder.

A file named military_whistle.jpg gives exactly what you requested.

A box containing a whistle under three unrelated objects gives surprise.

Surprise creates emotion.

This may be one reason people keep physical souvenirs even when photographs exist.

Objects are not only records.

They are interruptions.

They pull the past into an ordinary afternoon without being asked.

The whistle did that in 2012.

I had been out of military service long enough for the object to become strange again.

Then I tested it.

Its silence was funny.

But perhaps silence was also appropriate.

A whistle exists to demand attention.

To create a sharp sound.

To signal.

To call.

Years later, the souvenir did the opposite.

It made no sound.

And yet it still got my attention.

That is a nice accidental metaphor, though the original entry certainly did not present it this way.

The whistle no longer needed to whistle.

Its presence was enough.

Old objects become quieter as their meanings deepen.

They do not explain themselves.

You have to know.

Someone else could find the same whistle after me and see junk.

No story.

That dependence on memory makes personal artifacts fragile.

When the person who knows the story is gone, the object often loses value instantly.

Families discover boxes after deaths and face impossible decisions.

What is important here?

Why was this kept?

Is this random?

Was there a reason?

Without the story, every souvenir risks becoming trash.

This is where writing helps.

The 2012 entry gives the whistle a label.

Not physically.

Narratively.

Now the object has provenance.

Military service.

Issued to everyone.

Never used.

Discovered later.

Did not work.

Could not throw it away.

That is enough to rescue meaning even if the object itself eventually disappears.

Perhaps that is the ideal relationship between objects and writing.

The object triggers the memory.

The writing preserves the memory.

Then someday the object may no longer need to carry the entire burden.

I do not know whether I still have the whistle.

The source does not tell me what happened after January 2012.

I will not invent a current status.

Maybe it remained in a drawer for years.

Maybe it was eventually thrown away.

Maybe it still exists somewhere.

The uncertainty is fine.

The story has already survived independently.

This reveals another interesting thing about souvenirs.

We fear that throwing away the object will destroy the memory.

Sometimes it does weaken access.

But memories can migrate.

From object to story.

From story to writing.

From writing to another person.

The physical trigger is only one stage.

Once I wrote about the whistle, it gained a second life.

The object could disappear and the absurdity would remain.

That is what archives do.

They convert private matter into durable language.

And perhaps language is the lighter souvenir.

No drawer required.

Still, I am glad I did not throw it away immediately.

Had I behaved efficiently, there might have been no entry.

No rediscovery.

No failed test.

No joke about returning it.

The useless object created useful memory.

That is a respectable career for a broken whistle.

There is also something revealing in the fact that I never used it during service.

Objects can become symbols of periods without participating in anything dramatic.

The whistle did not save anyone.

Did not signal an emergency.

Did not mark an important event.

It was simply issued and carried.

This matters because memory does not require action.

Proximity can be enough.

An object sits beside you during a period.

Years later, it absorbs meaning from adjacency.

The desk where you studied.

The lamp in the apartment.

The chair from the first office.

Nothing happened to them specifically.

They were present while life happened nearby.

We assign them some of the emotional residue.

This is why replacing old furniture can feel unexpectedly strange.

You are not attached to wood.

You are attached to the scenes that used the wood as background.

The whistle was background too.

A tiny standardized background object from military service.

Then civilian life turned it into foreground.

I think that is why collections of military souvenirs often look unimpressive to outsiders.

Badges.

Coins.

Patches.

Cheap equipment.

Paper.

Photographs.

Their value cannot be seen directly.

The value is relational.

This was mine.

I was there.

Again, that phrase.

I was there.

Perhaps every souvenir says it.

A shell from a beach.

I was there.

A boarding pass.

I was there.

A concert ticket.

I was there.

A broken whistle.

I was there.

And more specifically:

I was that person there.

This is the deeper function.

The souvenir does not only prove a location or event.

It connects the current self to an earlier self.

The person holding the whistle in 2012 is not exactly the person who received it during service.

Enough time has passed for one to observe the other.

The object forms a bridge.

That is why small artifacts become more powerful as we age.

At twenty, five years ago may feel close.

At forty, twenty years ago contains another adult.

The object says both people belong to one biography.

Continuity is not always obvious from inside.

We change jobs.

Homes.

Cities.

Habits.

Relationships.

Bodies.

Opinions.

Then some ridiculous piece of plastic survives several transformations and quietly insists:

Same life.

That is comforting.

It is also slightly absurd that plastic may outlast many of the situations we once considered permanent.

Objects have their own patience.

They wait in drawers while human priorities rotate.

Then one day they reappear.

What are you doing here?

Nothing.

Waiting for you to remember.

The broken whistle was very good at that.

Bad at whistling.

Excellent at waiting.

And perhaps that is why I kept it.

Not because it worked.

Because I did.
