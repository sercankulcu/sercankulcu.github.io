title: "A Thursday Night at Nil Bar"
date: 2010-04-16

Some places are remembered because they were beautiful.

Others survive because they were small, crowded, inconvenient, loud, slightly ridiculous, and somehow exactly right for that period of your life.

Nil Bar belonged to the second group.

I had not been there for a while.

On Thursdays, Şeytan Tüyü was playing.

That was enough reason to go back.

The place was already small, and by the time we arrived the downstairs area was completely full.

There was nowhere sensible to sit.

So we did what people do in places they actually want to stay:

we sat outside and waited.

There is something very specific about waiting outside a crowded bar for the inside to become available.

You are technically already there.

But not really.

The music exists below you.

People are moving in and out.

Every time the door opens, a small piece of the room escapes.

Sound.

Smoke.

Warm air.

Voices.

You are close enough to know the night has started and far enough away to feel excluded from it.

We stayed outside until around eleven.

Eventually the place emptied just enough.

We went downstairs.

And after roughly two years, I heard Şeytan Tüyü live again.

Two years is not a dramatic amount of time when you look at a calendar.

It can be enormous when you return to something familiar.

People change.

Bands change.

You change.

The first reaction is often unfair.

You compare the present not with the past itself, but with the version of the past you preserved.

That version never ages.

The real people do.

In my entry, I joked that the band had aged considerably in those two years.

Of course they had not suddenly become old men.

Two years had simply passed.

But when you remember a group from an earlier period of your life and then see them again, time becomes visible.

Hair.

Faces.

Posture.

The way someone holds an instrument.

The audience.

The room.

The songs may be the same.

Everything else tells you that the calendar has moved.

I noticed something else that night.

For the first time, I saw Serkan and Volkan actually playing guitar.

That sounds strange.

How could I have listened to the group before and not noticed?

Memory is selective.

Maybe I had always focused on something else.

Maybe the setup had changed.

Maybe I simply had never paid attention.

That is another strange thing about returning to familiar places.

You can notice something for the first time in a scene you think you already know.

Familiarity creates blindness.

We stop looking because we assume we have already seen.

Then one evening, the light is different.

Or we are.

Much of the singing that night was done by Cem from Pilli Bebek.

In my old entry, I wrote that his voice was clearly better.

There was no attempt at diplomacy.

The younger version of me was not especially interested in diplomatic criticism.

He heard a voice he liked more and wrote exactly that.

I still like that honesty.

Not because every judgment was fair.

Because old entries preserve reactions before they are polished into respectable opinions.

That is one reason they are useful years later.

You can see what caught your attention in real time.

Not what you later decided should have mattered.

What really mattered at Nil Bar was not technical quality anyway.

It was proximity.

The place was so small that the normal distance between audience and band almost disappeared.

You did not feel as though performers existed in another world.

They were simply there.

A few meters away.

The same room.

The same narrow corridors.

The same toilet queue.

That last part became the most memorable scene of the night.

I was waiting for the toilet.

Then the drummer appeared.

He said they were waiting for him on stage and asked if he could go ahead.

Fair enough.

There are not many convincing reasons for cutting a toilet line.

"The band is waiting for me on stage" is one of them.

So he went.

Then the bass player arrived.

Same problem.

They were waiting for him too.

At this point the situation had become absurd.

I told him there was already someone inside who was apparently also being waited for on stage.

He went in anyway.

This is the kind of thing that would be annoying almost anywhere else.

In a tiny live-music bar, it becomes part of the evening.

The musicians were not distant celebrities disappearing through a private backstage corridor.

Their backstage problem was your toilet queue.

That is what made the place feel different.

The boundary between performer and audience was almost gone.

You could feel as if you knew the band.

Not because you actually did.

Because the architecture forced everyone into the same human scale.

Small venues do this well.

Large venues create spectacle.

Small venues create familiarity.

At a stadium concert, the performer is an image.

Lights.

Screens.

Distance.

At a bar, the performer needs to get past you to reach the toilet.

That changes the mythology.

And maybe that is why certain small places become more important in memory than much larger ones.

They give you participation instead of observation.

The night does not happen in front of you.

It happens around you.

Ankara had many places like that in different periods.

Some lasted.

Some moved.

Some changed names.

Some disappeared.

Baraka was another one I remembered strongly.

Dark.

Crowded.

Loud.

Not exactly clean.

Not exactly safe-feeling in every memory either.

But alive.

Then it changed.

The old version disappeared.

A new address could carry the same name without carrying the same place.

This happens constantly in cities.

We say:

"That bar moved."

But places do not really move.

Businesses move.

The place stays in time.

The new room may have the same sign.

It cannot have the same people, the same age, the same Friday night, the same version of you.

That is why returning can disappoint.

You expect continuity from geography.

Memory does not work that way.

A place is not just coordinates.

It is a combination.

Music.

People.

Lighting.

Prices.

Habits.

Who you were going there with.

What time you used to arrive.

Whether you had work the next morning.

Whether you cared.

Change one enough and the place becomes something else.

Nil Bar still had its own atmosphere when I returned.

That was what I wrote.

Small as it was, it had a special feeling.

You could feel as if you were friends with the band and the people around you.

That sentence says more about the place than any review score could.

The best bars are not necessarily the ones with the best furniture, acoustics, service, menu, or location.

Sometimes they are the ones where the social distance is smallest.

You enter alone and do not remain psychologically alone.

Faces repeat.

The band repeats.

People stand in the same areas.

Someone recognizes someone.

The room slowly creates a temporary community.

Not a deep one.

Not necessarily a lasting one.

But enough for a Thursday night.

My old entry also contained the usual observations of a young man in a bar.

The gender ratio.

Who was looking at whom.

A well-dressed drunk woman went outside to talk on the phone, and I joked that the inside nearly emptied because so many men followed the visual event.

Not literally, perhaps.

But close enough to become a joke.

I also concluded that Nil Bar was a better place to go for Şeytan Tüyü than to go looking for women.

That sentence belongs very clearly to 2010.

It carries the priorities, language, and comic exaggeration of that version of me.

I would write it differently now.

But I would not remove the atmosphere behind it.

A live-music bar in your twenties is not experienced as a neutral cultural venue.

It is music.

Friends.

Alcohol.

Attraction.

Noise.

Observation.

Possibility.

Disappointment.

Standing around.

Waiting.

Trying to hear what someone is saying.

Pretending not to look.

Looking anyway.

The whole social mess is part of the memory.

Cleaning it too much would make the night false.

What matters is understanding it from farther away.

At the time, I was reviewing a bar.

Years later, I think I was documenting a disappearing form of social life.

Before everything became heavily mediated through phones, apps, photographs, and carefully maintained online identities, places like this did much of the social work.

You went somewhere.

You saw who was there.

You heard a band.

You stayed because the room felt good.

You ran into people.

You waited.

Nothing had to become content.

The night could remain local.

Temporary.

Unrecorded except for a few memories and, in my case, an Ekşi Sözlük entry.

That feels increasingly valuable.

Not because the past was better.

It was not.

Bars were still overcrowded.

Toilets were still terrible.

People were still annoying.

Drunk people were still drunk.

Young men were still staring at women.

Bands were still better or worse depending on the night.

Nostalgia should not sterilize reality.

What was different was our relationship with the moment.

There was less pressure to preserve it perfectly.

So imperfect memories became enough.

I do not remember every song played that night.

I do not remember what I drank.

I do not remember exactly who sat where.

I remember waiting outside until around eleven.

Going downstairs.

Hearing a band I had not heard in roughly two years.

Noticing time on their faces.

Cem singing.

The strange closeness of the room.

The drummer asking to cut the toilet line.

Then the bass player doing the same.

That is enough.

Memory often survives through ridiculous details.

Maybe because ridiculous details are human.

A perfect concert photograph tells you what the stage looked like.

The toilet queue tells you what it felt like to be there.

Years later, that is the part I value most.

Not whether Nil Bar was objectively one of Ankara's best venues.

Not whether Şeytan Tüyü played their best set.

Not whether every observation in my old review was fair.

The value is that one ordinary Thursday night still exists somewhere.

A small room in Ankara.

A band playing downstairs.

Too many people inside.

Us waiting outside.

Then finally going in.

For a few hours, everyone felt close enough to be a friend.

And the musicians still had to wait for the same toilet as the rest of us.
