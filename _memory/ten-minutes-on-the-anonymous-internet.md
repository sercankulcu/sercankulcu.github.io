On 4 September 2011, I gave a website ten minutes.

Connected2.me.

That was enough.

I wrote that I could not remember any other ten-minute period in my life in which I had received and delivered so much swearing.

Ten minutes.

Full allocation used.

Experiment complete.

My conclusion was not subtle:

the idea might be good in theory, but it did not suit us.

The “us” was Turkey, or perhaps more broadly the online culture I had just encountered.

The entry asks what kind of strange society finds pleasure in insulting complete strangers.

The language is crude because the experience was crude.

But the question survived.

What happens to people when identity becomes cheap?

Anonymous communication is one of the oldest internet experiments.

Give people a name and history, and they behave one way.

Remove persistent identity and some behave another.

Not everyone.

Not always.

But enough for entire platforms to develop reputations around it.

The appeal is obvious.

Anonymity can create freedom.

Ask a question you are embarrassed to ask publicly.

Talk about loneliness.

Fear.

Sex.

Family.

Work.

Politics.

Health.

Confession.

A stranger can sometimes be easier to talk to precisely because they do not know who you are.

There is no tomorrow.

No reputation to protect.

No shared social circle.

No awkward meeting next week.

You can say the thing and disappear.

That is powerful.

It can be humane.

Then there is the other side.

No tomorrow.

No reputation.

No shared circle.

No consequence.

The same conditions that make confession possible can make cruelty cheap.

A stranger becomes an object.

There is no face.

No social cost.

No need to repair the interaction.

Insult.

Leave.

New stranger.

Repeat.

My ten-minute visit apparently concentrated that second side so effectively that I abandoned the experiment.

This is a useful reminder about technology:

a good feature does not guarantee a good culture.

Anonymous matching is a mechanism.

Culture is what people do with it.

Design can encourage some behaviors and discourage others, but users always complete the product.

A platform may imagine:

spontaneous conversation.

Users may discover:

competitive abuse.

The gap between intended use and actual use is often where internet history becomes funny.

Or depressing.

Connected2.me was not the first place to learn this.

Chat rooms had learned it.

Forums.

IRC.

Comment sections.

Online games.

Later social media.

Every system that reduces social friction also reduces some social restraints.

The internet makes connection easier.

It also makes departure easier.

That second part matters.

In face-to-face life, insulting someone may produce consequences immediately.

A look.

An argument.

Social exclusion.

A punch.

At minimum, you remain in the same physical environment after speaking.

Online, especially anonymously, exit is a button.

Cruelty becomes more affordable when escape is instant.

This does not mean people are naturally monsters held back only by identity.

That is too simple.

Many anonymous spaces are supportive.

Some people become kinder when status disappears.

They listen without prejudice.

They admit vulnerability.

The tool amplifies possibility in both directions.

Anonymity removes one layer of inhibition.

What appears underneath depends on the person and the surrounding norms.

Norms are important.

If the first ten interactions are hostile, the eleventh person arrives already defensive.

They insult first.

The culture reproduces itself.

Soon people say:

That is just how the platform is.

But “how the platform is” is often a collective habit stabilized by expectation.

New users copy what they see.

This is how online spaces become countries with local customs.

One forum is helpful.

Another sarcastic.

One subreddit is patient.

Another hostile.

One game server is friendly.

Another toxic.

The software may be almost identical.

The people create climate.

My old entry said:

nice idea in theory.

That phrase applies to an enormous amount of social technology.

Connect strangers.

Great.

Allow comments.

Great.

Let everyone publish.

Great.

Let people rate each other.

Great.

Let anyone reply instantly.

Great.

Then humans arrive.

This is not cynicism.

It is the actual design problem.

Social products are partly behavioral systems.

They cannot be evaluated only by whether the feature works technically.

Can two strangers connect?

Yes.

Now:

What happens after they connect?

That is the harder question.

The old entry answers with ten minutes of mutual swearing.

Not an ideal KPI.

There is another interesting part: I included myself.

I did not write only that people insulted me.

I wrote that I had both received and given an extraordinary amount of abuse.

That matters.

It is easy to describe toxic culture as something other people do.

Bad users.

Idiots.

Trolls.

But environments recruit behavior.

A hostile space can make a normal person hostile quickly.

Someone insults you.

You answer.

Another arrives.

You assume the pattern.

Now you are helping produce exactly the culture you dislike.

This is how systems trap participants.

The person says:

Everyone here is awful.

While contributing.

That self-implication makes the memory better.

I was not standing above the mess.

I was in it.

Ten minutes was enough to change my language.

This is a strong argument for leaving environments that reliably produce a worse version of yourself.

We often ask:

Is this platform useful?

Another question may be more important:

Who do I become while using it?

More patient?

More curious?

More anxious?

More angry?

More performative?

More jealous?

More cruel?

Technology shapes mood through repetition.

Not magically.

Behaviorally.

If every interaction invites conflict, you begin opening the app prepared for conflict.

If every metric invites comparison, you begin comparing.

If every notification suggests urgency, you live urgently.

A tool can work perfectly while damaging the user experience at a deeper level.

The ten-minute Connected2.me experiment was crude enough to make the answer obvious.

I did not need weeks of usage analytics.

I already disliked who everybody had become, including me.

Exit.

There is something healthy about short experiments.

Modern platforms are designed to overcome the impulse to leave.

Notifications.

Streaks.

Social graphs.

Saved history.

Followers.

Recommendations.

The longer you stay, the more leaving costs.

An anonymous stranger platform has less lock-in.

That allowed the simplest possible review:

I gave you ten minutes.

You used them.

Goodbye.

We could probably use more of that discipline now.

Not every platform deserves months.

Not every app deserves a habit.

Not every social environment deserves adaptation.

Sometimes the correct response to a bad first impression is not:

Maybe I need to learn the culture.

It is:

No.

The entry also belongs to a transitional internet.

2011 was already social-media heavy, but the modern ecosystem of identity, feeds, algorithmic ranking, creator economies, and constant mobile presence was still developing.

Anonymous chat felt like a distinct mode.

You chose to enter it.

Today anonymity and pseudonymity are distributed across many services.

Temporary accounts.

Comments.

Messaging.

Gaming.

Platforms where the name is real but the social distance is effectively anonymous.

The underlying question remains:

How much accountability is necessary for good conversation?

Too much identity can suppress honesty.

Too little can suppress restraint.

There is no universal answer.

Different communities need different balances.

A support group may benefit from anonymity.

A marketplace needs trust.

A neighborhood forum may need identity.

A political discussion may need protection from retaliation.

Design is context.

What my 2011 entry proves only is that one anonymous interaction environment gave me an extremely bad ten-minute sample.

That is enough for a personal memory.

Not enough for a universal theorem.

The younger me nevertheless tried one:

“this does not work for us.”

That exaggeration is part of the period voice.

We often universalize immediately after a vivid experience.

Bad restaurant:

Nobody knows how to cook anymore.

Bad driver:

This country cannot drive.

Bad app:

This society cannot use technology.

The intensity of the moment creates national sociology.

Years later, it is funny.

But sometimes the exaggerated complaint points toward a real mechanism.

Anonymous interaction can expose how thin civility becomes when social cost approaches zero.

The mechanism remains relevant even if the national conclusion was too broad.

I also like the fixed duration.

Ten minutes is cinematic.

It gives the memory shape.

Open.

Connect.

Insult.

Receive insult.

Repeat.

Enough.

Close.

There is no need for extra scenery.

No usernames.

No invented conversations.

The source gives none.

The absence helps.

Everyone remains anonymous even in the memoir.

Appropriate.

Perhaps the best line to keep is not the insult but the limit.

“I gave it ten minutes.”

Attention is one of the few resources users can still control directly.

Platforms compete for it.

Advertisers value it.

Algorithms optimize around it.

The user can still sometimes say:

You get ten.

That is agency.

In 2011, I spent those ten minutes discovering that connection without context can become collision.

The experiment ended badly.

The exit worked perfectly.
