---
title: "The Day I Stopped Picking Up Hitchhikers"
date: 2010-07-13
tags:

---

Experience does not always make you wiser.

Sometimes it simply makes you suspicious.

There was a time when seeing someone hitchhiking by the road created a simple question:

Should I stop?

Then experience added several more questions.

Where exactly are they going?

Will "somewhere on the way" remain somewhere on the way?

Will they sit in the front?

Will they say thank you?

Will I somehow become a free taxi service before this is over?

Years ago, I wrote about the strange hesitation that can appear when a very attractive woman is hitchhiking.

From a distance, the decision looks easy.

Of course you stop.

You are helping someone.

She needs a ride.

You have a car.

Civilization survives through small acts of kindness.

Then memory arrives.

Memory is less romantic.

I had apparently learned that picking someone up could become a much larger commitment than the original roadside agreement suggested.

The arrangement begins simply.

"I'm going that way."

"Great, I can drop you there."

Everybody understands the contract.

Or at least you think everybody understands it.

Then you reach the agreed place.

And hear:

"Could you maybe take me a little farther?"

This is a dangerous sentence.

The words "a little" have caused enormous logistical problems throughout human history.

A little farther.

Just around there.

It's not far.

You are already going in that direction.

You are not.

But now refusing feels unnecessarily rude.

So naturally you say:

"Sure."

Because saying "sure" is much easier than explaining why you do not want to drive another ten or fifteen minutes for a complete stranger.

Then the route changes.

Your plan changes.

You are no longer helping someone along your journey.

You have begun participating in theirs.

That is a small but important distinction.

At the time, I joked that a beautiful hitchhiker might be especially dangerous because you would happily change your route.

Of course.

No problem.

Anything else?

Would you like me to carry the bags upstairs?

Should I wait outside in case you need another ride later?

Attraction has a remarkable ability to make inconvenience look voluntary.

The problem was not even the extra distance.

It was what happened at the end.

After being taken beyond the original destination, the person could leave without even saying good evening.

Not a phone number.

That would have been optimistic.

Not even a basic farewell.

Door opens.

Passenger disappears.

End of service.

This is the moment when generosity becomes comedy.

You sit there for a second and mentally review the transaction.

I stopped.

I gave you a ride.

I changed my route.

I drove farther because you asked.

And apparently the final human interaction is the sound of the car door closing.

Excellent.

Five-star passenger experience.

There was another version too.

The passenger gets into the back seat.

That changes everything immediately.

If there are only two people in a car and the other person sits directly behind you, the social geometry becomes very clear.

You are no longer two strangers sharing a ride.

You are the driver.

They are the passenger.

The difference is not legal.

It is psychological.

Suddenly you feel like you should ask:

"Which route do you prefer?"

Perhaps turn on the meter.

Maybe issue a receipt.

The back seat has power.

Friends generally sit in front.

Someone you are helping usually sits in front.

The back seat says:

Take me there.

This is not universal, of course.

There can be perfectly reasonable reasons to sit in the back.

But in the particular kind of memory that becomes an internet entry, reasonable explanations are not invited.

Only irritation survives.

Looking back, the story is funny because almost nothing happened.

Nobody robbed me.

Nobody threatened me.

No dramatic disaster.

I simply did someone a favor and did not feel appreciated.

That was enough to alter future behavior.

This is how trust often changes.

Not through catastrophe.

Through small unpaid debts of courtesy.

Someone does not say thank you.

Someone takes more than was offered.

Someone treats generosity as an entitlement.

Nothing is serious enough to become a major story.

But the next time a similar situation appears, you remember.

Experience enters the car before the passenger does.

That is why older people sometimes look unnecessarily cautious to younger people.

The younger person sees the current situation.

The older person sees the previous five situations standing behind it.

This can become unfair.

One rude passenger does not mean the next person will be rude.

One bad experience does not justify treating everyone as a problem.

But the brain is not a neutral statistical system.

It remembers inconvenience.

Especially inconvenience we volunteered for.

There is a particular resentment in thinking:

I did not have to do this.

That sentence changes the emotional accounting.

If you are required to do something unpleasant, you blame the requirement.

If you volunteer and regret it, you blame yourself.

Maybe that is why small acts of generosity need boundaries.

Helping someone does not require surrendering the rest of your plans.

You can say:

"I can take you this far."

Then actually stop there.

This sounds obvious.

It is surprisingly difficult in practice.

People do not like appearing ungenerous after already being generous.

Once you say yes, the second request becomes easier to extract.

There is probably a broader lesson in this.

Many boundaries are not tested at the beginning.

They are tested after you have already agreed to something.

Can you help with this?

Yes.

And this?

Maybe.

And while you're here...

And suddenly a favor has expanded into a responsibility.

The hitchhiking version is simply more visible because the kilometers increase on the road.

You can literally watch the boundary moving.

I also see a younger version of myself in that old entry.

The joke was partly about the woman being beautiful.

The expectation was that perhaps the encounter might become something more interesting than transportation.

Maybe there would be conversation.

Maybe a phone number.

Maybe at least a memorable goodbye.

Instead:

Special-driver treatment.

Extended route.

No "good evening."

The universe had refused to reward the good deed according to the private fantasy attached to it.

That is another useful lesson.

A favor is safer when it is actually a favor.

If you expect gratitude, affection, attention, or a future opportunity in return, you have quietly converted it into a transaction.

The other person may not know there is a transaction.

Then disappointment becomes almost guaranteed.

Stop because you want to help.

Or do not stop.

Both can be honest decisions.

But stopping because you imagine a story afterward is dangerous.

Real people are under no obligation to follow the script.

That does not excuse basic rudeness.

A thank-you costs very little.

So does a goodbye.

Courtesy is still courtesy.

But expectations should remain proportional to what actually happened.

You gave someone a ride.

You did not purchase a chapter in their life.

Years later, what I remember most is not the passenger.

It is the change in myself.

Before the experience, the roadside question was simple.

Afterward, hesitation appeared.

That is what experience often is:

a small delay between impulse and action.

You see the same situation.

But now there is history inside it.

Maybe you still stop.

Maybe you do not.

Either way, you are no longer making the decision for the first time.

And if the person gets into the back seat, at least consider asking where they would like the receipt sent.
