In April 2010, I wrote a short note about Kentpark in Ankara.

At first glance, it reads like a simple shopping-mall review.

Food on the ground floor.

A balcony upstairs for smokers and people eating.

More spacious tables for non-smokers.

A cinema I did not like.

A toy shop.

And toilets so aggressively clean, or at least so aggressively filled with cleaning-product smell, that I thought I might faint.

That was the entire review.

Years later, I like it less as consumer advice and more as a small record of how cities enter memory.

Not through architectural criticism.

Not through urban-planning theory.

Through where the food is.

Whether there is a place to smoke.

Whether the tables are too close together.

Whether the cinema is any good.

Whether the toilets smell like a chemical attack.

This is how people actually experience buildings.

A shopping mall can publish floor plans, brand lists, square meters, parking capacity, and promotional photographs.

A visitor remembers:

There was a balcony upstairs.

That distinction matters.

Official descriptions preserve what a place was designed to be.

Personal memory preserves what it was like to use.

The first thing that surprised me was the food.

I wrote that Kentpark was the first mall I had seen with places to eat on the entrance floor.

That tells me something about my own expectations in 2010.

Apparently I already had a model of how shopping malls were supposed to work.

You enter.

Shops.

More shops.

Eventually you climb upward toward food.

Kentpark broke that little internal rule.

Today, a detail like that may sound trivial.

It was trivial then too.

But trivial differences are exactly what people notice when many places begin to resemble one another.

Shopping malls are built around repetition.

Corridors.

Glass.

Escalators.

Storefronts.

Lighting.

Food.

Cinema.

Restrooms.

You can travel between cities and enter spaces that seem to speak the same visual language.

That makes deviations more noticeable.

Food here?

Interesting.

A balcony?

Good.

Tables not packed together?

Even better.

The small differences become identity.

This was especially true in Ankara during a period when malls were becoming increasingly central to urban life.

Not necessarily because they were culturally profound.

Because they were convenient.

Warm in winter.

Cool in summer.

Food available.

Cinema available.

Parking available.

Bathrooms available.

Places to sit.

A mall compresses several ordinary needs into one controlled environment.

That convenience can feel almost invisible once you get used to it.

But when a new place opens, you compare.

This one does something better.

That one has a worse cinema.

This one has room to breathe.

The old entry is exactly that kind of comparison.

The balcony mattered because smoking still shaped how people used social spaces much more visibly.

The entry separates smokers and non-smokers almost naturally.

Upstairs, there was a successful balcony for people smoking and eating.

For non-smokers, there were also tables with more space between them.

That sounds like simple furniture arrangement.

In memory, it carries an era.

Smoking organized social geography.

Who goes outside?

Who stays?

Where can we sit?

Is there a balcony?

How cold is it?

Can we take our drinks there?

A smoker evaluating a venue does not evaluate only the venue.

He evaluates the smoking infrastructure.

This was true of bars, restaurants, offices, malls, and almost every place where people spent time.

A good balcony could meaningfully improve a building.

That is a strangely specific period detail.

The balcony was not an architectural bonus.

It was operationally important.

I had already written other memories where balconies appeared for the same reason.

Work breaks.

Bars.

Cold nights.

Standing outside.

Smoking creates its own secondary city.

A city made of doorways, balconies, pavements, terraces, and places just outside the official place.

People who smoke learn those edges.

That was part of how I read Kentpark too.

Not only inside.

Where can you step slightly outside while still remaining there?

Then there were the non-smoker tables.

I specifically noticed that they were not packed tightly together.

More spacious.

That is another thing official photographs often fail to communicate.

Density.

A place can look beautiful and still make you feel like the stranger at the next table is part of your conversation.

Space is experienced through other bodies.

Can I move the chair?

Can someone pass behind me?

Am I eating with my friend or with six neighboring tables?

You remember comfort physically.

That is why “the tables were not too close together” can survive longer than the names of stores.

Retail brands change.

The sensation of room remains.

Then comes the cinema.

My review is wonderfully useless:

I am not saying anything about the cinema.

I did not like it.

That is the entire verdict.

No screen size.

No sound.

No seats.

No reason.

Just no.

This is where personal archives become honest in a way professional reviews often cannot.

Not every opinion arrives with evidence.

Sometimes you leave somewhere and think:

Didn’t like it.

That is all the mind produces.

Years later, we are tempted to improve the younger self.

Surely there was a reason.

Maybe the seats were bad.

Maybe the sound was weak.

Maybe the layout annoyed me.

The source gives none of that.

So the reason should remain lost.

I did not like the cinema.

Memory does not owe us a technical report.

There was also a toy shop.

One.

That detail has almost no narrative importance.

Which is exactly why I enjoy it.

A person walking through a new mall notices random things.

Not according to hierarchy.

Not according to commercial importance.

The brain takes snapshots.

Food downstairs.

Balcony.

Spacious tables.

Cinema bad.

Toy shop.

Bathroom.

This is not a structured review.

It is a walk.

You can almost reconstruct movement through the building from the order of attention.

That does not mean I know the exact route I took.

I do not.

But the entry feels spatial because the observations are spatial.

A place becomes a sequence of impressions.

Then comes the strongest one.

The toilets.

Extremely clean when I visited.

So clean that the smell of cleaning products was overwhelming.

I wrote that I nearly fainted.

Of course the comedy is obvious.

Cleanliness has been pushed so far that it becomes another form of discomfort.

There is a point where hygiene stops smelling like cleanliness and starts smelling like a chemistry department.

The room says:

We cleaned this.

Your lungs say:

I believe you.

That detail saves the whole entry from becoming generic.

Without it, Kentpark is simply another mall with food and a balcony.

With it, the place has a smell.

Smell is one of memory’s strongest anchors and one of writing’s hardest things to preserve.

Photographs cannot do it.

Floor plans cannot do it.

Promotional videos cannot do it.

But one sentence can.

Too much cleaning product.

Almost fainted.

Now the bathroom survives.

This is why seemingly ridiculous details matter in personal history.

Important memories often come with images.

Ordinary memories often come with sensations.

A smell.

A temperature.

The feel of a chair.

The sound of a ventilation system.

The heaviness of a door.

The brightness of a corridor.

These things rarely deserve deliberate documentation at the time.

Then years pass.

They become the difference between remembering a place and merely knowing that you once visited it.

I know many places only informationally.

I was there.

I remember almost nothing.

Kentpark has at least these fragments.

That is more than many buildings get.

There is another layer here about newness.

New places often smell intensely artificial.

Paint.

Plastic.

Wood treatment.

Cleaning chemicals.

Air conditioning.

Restaurants not yet fully absorbed into the background.

A new mall is designed to feel polished.

But polish has a sensory cost.

Everything is too clean.

Too bright.

Too controlled.

Older places soften.

Surfaces wear.

Smells mix.

People leave traces.

A building becomes less like a product and more like a habitat.

I cannot say whether Kentpark was brand-new during my visit or exactly what stage it was in; the source does not establish that.

So I will not turn this into a claim about its opening period.

What I can say is that I experienced it as something worth evaluating feature by feature.

That usually happens when a place has not yet become routine.

Routine compresses observation.

The first few visits:

Look at this.

Look at that.

Later:

Meet me at the usual table.

Familiarity removes architecture.

You stop seeing the building.

You see your route through it.

This is true of campuses, offices, malls, houses, and cities.

At first, you notice design.

Later, you notice whether your coffee is ready.

Memory is full of these transitions.

A place begins as object.

Then becomes background.

Then, after enough time passes, becomes memory again.

The old entry catches Kentpark before complete familiarity.

It is still an object being inspected.

Food floor.

Balcony.

Tables.

Cinema.

Toy shop.

Toilets.

Checklist complete.

What also strikes me now is how much social life around malls depended on simply having somewhere to be.

Not necessarily somewhere extraordinary.

Somewhere.

Urban adulthood requires meeting points.

Coffee shops.

Bars.

Parks.

Restaurants.

Malls.

Places where one person can say a name and everyone knows how to get there.

The value of such places is partly logistical.

Easy to explain.

Easy to wait.

Easy to eat.

Easy to continue somewhere else afterward.

A city is held together by these ordinary rendezvous points.

They may be commercially designed, culturally unromantic, architecturally repetitive.

Still, friendships happen inside them.

Dates happen.

Arguments happen.

Movies happen.

People wait for one another.

Someone arrives late.

Someone orders food.

Someone says:

Let’s go somewhere else.

The building becomes part of private history without earning any official historical importance.

This is why I do not dismiss mall memories.

They may not be the most poetic form of city life.

But they are real city life.

Ankara in my archive is not only streets and bars.

It is traffic rules.

Late-night meatballs.

IKEA.

Nil Bar.

University campuses.

And, apparently, noticing that Kentpark put food on the entrance floor.

A city is made from accumulated specificities.

The things you complain about.

The places you recommend.

The places you do not recommend.

The route you know without thinking.

The balcony you prefer.

The bathroom you remember for all the wrong reasons.

These fragments are more personal than any tourist guide.

Tourist guides ask:

What should a visitor see?

Memory asks:

What did I notice?

Those are different questions.

I did not record Kentpark’s most important features.

I recorded mine.

That is exactly what makes the entry useful now.

A professional review expires quickly.

Stores close.

Restaurants change.

Cinema operators change.

Furniture changes.

Policies change.

Prices change.

A 2010 shopping guide becomes unreliable.

A personal memory does not have the same problem.

It remains accurate about one thing:

what I thought on 16 April 2010.

That is enough.

This distinction is important for old web writing.

Many entries that once tried to be informative are now obsolete as information.

But they can become valuable as time capsules.

“The balcony was good” is no longer consumer advice.

It is evidence of what mattered to a smoker evaluating a mall in 2010.

“The tables were more spacious” records comfort expectations.

“I didn’t like the cinema” records taste, even if the reason is gone.

“The toilets smelled overwhelmingly of cleaning products” preserves sensory atmosphere.

The entry changes genre with age.

Review becomes memoir.

This happens to technology writing too.

A review of a phone from 2008 is useless if you are shopping today.

It is fascinating if you want to remember what counted as impressive in 2008.

Storage size.

Camera quality.

Keyboard.

Battery.

The standards reveal the era.

A mall review does the same.

What did I notice?

Smoking balcony.

There is 2010.

That may be the strongest historical marker in the whole piece.

Not because smoking has disappeared.

It has not.

But because the social choreography around it has changed across time, regulations, and personal habits.

The younger self evaluates access almost automatically.

The current reader sees an era.

This is one of the pleasures of archives:

you write without knowing what detail the future will find interesting.

The writer thinks the topic is Kentpark.

The future thinks the topic is you.

How you moved.

What you valued.

What irritated you.

What surprised you.

What you considered normal enough not to explain.

That last category is often the richest.

The things we do not explain reveal what the period assumed.

I did not explain why a smoking balcony was important.

No need.

The audience understood.

Years later, the absence of explanation becomes evidence.

This is also why preserving raw old language matters.

Not every old judgment needs polishing into timeless neutrality.

The cinema was bad because I said it was bad.

The smell was absurd.

The toy shop was merely there.

The younger voice was not writing urban criticism.

He was reporting back from a mall.

That tone deserves to survive.

The mature layer can add context without erasing it.

I can now say:

Look how ordinary these details are.

Look how much an era hides inside them.

But I should not pretend the 2010 entry secretly intended that depth.

It did not.

It was a review.

Maybe written quickly.

Maybe after a visit.

The source tells me only what is on the page.

And what is on the page is enough.

A good personal archive does not require every entry to be profound at birth.

Some entries age into meaning.

A mall review becomes a record of city habits.

A pair of headphones becomes a history of wired freedom.

A broken whistle becomes a souvenir of military service.

Two bottles of soda become student life.

The value arrives later.

That makes me more sympathetic to trivial writing.

We often ask ourselves whether a small observation is worth recording.

Probably not, in the moment.

But that is the wrong test.

The question is not:

Is this important now?

It is:

Will this help reconstruct what ordinary life felt like later?

We cannot know.

So some triviality is worth keeping.

Not all of it.

Nobody needs every receipt.

But a sentence with a strange smell can outlive a photograph.

Kentpark, April 2010.

Food on the entrance floor.

A good balcony upstairs.

Roomier tables.

Cinema: no.

One toy shop.

Toilets so clean they almost defeated the visitor.

That is not an architectural review.

It is better.

It is a small map of attention.

And perhaps that is what most memory really is.

Not a record of places.

A record of what our eyes, lungs, habits, and moods decided was worth noticing while we were there.
