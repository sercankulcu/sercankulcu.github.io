For the first few days, I could not find anything without thinking about the route.

The building was enormous. Corridors looked like other corridors, doors required badges, stairs carried me to places that seemed reasonable but were not where I wanted to go. I began creating landmarks in my head: turn after this room, not that elevator, too far if you see those doors.

Outside, aircraft passed close enough to remind everyone what the work was connected to. I was visiting an aerospace company, and even before seeing the simulators, the scale of the place made an impression on me.

University had taught engineering with diagrams. A system was usually a box connected to another box by an arrow. Inside this building, the boxes had become rooms. They had racks, cables, seats, displays, noise, and people walking around them carrying coffee.

Yet the things I remember most clearly are not technical.

There was the cafeteria, where the people serving food quickly developed opinions about what visitors would like. They would look at a dish, look at you, and sometimes make the decision on your behalf. In a building full of advanced technology, lunch still depended on ordinary human judgment.

Then there was smoking.

Smoking was not allowed inside, which was sensible. The problem was that if you happened to be deep inside the building, reaching an exit could take so long that a cigarette break became a journey. By the time you returned to your desk, the walk had taken more time than the cigarette.

Security had its own personality. Bringing a laptop into the building required procedures, but taking the same laptop out could produce a new conversation.

“It is mine.”

“Yes, but you are taking it out.”

“I also brought it in.”

“That is different.”

I learned that logical continuity and security procedure were not always the same thing.

The workplace was international, and accents from different countries filled meetings and corridors. At first, I tried to understand people through national categories. After a while, those categories became less useful. Some people were patient, some impatient, some direct, some careful. Mostly they were engineers trying to finish work.

One small object caught my attention near the simulator area: a Turkish flag.

At home, a flag would not have surprised me. There, among thousands of employees in a foreign technical environment, it felt different. It was a small familiar sign inside a machine much larger than anything I had worked in before.

By the end of the visit, I had learned how to reach the cafeteria, the exits, the simulator area, and most places I needed. The building had become less mysterious.

The work had not.

That was probably the more useful lesson.

A young engineer can enter a large company believing he is there to see advanced systems. Then he discovers something bigger: every system is surrounded by corridors, rules, meals, security guards, misunderstandings, and people.

Engineering was not only inside the machines.

It was also the building around them.
