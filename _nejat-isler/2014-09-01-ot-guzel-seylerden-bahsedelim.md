## OT: Güzel Şeylerden Bahsedelim

**OT — September 2014**  
**Interview by Ercan Mehmet Erdem**

In September 2014, Nejat İşler returned to a long-form interview after one of the most serious periods of his life.

Earlier that year, he had been hospitalized with a severe respiratory illness and septic shock and had spent an extended period in intensive care.

Public attention was naturally focused on one subject:

his health.

The OT interview could easily have become a dramatic survival narrative.

İşler resisted that immediately.

When Ercan Mehmet Erdem asked whether they should talk about the illness, İşler replied:

**“Güzel şeylerden bahsedelim.”**

Let's talk about good things.

That response is more important than it first appears.

### Refusing to Become Only "The Man Who Survived"

By September 2014, a new public identity was available to Nejat İşler.

Not leading man.

Not rebellious actor.

Not *Kaybedenler Kulübü* icon.

Not the dark figure associated with *Barda*.

Now he could become:

**the actor who nearly died.**

Celebrity culture often turns major illness into a complete narrative.

Before the illness.

The crisis.

The recovery.

The new person afterward.

İşler appears uncomfortable with allowing the event to take over the conversation.

He does not deny what happened.

He simply refuses to make it the only interesting thing about him.

That distinction matters.

### A Different Kind of Control

Earlier interviews show İşler trying to control how his public identity is defined.

In 2003, he resisted the “leading man” label.

In 2010, he resisted the assumption that being famous made his private life public property.

In 2012, he said the man people imagined from outside was not the same as the person at home.

Now the same struggle appears in a more serious form.

The public wants a recovery story.

İşler changes the subject.

This is another act of narrative control.

### Memory Instead of Medical History

Rather than building the interview around illness, the conversation turns toward memory.

Childhood.

Eyüp.

Past relationships.

Films.

Stories.

The places and people that remain in his mind.

This makes the interview unusually revealing.

A medical crisis places mortality in the room even when İşler refuses to speak directly about it.

As a result, memories acquire greater weight.

What remains from a life?

Which scenes are remembered?

Which people?

Which mistakes?

Which stories survive?

The interview approaches these questions indirectly.

### Eyüp as an Origin

İşler repeatedly returns to Eyüp when discussing his earlier life.

This is important because later public mythology will attach him strongly to other places:

Beyoğlu,

Istanbul nightlife,

Gümüşlük,

Bodrum.

But Eyüp remains an older layer of identity.

It represents a Nejat before celebrity.

Before the industry's labels.

Before the public construction of the rebellious actor.

When late-career İşler discusses childhood and youth, geography becomes a form of memory.

A place is not only where something happened.

It is evidence of who he was before other people began telling him who he was.

### "Remember It Beautifully"

One of the most interesting ideas in the surviving interview material concerns memory and storytelling.

İşler recalls an idea he learned through a past relationship:

an event can be painful or unpleasant, but the way it is remembered affects the way it will later be told.

The principle is close to:

**remember it beautifully, tell it beautifully.**

This is not the same as falsifying the past.

It suggests that memory is partly an act of composition.

We do not carry raw experience forever.

We carry versions of experience.

And every time we tell a story, we shape it again.

This idea is especially important for a Nejat İşler archive.

Because an interview archive is itself a collection of repeated self-narration.

The same childhood.

The same jobs.

The same relationships.

The same career.

The same illness.

But the meaning changes depending on when he tells the story.

### Life as Retelling

This allows us to read Nejat İşler's interviews differently.

An interview does not simply reveal facts.

It reveals:

**how he understands those facts at that moment.**

The difference matters.

A memory told at thirty is not necessarily the same memory told at fifty.

The event may be identical.

The interpretation is not.

This becomes particularly important after 2014.

A person who has confronted mortality may reorganize older memories around different priorities.

What once looked like failure may become funny.

What once seemed important may become trivial.

What seemed ordinary may become precious.

### The Illness Is Present Even When Unspoken

The remarkable thing about the OT interview is that the illness remains central precisely because İşler refuses to center it.

Every ordinary subject gains a slightly different meaning.

Childhood.

Love.

Cinema.

Friendship.

Memory.

They are no longer simply biographical topics.

They are things that survived.

The conversation therefore becomes less about medicine and more about continuity.

Who is Nejat İşler after the body has forced an interruption?

The answer is not presented as:

**a completely new man.**

Instead, he returns to older parts of himself.

### This Is Not Yet the Gümüşlük Philosophy

It is important not to read later philosophy backward into this interview.

In 2017, İşler will speak clearly about:

- buying time,
- reducing attachment to property,
- living away from Istanbul,
- organizing life differently.

Those ideas are not yet fully developed here.

In September 2014, he seems closer to a transitional state.

The crisis has happened.

The long-term philosophy is still forming.

This makes the interview valuable.

It captures the space between disruption and explanation.

### The Temptation to Invent a Transformation

Celebrity biographies often simplify life into turning points.

Before the illness, reckless.

After the illness, wise.

Before, city.

After, nature.

Before, alcohol.

After, health.

Real lives are rarely that clean.

The earlier archive already shows that İşler wanted distance from the industry in 2010.

He was already skeptical of conventional success in 2011 and 2012.

So it would be inaccurate to say that the 2014 illness created his later worldview from nothing.

A more careful interpretation is:

**the illness intensified questions that were already present.**

### The Body Changes the Meaning of Time

Even without a direct philosophical speech about mortality, the health crisis changes the context of every later discussion about time.

Before 2014, İşler complains that television takes too much of his life.

After 2014, time becomes more than a labor issue.

It becomes finite.

That distinction will become explicit in later interviews.

The road from:

**“I don't want to work like this”**

to:

**“I want to buy time”**

passes through 2014.

### Why Avoid the Illness?

There are several possible interpretations of İşler's refusal to discuss his health.

It may be emotional fatigue.

He may simply be tired of answering the same questions.

It may reflect privacy.

It may be an attempt to prevent public sympathy from becoming part of his professional identity.

Or it may be something simpler:

he wants to talk about life rather than the event that nearly ended it.

The archive should not pretend to know which explanation is correct.

But the refusal itself is meaningful.

### From Public Body to Private Memory

Illness made İşler's body public in an unusually intrusive way.

Hospital reports.

Medical updates.

Photographs.

Rumors.

Questions about his condition.

The OT interview moves in the opposite direction.

Away from the public body.

Toward private memory.

That movement feels consistent with his earlier resistance to celebrity culture.

Again, he tries to decide which version of himself will be discussed.

### Friendship and Conversation

The interviewer also matters.

Ercan Mehmet Erdem was not simply an anonymous magazine reporter.

He was a screenwriter strongly associated with *Behzat Ç.*, a world in which İşler had created Ercüment Çözer.

That familiarity changes the tone.

The interview feels less like a formal celebrity interrogation and more like a conversation between people from overlapping creative circles.

This may partly explain why the discussion can move easily between memory, cinema, personal history and humor.

### The Return of Humor

Humor is significant in post-crisis interviews.

There is a tendency for serious illness to force public figures into solemn language.

İşler often resists that.

He returns to stories.

Irony.

Self-mockery.

The refusal to become permanently solemn is itself part of continuity.

The person after illness is still connected to the person before it.

### Memory as Survival

There is a deeper way to read this interview.

A person survives biologically.

But identity survives through memory.

Old neighborhoods.

Old lovers.

Films.

Friends.

Stories.

Repeated jokes.

The self continues because these connections remain available.

In that sense, the OT interview becomes less a recovery interview than a **memory interview**.

### A Useful Comparison with 2016

By 2016, İşler will speak much more directly about the consequences of the health crisis.

He will ask what would have remained if he had died.

He will explain that he no longer wants to live only for himself.

That is a much more developed moral interpretation.

The 2014 interview comes before that conclusion.

Here he is not yet explaining what the crisis means.

He is simply back.

Talking.

Remembering.

### A Useful Comparison with 2019

The memory theme will return strongly in the 2019 Socrates interview.

By then, İşler has also returned to writing.

That matters because writing gives memory a more permanent form.

The progression becomes:

**2014 — remember and tell.**

**2016 — reconsider what the remaining life should be used for.**

**2019 — turn memory and lived experience into writing.**

This is one of the strongest long-term threads in the archive.

### A Useful Comparison with Miras

The same thread becomes even more important in 2025 with *Miras*.

There, the older İşler deliberately returns to the voice and world of his younger self.

Memory becomes not merely something discussed in interviews but material for literature.

This makes the 2014 OT conversation look even more significant in retrospect.

The post-illness period begins with storytelling before it becomes philosophy and later fiction.

## Why This Interview Matters

The September 2014 OT interview is one of the central transition documents in Nejat İşler's archive.

It comes immediately after a major health crisis, but İşler refuses to allow the crisis to dominate his identity.

His response —

**“Güzel şeylerden bahsedelim.”**

— establishes the tone.

Rather than turning the interview into a medical confession, he returns to childhood, memory, love, cinema and stories.

The result is important because it captures İşler before he has fully developed the later philosophy associated with Gümüşlük, time and reduced attachment to conventional success.

The crisis has happened.

The interpretation is still forming.

And perhaps the most important theme is not illness but memory:

**life becomes not only what happened, but what can still be remembered and how it is chosen to be told.**
