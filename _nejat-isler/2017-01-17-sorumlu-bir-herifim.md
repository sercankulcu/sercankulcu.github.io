## Sorumlu Bir Herifim

**L'Officiel Hommes Türkiye — January 2017**  
**Habertürk reproduction — 17 January 2017**

By early 2017, two very different versions of Nejat İşler existed in public.

One was familiar from celebrity culture:

rebellious,

unpredictable,

difficult,

a man who disliked rules.

The other was increasingly visible in Gümüşlük:

football-club president,

organizer,

fundraiser,

someone responsible for a youth program and a growing local institution.

The L'Officiel Hommes interview confronts this contradiction directly.

When the interviewer suggests that the “serseri” and “huysuz” reputation may hide the useful things he does, İşler answers:

**“Ben serseri falan değilim! Sorumlu bir herifim.”**

He is not interested in replacing the rebel myth with a heroic one.

But he insists on one correction.

He has responsibilities.

### The Public Image Is Missing Something

By this point, the hard Nejat İşler persona is well established.

The roles helped create it.

So did:

nightlife reporting,

conflicts with journalists,

his resistance to television,

*Kayıbedenler Kulübü*,

Ercüment Çözer.

But public images are built through selection.

Certain stories circulate more easily than others.

A confrontation is better celebrity news than a youth football school.

The result can be a person who is publicly understood through the loudest fragments of his biography.

### Responsibility Does Not Look Rebellious

Gümüşlükspor creates a very different kind of daily life.

By January 2017, the club is providing free football education to more than a hundred children, while İşler is helping develop a larger infrastructure project around the club. Income from his book *Gerçek Hesap Bu!* is also being directed toward the organization.

This is not symbolic support.

Administration creates routine obligations.

Money.

Facilities.

Children.

Coaches.

Matches.

Planning.

The supposedly irresponsible celebrity has deliberately placed himself inside a system of responsibility.

### After Illness, Responsibility Becomes Personal

İşler connects this change partly with the period after his severe illness.

He says he thought a great deal about how much his mother had suffered.

Then comes one of his simplest late-life wishes:

he wants to be a son his mother loves and can feel good about.

This is striking because it is so small compared with celebrity ambition.

Not:

be remembered as a great actor.

Not:

become richer.

Not:

become culturally important.

Be a good son.

### The Mother's Measure

This provides a very different measure of success.

Public success is external.

Awards.

Audience.

Money.

Recognition.

The mother's measure is private.

What kind of person have you become?

The contrast helps explain why İşler's post-illness priorities increasingly move away from career metrics.

### A Useful Link to 2013

The 2013 Asu Maro interview already showed how strongly İşler admired his mother's instinct to help other people.

As a younger person, he had sometimes found her constant generosity difficult to understand.

With age, he understood it better.

The 2017 statement makes that family influence even clearer.

His philosophy of contribution does not come only from illness.

Illness may have intensified values that were already present in his family model.

### "I Wasn't in Love with This Profession"

The interview also contains an unusually unsentimental statement about acting.

İşler says he was not originally in love with the profession.

Now he loves it, but that affection developed later.

He describes acting as something he discovered he could do without excessive effort.

This is important because actor biographies are often rewritten backward.

Child loves cinema.

Child dreams of stage.

Destiny is fulfilled.

İşler consistently refuses that story.

### Ability Before Calling

Acting begins more accidentally.

School theatre.

Conservatory.

Opportunities.

Work.

Only later does professional affection deepen.

This gives his career a different structure.

He did not spend his life serving a childhood dream.

The profession became meaningful through doing it.

### No Need for Sacred Origin

This fits his broader resistance to mythology.

A profession can matter deeply without being destiny.

A person can become good at something without having always known it was the only possible life.

That makes his later expansions into:

writing,

football,

producing

much easier to understand.

Acting never had to occupy the entire identity.

### The Television Question Returns

The interviewer then points out an obvious financial contradiction.

Gümüşlükspor needs money.

Television series can pay very well.

Why not return to series work?

İşler's answer is immediate:

**“Yapmam.”**

This is one of the clearest moments where money fails to defeat the time principle.

### Television Made Him Famous

Importantly, İşler does not deny what television gave him.

He openly calls himself a television child and acknowledges that television played a major role in making his career possible.

That makes the refusal more credible.

He is not attacking a medium from outside.

He knows what it gave him.

He also knows what it cost.

### The "Bad Marriage" Metaphor

His comparison is excellent.

Television series, he says, are a little like bad marriages.

At the beginning you are promised the beautiful house with the garden.

Then, as the relationship continues, conditions change.

Cinema is different.

The terms are clearer.

The end is visible.

The metaphor captures almost twenty years of his complaints about television production.

### The Problem Is Changing Conditions

This connects directly with the archived 2007 *Barda* interview.

Even then, İşler complained that production conditions could change after an actor had already agreed to a project.

By 2017, he has transformed that professional frustration into a memorable metaphor:

the agreement you entered is no longer the agreement you are living.

### Cinema Has an Ending

Again, the appeal of cinema is structural.

A film begins.

You know broadly what you are making.

You know that it will finish.

The end matters enormously to İşler.

An endless series can slowly take ownership of a life.

A film has boundaries.

### Illness and Television

İşler also says that he believes the pressure of series work contributed to the collapse that preceded his serious illness.

This should be treated carefully.

It is **his own retrospective interpretation**, not a medical conclusion established by the interview.

Archivally, however, it is important.

By 2017, he personally understands television exhaustion not merely as inconvenience but as something connected in his mind with physical danger.

### From Labor Complaint to Bodily Memory

That gives the chronology additional weight.

**2007:** production conditions can become intolerable.

**2010:** television schedules are physically unreasonable.

**2014:** severe illness.

**2017:** İşler retrospectively connects overwork with what happened to him.

Even without claiming medical causation, the psychological sequence is clear.

The body has become part of the labor argument.

### There Is No Going Back to the Old Exchange

Before illness, the television bargain was:

sell large amounts of time,

receive large amounts of money.

After illness, that bargain has become much harder for him to accept.

Money is still useful.

Gümüşlükspor needs it.

But money cannot automatically justify the work that earns it.

### Responsibility Creates a Paradox

This creates a fascinating contradiction.

İşler now has **more** responsibilities.

The football club.

Children.

Projects.

Yet those responsibilities do not push him back toward the highest-paying work.

Instead, he tries to support them while preserving the boundaries he created around television.

That suggests there are two kinds of responsibility.

### Chosen Responsibility vs Imposed Obligation

Television can create imposed obligation:

you signed,

the series continues,

the schedule expands,

your time disappears.

Gümüşlükspor creates chosen responsibility:

he entered voluntarily,

believes in the purpose,

accepts the burden.

This distinction becomes central to his mature idea of freedom.

Freedom does not mean having no responsibilities.

It means choosing responsibilities you believe are worth carrying.

### "I Am Not a Hero"

The interview also challenges another possible mythology.

When conversation moves toward public expectation, İşler rejects the idea that people should expect heroism from him.

This echoes his 2016 statement:

**“Benden rol modeli falan olmaz.”**

Again, contribution does not require sainthood.

### Responsibility Without Heroism

This is perhaps one of the most consistent principles of the Gümüşlük period.

Do something useful.

Take responsibility.

Use visibility if useful.

But do not construct a moral monument around yourself.

The distinction protects both him and the work.

### Fenerbahçe Becomes Secondary

There is also a humorous but revealing football shift.

İşler says that once the Gümüşlükspor project became serious, even Fenerbahçe began to move into the background.

The local club's Sunday match could create more immediate excitement than the giant club he had supported throughout his life.

This demonstrates the difference between fandom and participation.

### Participation Beats Spectatorship

Fenerbahçe is emotionally important.

But Gümüşlükspor requires action.

The result of the local match is tied to work he and others have actually done.

Participation creates a different intensity.

This is another reason football becomes much more than entertainment during these years.

### The Rebel Becomes Accountable

If the archive stopped around 2011, Nejat İşler could easily be summarized as an anti-hero figure.

By 2017, that summary is no longer adequate.

He has become responsible for:

people,

money,

an institution,

community expectations.

The outsider has not joined conventional respectable life.

But he has become accountable.

### A Useful Comparison with January 2016

A year earlier, İşler rejected the label of role model after Gümüşlükspor's championship.

The 2017 interview explains the distinction more clearly.

He does not want to be a model.

He does want to be responsible.

Those are different identities.

### A Useful Comparison with May 2016

The May 2016 interview provided the moral argument:

he no longer wanted to live only for himself.

The January 2017 interview shows that principle becoming ordinary life.

Not philosophy.

Administration.

### A Useful Comparison with March 2017

Only weeks later, Mehmet Çalışkan's Habertürk interview will again bring forward the idea of buying time.

The two 2017 conversations work particularly well together.

**January:**  
What kind of responsibility is worth accepting?

**March:**  
How do you preserve enough time to choose your own life?

Freedom and responsibility are no longer opposites.

They support each other.

### A Useful Comparison with 2021

In 2021, İşler will explain that he deliberately keeps his life small enough to avoid unwanted work.

That later philosophy is already practical here.

Even the need to finance a football club does not convince him to return to a production structure he believes is harmful.

### Why This Interview Matters

The January 2017 L'Officiel Hommes interview is an important bridge between Nejat İşler's post-illness philosophy and its practical consequences.

Its central correction is simple:

**the public rebel is also a responsible person.**

Gümüşlükspor proves it through daily obligation.

His mother provides a private moral measure.

His refusal to return to conventional series work shows that responsibility does not automatically mean accepting the most profitable option.

And his “bad marriage” metaphor gives one of the clearest explanations of why television repeatedly became intolerable:

the conditions you agree to at the beginning do not remain stable.

Perhaps the mature Nejat İşler position can be summarized this way:

**freedom is not escaping responsibility.**

**It is gaining enough control to choose which responsibilities deserve your life.**
