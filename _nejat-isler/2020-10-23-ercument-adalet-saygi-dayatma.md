## Ercüment Çözer: Adalet, Saygı ve Dayatma

**T24 — 23 October 2020**

Three days before *Saygı: Bir Ercüment Çözer Dizisi* reached audiences, Nejat İşler gave one of his clearest explanations of how he personally understood Ercüment Çözer.

This short record is valuable because it separates two things that audiences often merge:

**understanding a character**

and

**agreeing with a character.**

İşler understands Ercüment's logic.

He does not approve of it.

That distinction is central to his entire approach to morally dark roles.

### Ercüment Begins with Small Acts

İşler explains Ercüment's worldview through an escalation.

Large crimes do not appear from nowhere.

Everyday life contains smaller acts that people ignore:

disrespect,

humiliation,

aggression,

the violation of another person's boundaries.

Ercüment believes that society's larger disorders grow because these smaller behaviors are tolerated.

At first, this can sound reasonable.

That is exactly what makes the character dangerous.

### A Reasonable Observation Can Produce an Unreasonable System

Ercüment notices a real social problem.

People often ignore small acts of cruelty until they become larger.

But his solution is not democratic.

It is authoritarian.

He decides that he understands justice better than everyone else.

Then he gives himself permission to enforce it.

The character therefore illustrates an important moral problem:

**a correct diagnosis does not automatically justify the cure.**

### From Respect to Control

The title *Saygı* is built around respect.

But Ercüment's version of respect is unstable.

Respect normally requires recognizing another person's dignity.

Ercüment increasingly turns it into obedience.

If somebody refuses the behavior he expects, he feels justified in intervening.

Respect becomes something imposed.

Once that happens, it is no longer really respect.

It is control.

### İşler Draws the Boundary Clearly

This is where Nejat İşler separates himself from his character.

He says he cannot personally view Ercüment positively.

He specifically objects to the character looking down on other people and trying to impose his own beliefs.

This matters because Ercüment's charisma can easily hide his authoritarianism.

İşler refuses that romantic reading.

### "Nobody Can Impose Their Truth"

The strongest principle in İşler's explanation is simple.

Nobody has the right to impose their:

truth,

belief,

worldview

on someone else.

And certainly not through violence.

This is one of the clearest moral statements in the archive.

It also fits his wider philosophy of autonomy.

### Freedom Has to Work in Both Directions

For decades, İşler has defended his own right to:

leave,

refuse,

protect privacy,

control his time,

choose how to live.

But personal freedom only becomes a coherent principle if other people receive the same freedom.

Ercüment fails this test.

He wants autonomy for himself.

He does not grant equivalent autonomy to others.

### The Authoritarian Anti-Hero

This is why calling Ercüment simply a rebel is misleading.

A rebel resists imposed authority.

Ercüment also creates authority.

He does not merely reject rules.

He invents his own rules and punishes others for failing to obey them.

In this sense, he can become the very thing a genuine anti-authoritarian position should oppose.

### Why Audiences Still Like Him

Yet Ercüment became enormously popular.

That paradox had interested İşler for years.

The character offers a fantasy of immediate justice.

Someone is rude.

Ercüment acts.

Someone abuses power.

Ercüment punishes them.

Real institutions can feel:

slow,

weak,

compromised.

Ercüment is immediate.

That can be emotionally satisfying.

### The Fantasy of Perfect Enforcement

The problem is that Ercüment himself decides:

what is wrong,

who is guilty,

what punishment is deserved.

There is no meaningful external limit.

The fantasy of perfect justice quietly becomes the fantasy of unlimited personal power.

This is why the character becomes more politically interesting than a conventional villain.

### The Childhood Explanation

İşler says Ercüment's hard concept of justice grows partly from childhood experience.

This again demonstrates his long-running acting method.

Behavior needs history.

A person does not become Ercüment simply because a script labels him strange.

The actor needs to understand:

what formed him?

What did he learn?

What does he believe he is protecting?

### Explanation Without Moral Surrender

This point is easy to misunderstand.

Humanizing a violent character does not mean declaring the character innocent.

It means refusing lazy psychology.

The audience can understand how somebody arrived at a worldview and still reject the worldview.

That is one of the strongest principles connecting *Barda* and Ercüment.

### "Kötüyü Kötü Oynamıyorum"

The later Episode interview gives this method its famous compact form:

**“Kötüyü kötü oynamıyorum.”**

The 2020 statement shows what that means in practice.

İşler can explain:

Ercüment's childhood,

anger,

social diagnosis,

the logic of respect.

But after doing all of that, he still says:

I cannot personally support him.

Understanding makes judgment better informed.

It does not remove judgment.

### The Small Crime Theory

The idea that large social problems begin with ignored small behavior is especially important.

There is something persuasive in it.

A culture of violence does not begin only with spectacular crimes.

It may also depend on ordinary tolerance for:

bullying,

humiliation,

sexism,

aggressive language,

abuse of weaker people.

Ercüment's error is not necessarily seeing continuity between small and large wrongs.

His error is deciding that this gives him sovereign power.

### Self-Improvement Before Policing Others

İşler gives an alternative.

People who have meaningful work and a worldview, he argues, should first be concerned with improving themselves rather than interfering endlessly with others.

This is almost the ethical opposite of Ercüment.

Ercüment:

fix society by controlling others.

İşler's alternative:

begin with yourself.

### "Can Sıkıntısı"

İşler also connects many social disturbances with boredom — people who have little meaningful focus beginning to interfere with others.

The claim is broad, but it fits an older theme in his interviews.

A meaningful life requires:

work,

interests,

community,

stories,

football,

books,

art.

Without those things, energy can turn destructive.

### This Explains His Own Multiplicity

İşler's later life is full of activities beyond acting:

writing,

books,

football,

local organization,

producing.

That can now be read not only as career diversification but as a philosophy of occupation.

Have something to do.

Build something.

Improve something.

Do not spend life controlling strangers.

### A Useful Comparison with Barda

The continuity with 2007 is extremely strong.

**Barda:**  
How can an ordinary person become capable of violence?

**Ercüment:**  
How can a person turn dissatisfaction with society into a private system of violent justice?

The subject develops from violence itself toward ideology.

### A Useful Comparison with 2016

In 2016, İşler says he no longer wants to spend life only on himself.

His answer is constructive:

football,

teaching,

writing.

Ercüment also wants to change the world around him.

But he chooses coercion.

This creates an interesting contrast between two forms of dissatisfaction:

**contribution**

versus

**control.**

### A Useful Comparison with 2021

The 2021 Milliyet Sanat interview will deepen Ercüment's psychology.

İşler discusses respect, his own anger and the public attraction to the character.

But the ethical boundary has already been established here.

The actor understands him.

The citizen rejects his methods.

### A Useful Comparison with 2022

By 2022, İşler will say explicitly that audiences love bad characters.

This 2020 record helps explain why.

The attractive villain usually contains something emotionally recognizable.

Ercüment's frustration with:

rudeness,

injustice,

social irresponsibility

is understandable.

His response is unacceptable.

The audience may identify with the first part and enjoy the fantasy of the second.

### The Danger of the Anti-Hero

This is one of the central risks of anti-hero storytelling.

A work may show a character in order to question him.

The audience may instead celebrate him.

The more charismatic the performance, the greater the risk.

Ercüment is one of the clearest examples in İşler's career.

### Acting Requires Distance

A good actor cannot simply hate the character.

Hatred creates caricature.

But the actor also does not have to surrender personal judgment.

İşler demonstrates both forms of distance:

**professional closeness:**  
I understand why he behaves this way.

**moral distance:**  
I do not accept what he does.

This balance may be one of the strongest explanations of his success with dark characters.

## Why This Record Matters

The 23 October 2020 Ercüment Çözer statement is a short but important archive document because Nejat İşler clearly separates **character psychology from personal morality**.

Ercüment believes social decay begins with small tolerated wrongs.

That insight gives him psychological credibility.

But he then appoints himself judge and enforcer.

İşler rejects that move.

Nobody, in his view, has the right to impose personal truths on others through violence.

The statement therefore gives a clearer ethical foundation to an acting method visible since *Barda*:

**understand the bad character deeply enough to make him human, but do not confuse understanding with approval.**
