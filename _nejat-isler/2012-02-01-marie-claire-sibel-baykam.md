## Marie Claire: Public Image and the Man at Home

**Marie Claire Türkiye — February 2012**  
**Interview by Sibel Baykam**

By 2012, Nejat İşler had acquired a remarkably stable public image.

He was widely associated with:

- nightlife,
- alcohol,
- relationships,
- independence,
- rebellion,
- unconventional masculinity,
- emotionally distant characters.

The success of *Kaybedenler Kulübü* had strengthened this image further.

Kaan Çaydamlı and Nejat İşler were already beginning to merge in the public imagination.

Then, in an interview with Sibel Baykam for the February 2012 issue of *Marie Claire Türkiye*, İşler described himself in almost the opposite way.

His basic claim was simple:

**the person people think they know is not the person he experiences at home.**

### "I'm Not What I Look Like from Outside"

The strongest statement in the interview is also the simplest.

İşler says he is not really like the person people see from outside.

He describes himself as a **home-oriented man**.

This matters because it directly challenges the public mythology surrounding him.

The popular Nejat İşler image suggests constant movement:

bars,

women,

night,

friends,

city life,

restlessness.

The private description suggests:

home,

continuity,

fidelity,

peace,

emotional security.

These two pictures do not necessarily contradict each other completely.

A person can enjoy nightlife and also value domestic stability.

But celebrity culture usually removes such complexity.

It selects the more dramatic version.

For İşler, that dramatic version had become dominant.

### The Public Nejat and the Private Nejat

This is one of the clearest examples in the archive of a recurring problem.

There are at least two Nejats.

One is produced by:

- films,
- television roles,
- magazine photographs,
- nightlife reports,
- gossip columns,
- audience projection.

The other exists in his own description of ordinary life.

The first is theatrical.

The second is domestic.

This difference will continue for years.

In 2018, he will say that people attach a “delikanlı” identity to him even though he does not recognize himself in it.

In a later interview, he will make the point even more directly:

**“O adam ben değilim.”**

The man audiences think they know is not really him.

The 2012 Marie Claire interview is an early and unusually clear version of that argument.

### What Matters: Honesty and Peace

İşler tells Sibel Baykam that honesty and peace are extremely important to him.

These are not particularly glamorous values.

That is precisely why they are interesting.

The public persona around him is built on intensity.

His own domestic ideal sounds comparatively quiet.

He does not describe happiness through:

success,

wealth,

status,

public admiration.

He describes it through emotional conditions.

Trust.

Honesty.

Peace.

Closeness.

This already places him at some distance from conventional celebrity ambition.

### Success? He Is Not Sure What It Means

One of the most revealing questions concerns success.

İşler is asked whether success requires selling one's soul.

His response begins from a more basic problem:

**he is not sure what success actually means.**

That answer fits very well with the Esquire interview from the previous year.

In 2011, he said that he had never felt a strong hunger for many things.

Now, in 2012, even the concept of success itself appears unstable.

What exactly would count as success?

More fame?

More money?

More lead roles?

More awards?

More recognition?

İşler does not seem convinced that these measurements are self-evidently meaningful.

This skepticism will become increasingly important later.

### From Ambition to Enough

Across these early interviews, a pattern is forming.

**2010:** he wants distance from the professional market.

**2011:** he says he has little hunger for many conventional goals.

**2012:** he questions what success even means.

These are not yet a complete philosophy.

But by 2017 they will become one.

His later idea of using money to “buy time” depends on a prior conclusion:

**enough can be more valuable than more.**

The 2012 interview belongs to the period when this idea is still emotional rather than fully articulated.

### Relationships and Fidelity

The interview received considerable media attention because İşler spoke openly about relationships.

Contemporary coverage summarized him as saying that he was a loyal, home-oriented man who wanted to love and be loved.

This again clashes with the image surrounding him after *Kaybedenler Kulübü*.

Kaan Çaydamlı's cultural mythology is closely associated with non-traditional relationships and emotional distance.

Nejat İşler's own description is considerably more conventional.

He talks about companionship.

He talks about stability.

He talks about fidelity.

This difference is useful because it reminds us not to interpret İşler through Kaan.

The role amplified an existing public persona, but it did not become a reliable biography.

### "The Right Woman"

İşler also discusses what makes a relationship workable.

Rather than presenting an idealized romantic theory, he emphasizes compatibility.

The right partner, in this framing, is someone with whom daily life can actually function.

This is another example of his tendency to remove glamour from subjects usually discussed romantically.

Love is not only intensity.

It is also coexistence.

This indirectly connects with one of the questions raised in *Barda*:

**Can people actually live together?**

Obviously the contexts are radically different, but the concern with coexistence appears repeatedly.

### Domesticity as a Counter-Image

The phrase “home man” is particularly interesting because celebrity journalism normally treats domesticity as less newsworthy than rebellion.

The rebellious Nejat sells magazines.

The man who wants peace at home is less dramatic.

This creates an asymmetry.

Public images are not simply false.

They are selectively constructed.

The dramatic traits remain visible.

The ordinary traits disappear.

Over time, the surviving public identity can begin to look more coherent than the person ever was.

The Marie Claire interview interrupts that process.

### Keşanlı Ali and Another Masculine Role

At the time, İşler was appearing as Ali in *Keşanlı Ali Destanı*.

Once again, he was playing a strongly coded masculine figure.

This means that the distinction between actor and persona becomes especially relevant.

On screen, he is repeatedly placed inside recognizable forms of masculinity.

Off screen, he keeps complicating them.

This will later become one of the archive's clearest themes:

**Nejat İşler repeatedly performs masculine archetypes while resisting being reduced to them.**

### Public Masculinity

By this point, the ingredients of the public Nejat İşler persona are becoming clear:

- physical confidence,
- emotional distance,
- rebellion,
- women,
- nightlife,
- drinking,
- toughness,
- resistance to authority.

But the interviews repeatedly add elements that do not fit easily:

- desire for companionship,
- discomfort with fame,
- domesticity,
- limited ambition,
- uncertainty about success,
- preference for peace.

The result is more interesting than either stereotype.

He is neither simply the rebellious outsider nor secretly a conventional domestic man.

He moves between both.

### Why Audiences Prefer the Harder Version

There is a cultural reason the harder version became dominant.

A rebellious male celebrity is narratively useful.

He can represent:

freedom,

refusal,

masculine independence,

resistance to social expectations.

A man who says he enjoys being at home is less useful as a symbol.

So audiences and media tend to preserve the version that carries more mythology.

This is partly why İşler repeatedly has to explain that the public version is incomplete.

### A Useful Comparison with 2018

The 2018 *Kaybedenler Kulübü Yolda* interviews make this continuity especially clear.

By then, İşler explicitly rejects the “delikanlı” identity attached to him.

The logic is almost identical to 2012.

2012:

**I am not really the person I look like from outside.**

2018:

**The masculine identity people attach to me does not really fit.**

Six years apart, the same problem remains.

### A Useful Comparison with Later Gümüşlük Interviews

The Marie Claire interview also gives us a useful baseline before Gümüşlük becomes central to his life.

In 2012, “home” is primarily an emotional idea.

Later, home becomes geographic.

Gümüşlük.

Distance from Istanbul.

Fewer obligations.

Less ownership.

More time.

But the underlying desire is similar.

A quieter zone protected from public demands.

In that sense, the later geographical withdrawal may have emotional roots that appear much earlier.

### Private Happiness Versus Public Success

Perhaps the deepest contrast in the interview is between two systems of value.

Public culture asks:

**Are you successful?**

İşler seems uncertain that the category matters.

Private life asks:

**Are you peaceful?**

That question appears much easier for him to answer.

This is an important distinction because later interviews will increasingly organize life around quality of time rather than career accumulation.

The 2012 interview shows that the emotional basis for that choice already exists.

## Why This Interview Matters

The February 2012 Marie Claire interview is important because it provides one of the clearest early challenges to the public mythology surrounding Nejat İşler.

After *Barda* and especially *Kaybedenler Kulübü*, he was increasingly imagined as a rebellious, emotionally independent and sexually confident masculine outsider.

His own description is much less cinematic.

He says he is home-oriented.

He values honesty and peace.

He describes fidelity as important.

And when asked about success, he questions what the concept even means.

This gap between **public Nejat** and **private Nejat** will remain central throughout the archive.

The roles change.

The public labels change.

But İşler repeatedly returns to the same argument:

**the person constructed from roles, photographs and headlines is not the whole person.**
