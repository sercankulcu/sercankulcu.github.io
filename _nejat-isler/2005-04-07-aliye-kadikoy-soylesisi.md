## Aliye ve Aile Üzerine Kadıköy Söyleşisi

**Kadıköy — 7 April 2005**

This 2005 event is not a conventional Nejat İşler interview.

It is valuable for a different reason.

During the period when *Aliye* was one of Turkey's most visible television dramas, İşler appeared at a public panel in Kadıköy together with members of the series and participated in a discussion about family life, relationships and the difference between television drama and everyday reality. Contemporary Kadıköy reporting records the event for **7 April 2005** and places İşler alongside other *Aliye* performers in a discussion moderated by psychiatrist Prof. Dr. Özcan Köknel.

It therefore captures something missing from the surrounding archive:

Nejat İşler at the height of mainstream television visibility, before *Barda*, before Kaan Çaydamlı, before Ercüment Çözer, and long before his later rejection of intensive television schedules.

### The Aliye Period

*Aliye* placed İşler inside a very different public image from the one that would later dominate his career.

He played Deniz.

The role belonged to a highly popular family drama.

The series dealt with:

marriage,

betrayal,

children,

divorce,

love,

family conflict.

This was mainstream prime-time television rather than the darker cinema and anti-hero roles that later became strongly associated with him.

### Before the Dark Persona

This is why the record is useful.

Looking backward from *Barda*, *Kaybedenler Kulübü* or *Behzat Ç.*, it can seem as if Nejat İşler had always occupied the position of the unconventional dark male figure.

He had not.

In the mid-2000s, a large television audience also knew him through romantic and dramatic roles inside conventional family narratives.

That earlier public identity should not disappear from the archive.

### Television as Public Conversation

The Kadıköy event also reflects an older television culture.

A successful television drama could become the starting point for a public panel about actual social questions.

The actors were not simply promoting the next episode.

They were placed beside an academic moderator and invited to discuss the real-life subjects dramatized by the series.

That gives the event a different character from modern promotional interviews.

The boundary between:

entertainment,

social discussion,

and public education

was deliberately blurred.

### Fiction and Real Families

The archived report says the discussion moved between the family structure shown in the series and family life in reality.

That distinction is important.

Popular drama simplifies.

Real relationships do not.

Characters need narrative functions.

Real people contain contradictions that may never reach resolution.

This tension later becomes extremely important in İşler's acting philosophy.

Do not reduce people to labels.

Do not play evil simply as evil.

Do not confuse the visible role with the whole person.

The 2005 panel does not yet articulate that later philosophy, but it sits at an interesting early point in the same territory.

### The Actor and the Character

At this stage, viewers were increasingly familiar with İşler through television characters.

This produces one of the basic problems of celebrity acting:

audiences know the face,

but they know it through fiction.

The actor gradually becomes associated with the emotional qualities of the role.

For İşler, this pattern would become stronger throughout his career.

Deniz.

Kaan.

Ercüment.

Each produces another public Nejat.

### A Mainstream Star Before the Outsider Myth

This matters especially when compared with his later image.

The later public mythology tends to emphasize:

rebellion,

nightlife,

independence,

darkness,

distance from mainstream culture.

Yet *Aliye* demonstrates that İşler was also deeply inside mainstream popular culture.

He was not an outsider who never belonged to the center.

He had already occupied the center.

His later distance from it was therefore a choice made by someone who knew what mass visibility felt like.

### Popularity Before Rejection of Popularity

This makes the 2010 interview more meaningful.

By then, İşler was openly complaining about:

television schedules,

public intrusion,

celebrity culture,

loss of privacy.

The *Aliye* period shows the environment that helped create that problem.

Before someone can become tired of mass television visibility, they generally have to experience it.

### The Missing Middle Between 2003 and 2007

The archive currently moves from:

**2003 — Gülbeyaz and sudden stardom**

to

**2007 — Barda and violence**

This 2005 record helps fill the gap.

It shows that the transition was not immediate.

Between the early leading-man phase and the darker cinema phase was a period of major mainstream television success.

That intermediate stage is important.

### Public Responsibility Without "Role Model"

The event also foreshadows a later tension.

Actors are invited to discuss social questions because visibility gives them public authority.

But İşler would later explicitly resist being called a role model.

That creates an important distinction.

A public figure can participate in public discussion without claiming moral superiority.

The 2005 event shows the public expectation.

The 2016 Gümüşlükspor interview shows İşler later placing limits around that expectation.

### Television Was Once More Than a Labor Problem

Later archive entries often approach television through exhausting working conditions.

This earlier record reminds us that television also gave İşler:

large audiences,

important roles,

professional opportunities,

and entry into public conversations beyond acting.

His relationship with television therefore should not be simplified into rejection.

The medium gave him enormous visibility before its industrial structure became something he increasingly wanted to escape.

### A Useful Comparison with 2003

The 2003 Milliyet interview catches İşler just as the “leading man” label is beginning to form.

By 2005, that visibility has become established.

He is no longer simply a promising actor receiving sudden attention.

He is part of one of television's recognizable ensembles.

The transition from actor to celebrity is already underway.

### A Useful Comparison with 2007

Two years later, *Barda* will radically change the moral atmosphere around his screen image.

The distance is striking.

**Aliye:**

family,

love,

relationships,

mainstream television.

**Barda:**

violence,

justice,

male brutality,

social collapse.

This range is important when evaluating the later idea that İşler is naturally a “dark actor.”

Darkness became one of his strongest territories.

It was not his only one.

### A Useful Comparison with 2010

By 2010, mainstream television has become something he wants to distance himself from.

That later position is easier to understand if we preserve records such as this one.

He had spent years close to the center of the system.

His rejection came from experience inside it.

Not ignorance from outside.

## Why This Record Matters

The 7 April 2005 Kadıköy panel is a minor archival record compared with the major long-form interviews.

But it fills an important chronological gap.

It captures Nejat İşler during the *Aliye* era, when he was part of mainstream prime-time television and before the darker anti-hero identity became culturally dominant.

It also demonstrates how quickly successful fictional roles can turn actors into participants in wider public conversations.

Most importantly, it prevents the archive from creating a false straight line:

**early actor → dark outsider.**

There was an important period in between:

**mainstream television star.**

Understanding that phase makes his later withdrawal from the television industry much more meaningful.
