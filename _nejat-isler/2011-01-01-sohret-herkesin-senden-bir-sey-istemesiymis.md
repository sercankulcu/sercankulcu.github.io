## Şöhret, Herkesin Senden Bir Şey İstemesiymiş

**Ters Ninja — 2011**  
**Interview by Ege Görgün**

One of the most persistent ideas in Nejat İşler's public image is that he dislikes interviews.

The simple version says:

he is difficult.

He does not like journalists.

He does not want to talk.

The 2011 Ters Ninja conversation gives a much better explanation.

İşler does not reject conversation itself.

What tires him is repetition.

A film comes out.

Promotion begins.

The same questions arrive.

The actor gives versions of the same answers again and again.

Eventually, the process becomes automatic.

That is where the problem begins.

### The Interview Becomes a Performance

İşler explains that promotional interviews can become routine because journalists often ask similar questions.

The answer is therefore no longer created naturally in the moment.

It has already been given before.

The actor repeats it.

Again.

And again.

This is important because the interview itself begins to resemble acting.

There is now:

the film role

and

the promotional role.

The second role is called:

**Nejat İşler talking about Nejat İşler.**

### A Celebrity Must Continuously Produce Himself

Acting produces characters.

Celebrity produces another obligation:

the actor has to continually explain his own identity.

What do you think?

What are you like?

Why did you do this?

Who are you dating?

Why are you angry?

What does success mean to you?

The public figure becomes responsible not only for work but for maintaining a readable public self.

İşler clearly finds this tiring.

### Fame Means Demand

The interview's most memorable idea concerns what fame actually feels like.

For İşler, fame is less about admiration than demand.

Once somebody becomes recognizable, people begin wanting things from them.

Attention.

Photographs.

Answers.

Access.

Favors.

Presence.

Statements.

The title captures his discovery:

**fame means everybody wants something from you.**

### Celebrity as Extraction

This is a useful way to understand his later conflicts with media.

Public culture usually presents fame as something received.

The celebrity receives:

money,

attention,

admiration,

privilege.

İşler emphasizes the opposite flow.

The celebrity is also constantly giving:

time,

attention,

image,

access.

Fame becomes a system of small extractions.

### The Price Is Time Again

This connects directly with one of the oldest themes in the archive.

Time.

Every photograph takes a little time.

Every interview takes time.

Every unwanted public conversation takes time.

Individually, these demands are small.

Collectively, they change daily life.

This helps explain why his later desire to “buy time” is connected not only with working schedules but also with fame.

### The Problem Is Not One Person

A fan asking for a photograph is not necessarily doing anything unreasonable.

A journalist requesting an interview is doing a job.

The problem emerges through scale.

One request is manageable.

Thousands of requests create another reality.

Fame transforms ordinary social behavior through accumulation.

### The Public Thinks It Knows Him

Recognition also creates a psychological asymmetry.

The stranger knows:

his face,

roles,

interviews,

possibly stories about his private life.

İşler knows nothing about the stranger.

Yet the interaction can begin as if an existing relationship already exists.

This imbalance is one reason celebrity can feel invasive even when people are friendly.

### The Observer Becomes Visible

This connects with the 2007 Ulusoy interview.

İşler had said that one of his pleasures was observing people.

Fame damaged that ability.

People behaved differently after recognizing him.

The observer became the observed.

The Ters Ninja conversation reveals another consequence:

the observed person is then expected to explain himself continuously.

### Watching People Is Part of the Profession

This is especially costly for an actor.

Ordinary observation supplies material.

How does someone sit?

Pause?

Lie?

Become embarrassed?

Become angry?

Actors collect these details.

Celebrity interferes with the natural environment from which those observations come.

### The Paradox of Successful Acting

The paradox becomes almost perfect.

Acting well creates fame.

Fame makes ordinary observation harder.

Ordinary observation helps create good acting.

Professional success damages one of the tools that helped produce it.

### He Does Not Hate Interviews

This distinction is worth preserving because İşler has participated in many substantial interviews across decades.

Milliyet.

Cumhuriyet.

Esquire.

Marie Claire.

OT.

Socrates.

Habertürk.

Episode.

Oksijen.

MediaCat.

Mirgün Cabas.

A man who literally rejected all interviews would not leave such a large interview archive.

What he rejects is closer to:

**compulsory repetition.**

### A Good Conversation Is Different

The strongest interviews in the archive usually have one thing in common.

They move away from standard celebrity questions.

They ask about:

work,

memory,

books,

football,

mortality,

acting,

society,

time.

Then İşler becomes much more expansive.

This suggests that his resistance is partly about the quality and purpose of the interaction.

### Interviews Can Flatten a Person

Repeated questions generate repeated answers.

Repeated answers produce a stable public identity.

Then that identity produces more repeated questions.

A feedback loop develops.

For example:

Nejat is rebellious.

Ask about rebellion.

Publish rebellious answer.

Use answer as evidence that Nejat is rebellious.

Ask about rebellion again.

The public persona becomes self-reinforcing.

### "Difficult Nejat" Can Be Manufactured

This mechanism matters when considering his reputation for being difficult.

If journalists continually approach someone through an expectation of hostility, even ordinary irritation can confirm the stereotype.

Eventually, both sides begin acting inside the story already written about them.

### Kaybedenler Kulübü Arrives at Exactly the Right Moment

The interview belongs to 2011, the year *Kaybedenler Kulübü* dramatically expanded the Nejat İşler mythology.

Kaan Çaydamlı provided audiences with a ready-made identity:

cool,

independent,

cynical,

sexually free,

slightly tired,

resistant to conventional society.

That character arrived when the real actor was already frustrated with being simplified.

### A Perfect Storm of Persona

Three things now reinforce one another:

**the actor's existing reputation**

+

**Kaan's fictional personality**

+

**promotional interviews about Kaan**

The result is a powerful cultural fusion.

Nejat begins to look like Kaan.

Kaan begins to look like Nejat.

### The Actor Knows the Difference

Yet İşler repeatedly keeps some distance from the identification.

He can recognize similarities.

Shared cultural references.

1990s experience.

Attitudes toward success.

But similarity is not identity.

The problem is that public culture prefers identity.

It is easier.

### Fame as Loss of Ambiguity

Before fame, a person can be contradictory without explanation.

Quiet today.

Social tomorrow.

Disciplined at work.

Messy at home.

Tender with friends.

Harsh with strangers.

Celebrity encourages consistency.

The audience wants a recognizable character.

İşler repeatedly resists that demand.

### A Useful Comparison with 2003

In 2003, the industry begins calling him a leading man.

He responds by insisting on the simpler identity:

actor.

By 2011, the problem has grown.

Now the audience does not merely assign a professional category.

It begins assigning a personality.

### A Useful Comparison with 2012

The Marie Claire interview makes the distinction explicit.

The person at home is not the person imagined from outside.

This Ters Ninja conversation explains part of how that gap developed.

Celebrity repeatedly asks the public person to reproduce himself.

The private person has no equivalent obligation.

### A Useful Comparison with 2021

Ten years later, İşler will tell Asu Maro something even more revealing:

sometimes he intentionally performs the rough Nejat İşler that people expect.

By then the process has reached another stage.

**2011:**  
The public persona becomes repetitive.

**2021:**  
He sometimes consciously plays it.

The celebrity identity has become an actual role.

### A Useful Comparison with 2025

His later conflicts over reporters, cameras and behind-the-scenes footage should also be read in this context.

They are not isolated explosions.

They belong to a twenty-year dispute about access.

When does professional visibility become a claim over the whole person?

İşler's answer has remained largely consistent:

it should not.

### Fame and Freedom

This also connects celebrity directly with his broader philosophy of autonomy.

Freedom does not only mean:

having enough money to refuse a television series.

It also means maintaining some areas of life where the public has no automatic claim.

Privacy is therefore another form of ownership over time and self.

### The Most Expensive Part of Fame

The real cost may not be being recognized.

It may be becoming permanently available.

Available to:

camera,

question,

judgment,

interpretation.

İşler repeatedly tries to withdraw that availability.

### The Archive Itself Creates an Irony

There is an obvious irony in building a large interview archive around someone who dislikes repetitive interviews.

But that is exactly why chronology matters.

Taken individually, interviews repeat themselves.

Taken across twenty years, the repetitions become evidence.

We begin seeing which ideas survive.

Which change.

Which become clearer.

The archive does not need to reproduce the promotional cycle.

It can reveal the pattern underneath it.

## Why This Interview Matters

The 2011 Ters Ninja conversation is valuable because it explains Nejat İşler's difficult relationship with interviews without reducing him to a man who simply dislikes journalists.

His problem is repetition.

Promotion turns conversation into routine.

Routine turns identity into performance.

And fame creates a permanent flow of requests from people who feel some degree of access to the public person.

The interview therefore adds an important concept to the archive:

**celebrity is not only visibility. It is availability.**

İşler's long struggle with public image can be understood partly as an attempt to limit that availability.

The actor can be public.

The entire person does not have to be.
