A perfectly ordinary pencil can become fascinating the moment production stops.

Discontinuation changes the psychology of use. When replacements were easy to buy, sharpening felt trivial. Once the remaining supply is finite, every centimeter suddenly appears valuable.

Collectors respond in predictable ways. Some stockpile boxes. Some search for older variants. Some begin comparing replacement models, hoping to find the same writing feel under a new name. Prices may rise even when the pencil itself has not changed.

This is where documentation matters. Rumors about discontinued stationery spread quickly. Packaging transitions, regional availability, temporary shortages, and true end-of-production events can look similar from a distance.

I try not to let scarcity become the only reason I value a pencil. A discontinued model is most interesting when there is a story: unusual graphite, distinctive design, historical importance, or a manufacturing change that cannot be reproduced exactly.

The last usable pencil in a box creates a strange question: preserve it or sharpen it? That tension is almost a definition of pencil collecting.
