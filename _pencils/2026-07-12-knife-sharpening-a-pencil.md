Sharpening a pencil with a knife looks old-fashioned, but it offers control that a conventional sharpener cannot match.

The basic advantage is geometry. I can decide how much wood to remove and how much graphite to expose. For drawing, a long section of exposed core makes it possible to use both the point and the side. For writing, I can leave more wood near the core for strength.

The method should be slow and controlled. I cut away from the hand, take thin slices, rotate the pencil, and avoid levering against the core. The goal is not to carve deeply; it is to gradually reveal the graphite.

Once exposed, the core can be shaped with fine abrasive paper if an especially long needle point or flat drawing edge is desired. That separates the jobs: the knife shapes the wood, and the abrasive shapes the graphite.

Knife sharpening also teaches a collector about construction. Grain direction, wood quality, glue, and core centering are obvious when the barrel is opened gradually rather than hidden inside a sharpener.

It is not the fastest method for classroom notes, and it demands proper care with the blade. But for drawing and close inspection, it turns sharpening into a precise part of pencil use rather than a routine interruption.
