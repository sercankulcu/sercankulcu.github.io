A pencil cannot be separated from the way it is sharpened. Change the sharpener and the same pencil can feel like a different tool.

A short, steep point exposes less graphite and leaves more wood close to the tip. It can feel strong and controlled. A long point exposes more core, which can be excellent for drawing and broad side strokes but may be more vulnerable to breakage.

Blade sharpness matters enormously. A dull blade tears wood instead of slicing it. It increases force, creates rough surfaces, and can put unnecessary stress on the core. Replacing a blade can make a supposedly bad pencil suddenly sharpen beautifully.

Core centering becomes obvious during sharpening. With a well-centered core, the wood recedes evenly around the graphite. An off-center core produces an asymmetric point and may expose graphite prematurely on one side.

For consistent pencil reviews, I try to use the same sharpener or at least the same point geometry. Otherwise I may accidentally be comparing sharpeners instead of pencils.

Collectors often focus on pencils and treat the sharpener as an accessory. In actual use, it is part of the system. Paper, pencil, and sharpener interact—and a good point begins with a good cut.
