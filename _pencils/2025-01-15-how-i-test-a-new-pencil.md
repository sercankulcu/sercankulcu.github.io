When a new pencil enters my collection, I try not to judge it from a single scribble. A first impression is useful, but it is also easy to distort. A freshly sharpened point feels different from a worn point, smooth paper can hide weaknesses that rough paper exposes, and one unusually good sample can make an inconsistent product look better than it really is.

A useful pencil test does not need laboratory equipment. It needs repeatable conditions.

The first rule is to control what can be controlled. I use the same papers, the same sharpeners, similar point geometry, and roughly the same writing pressure whenever I compare pencils. The goal is not to eliminate subjectivity. The goal is to make the subjective observations more meaningful.

I begin before sharpening.

The unsharpened pencil already reveals a great deal about manufacturing. I inspect barrel straightness, lacquer coverage, imprint sharpness, end treatment, ferrule alignment if present, eraser condition, and whether the core appears visibly centered at the cut end.

Barrel geometry matters too. On a hexagonal pencil, I check whether the flats appear even and whether the corners are sharp, rounded, or inconsistent. On round or triangular pencils, I look for distortion or asymmetry.

The finish should be examined under angled light. Glossy lacquer can hide runs and thin spots when viewed straight on. Rotating the pencil reveals uneven coating, dust inclusions, scratches, foil defects, and surface waviness.

I record the imprint exactly when the pencil is vintage or potentially interesting as a variant. Manufacturer wording, model number, grade, country marking, trademark symbols, and typography can matter later even if they do not affect writing performance.

Then I photograph the pencil before use.

An unsharpened photograph preserves factory condition. Once the sharpener removes the end, that state cannot be reconstructed.

The first sharpening is one of the most informative parts of the test.

I normally use a known sharpener with a fresh or well-maintained blade. If I am comparing several pencils, I use the same sharpener for all of them.

This controls point geometry.

A long-point sharpener and a compact school sharpener can make the same pencil feel very different. If point angle changes between samples, the writing comparison becomes less useful.

While sharpening, I pay attention to force.

Good pencil wood should cut without requiring excessive pressure. Clean, continuous shavings suggest straight grain and a sharp blade.

Tearing, splintering, or abrupt resistance deserves attention.

But I do not immediately blame the pencil. A dull blade can make excellent wood look poor.

This is why I keep the sharpener itself under control.

The sharpened cone exposes the core.

I check centering, diameter, bonding, and whether the graphite appears smooth or fractured.

An off-center core creates uneven wood thickness around the point.

A visibly wandering core can reveal alignment problems that were impossible to see before sharpening.

The wood itself is worth noting.

Color, grain fineness, smell, and how cleanly it cuts all contribute to the experience.

I avoid identifying wood species from appearance alone unless the manufacturer documents it.

The next step is a normal writing sample.

I do not begin with a dark swatch because most pencils are not used as swatch-making tools.

I write several sentences at my usual speed.

This reveals point wear, rotation behavior, friction, sound, line width, and whether the pencil becomes more comfortable after the initial needle point rounds.

I record darkness separately from smoothness.

A pencil can be dark but toothy.

It can also be smooth but relatively pale.

Combining both into one vague impression makes comparisons less useful.

Feedback deserves its own category.

A pencil with controlled tactile resistance can feel precise without feeling scratchy.

Unexpected gritty spots are different.

Those usually indicate irregularity in the core.

I also note audible feedback.

Harder pencils often produce more sound.

Very soft pencils can be nearly silent.

The sound itself is not a quality score, but it helps describe friction.

Point retention is measured over actual use rather than guessed from grade.

After the writing sample, I compare the tip to its original geometry.

Has it flattened rapidly?

Does it still hold a useful edge?

Did I need to rotate the pencil constantly?

A dark soft pencil may feel excellent but require frequent sharpening.

A firmer pencil may be less dramatic but remain useful much longer.

That tradeoff is central to performance.

Next I make controlled lines.

I draw several parallel strokes at light, medium, and heavy pressure.

This reveals pressure sensitivity and whether darkness increases gradually or abruptly.

A well-behaved graphite pencil should usually offer a predictable transition.

Then I make a pressure gradient.

Starting lightly, I increase force along one continuous stroke or shaded strip.

This helps reveal the usable tonal range.

Soft grades should deepen readily.

Hard grades should remain lighter and more controlled.

The important question is whether the transition is smooth.

I then make a small shaded square.

This tests broad deposition and side-of-point behavior.

It also reveals graphite sheen.

After building a dark area, I tilt the paper under a lamp.

Some pencils produce strong metallic reflection when heavily layered.

Others remain visually darker at oblique angles.

Graphite shine is not automatically bad, but it matters for drawing.

I test smudging after a consistent delay.

A fresh mark is more vulnerable than one that has sat for several minutes.

I use the same waiting time when comparing pencils.

Then I rub the mark lightly with clean paper or a finger using similar pressure.

The amount of transfer becomes a useful practical measure.

For notebooks, smudging may matter more than maximum darkness.

For drawing, easy blending may be an advantage.

Erasing is tested separately.

I erase a normal writing line and part of a shaded patch.

A clean erasure from light pressure is useful.

Heavy-pressure lines often leave physical indentations even after the graphite is removed.

That is a paper effect as much as a pencil effect.

I therefore avoid blaming the core for grooves created by excessive pressure.

Paper is one of the largest variables in pencil testing.

I never rely on one sheet alone.

At minimum, I use one ordinary notebook paper and one smoother or toothier contrasting paper.

The same pencil can behave differently enough to change a review completely.

On rough paper, graphite deposits faster and point wear increases.

On smooth paper, the pencil may feel more refined and retain a point longer.

A complete review should mention the surface.

I also check performance on paper with a different color if that matters to the intended use.

Hard grades can look much paler on cream paper than on bright white stock.

Contrast changes perception.

The second sharpening is important.

A pencil that sharpens perfectly once may contain hidden fractures farther down the core.

Repeated sharpening can reveal internal damage, wandering cores, or inconsistent wood.

If the second point behaves differently from the first, I record that.

I also compare how much pencil length was consumed.

Some sharpeners remove more material than others, but if the setup is constant, unusually rapid loss may indicate fragile graphite or repeated breakage.

For eraser-tipped pencils, I test the eraser as part of the object.

A pencil with excellent graphite and a useless attached eraser is still an internally inconsistent writing tool.

I check how cleanly the eraser removes the pencil's own mark, whether it smears, how abrasive it feels, and whether the ferrule remains secure.

Ferrule stability is easy to ignore until repeated erasing makes it rotate or loosen.

For drawing pencils without attached erasers, I focus more on tonal control, layering, point retention, and side shading.

The weighting of the test depends on the intended role.

A school pencil should be judged differently from a 6B artist pencil.

A hard drafting pencil should not lose points because it is less dark than a 2B.

Purpose has to come before ranking.

I sometimes compare three or four pencils at once.

This is more useful than testing isolated products weeks apart.

Memory is poor at fine sensory distinctions.

Side-by-side testing makes differences in darkness, feedback, wear, and sharpening immediately visible.

Blind testing can be useful too.

If brand reputation strongly affects expectations, covering the imprint and comparing numbered pencils can reveal bias.

This does not remove subjectivity, but it can expose how much branding influences perception.

Sample size matters.

One pencil cannot prove manufacturing consistency.

If a box is important enough, I sharpen several examples.

I compare centering, wood, lacquer, smoothness, and darkness across samples.

A premium product should usually show low variation.

Vintage pencils require additional caution.

Age and storage may have changed erasers, lacquer, wood moisture, adhesives, or core behavior.

One old pencil should not automatically define how the model performed when new.

For rare examples, destructive testing should be limited.

I may preserve one pencil unsharpened and use a duplicate for performance testing.

The catalog should record which specimen was used.

This prevents later confusion.

A practical review sheet can include these fields: appearance, centering, sharpening, wood quality, darkness, smoothness, feedback, point retention, smudging, erasing, core strength, barrel comfort, and overall consistency.

For drawing pencils I add tonal range, layering, sheen, and side-of-point performance.

For school pencils I emphasize durability and eraser quality.

For collector pieces I add imprint, packaging, variant evidence, and condition.

I avoid collapsing everything into one numerical score.

A single score hides tradeoffs.

A pencil can be excellent for fast note-taking and mediocre for detailed drawing.

Another can be brilliant for shading and annoying in a pocket notebook.

The profile matters more than the total.

I do sometimes use relative ratings inside categories.

For example, smoothness 4/5 and point retention 2/5 can communicate a useful contrast.

But the written description remains more important.

“Dark, low-friction HB with fast point wear and moderate smudging” is more informative than “8.5/10.”

The final step is delayed judgment.

I use the pencil again after the first test.

A product that seems impressive for five minutes may become tiring over a full page.

A pencil that initially feels plain may reveal excellent consistency after repeated sharpening.

Longer use often changes the ranking.

This is especially true for barrel comfort.

Sharp hexagonal edges may become noticeable only after extended writing.

A slippery round finish may require more grip force over time.

Short tests miss those effects.

I keep the test routine simple enough that I will actually repeat it.

A perfect protocol that is too complicated to use is worthless.

The most important parts are consistency, multiple papers, repeated sharpening, normal writing, controlled marks, and separate categories.

The purpose is not to transform pencil collecting into laboratory metrology.

It is to replace vague impressions with observations that can be compared later.

Instead of writing “this pencil is nice,” I want to know why.

Was it dark?

Was it smooth?

Did it keep a point?

Did the wood sharpen cleanly?

Did the core stay centered?

Did it smear?

Did it erase?

Did it behave the same on the second sharpening?

Once those questions are recorded consistently, the collection becomes much more interesting.

The pencils stop being isolated favorites and begin to form a comparative system.
