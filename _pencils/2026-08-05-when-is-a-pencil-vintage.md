Collectors use the word "vintage" freely, but the boundary is rarely precise.

Age is part of it, yet a twenty-year-old pencil and a seventy-year-old pencil do not carry the same historical weight. Discontinued manufacture, obsolete branding, old packaging, factory changes, and cultural context can make a relatively recent pencil feel historically significant.

I prefer descriptive dates when possible. "1990s production" is more useful than simply "vintage." If the date is uncertain, I record the evidence: logo style, company address, country marking, packaging, or catalog appearance.

The term should also not be confused with rarity. Millions of pencils from an earlier era may still exist, while a recent limited run can be difficult to find. Old is not automatically rare, and rare is not automatically valuable.

Condition adds another dimension. An unused pencil in its original dozen box may be desirable because it documents retail history. A worn advertising pencil may be more interesting because the company or event printed on it has disappeared.

For me, "vintage" is a convenient doorway, not a scientific classification. The real work begins after the label: determining when, where, why, and for whom the pencil was made.
