A pencil collection can become geographically distorted very quickly. Germany, Japan, Switzerland, and the United States have famous models that are easy to document and easy to buy. India deserves a place beside them, and the Nataraj 621 is a good point of entry.

Hindustan Pencils was founded in Mumbai in 1958. The company identifies Nataraj as its first brand and says it was created around a simple idea: pencils for everyone. That positioning matters. Nataraj was not conceived as a luxury drawing line. It was built for the huge everyday market of school, home, and office writing.

The scale eventually became enormous. Hindustan Pencils describes itself as India’s largest pencil manufacturer and exports to more than fifty countries. A recent company catalogue states that twelve factories across five locations produce about 9.6 million pencils per day, alongside erasers, sharpeners, rulers, and pens.

The 621 belongs to that industrial world.

It is not interesting because it uses an exotic core or an unusual shape. Its importance comes from repetition: millions of inexpensive pencils made consistently enough to become ordinary objects in classrooms and pencil boxes.

That kind of pencil is easy for collectors to underestimate.

A premium artist pencil advertises itself through elaborate packaging, long grade ranges, and technical claims. A school pencil has a harder job. It must be inexpensive, sharpen reliably, survive transport, make a clear line, erase reasonably well, and remain recognizable in a crowded stationery market.

The Nataraj 621 family is built around HB writing.

Current international catalogues list 621 variants such as Pearl and Jade as universal HB pencils for writing, drawing, and sketching. The pencils use a hexagonal body and a striped visual treatment that makes them easy to identify in a school-supply display.

Earlier 621 catalogues emphasize a bonded HB lead, clear writing, easy sharpening, and easy erasing. None of those features sounds dramatic until they are considered as a manufacturing specification.

Bonding matters because the graphite core is the most fragile part of a wooden pencil. If the core separates from the surrounding wood or fractures internally, sharpening exposes broken sections again and again. A school pencil that is frequently dropped needs the core and casing to behave as one structure.

The hexagonal body solves another everyday problem. It resists rolling off a desk while providing broad surfaces for printing and branding. In a classroom, that is more useful than a perfectly round barrel.

HB is equally practical. It sits near the middle of the graphite scale: dark enough for ordinary writing, firm enough to retain a useful point, and generally easier to erase than softer drawing grades.

The result is a pencil optimized around routine rather than spectacle.

Hindustan Pencils also gives useful clues about how a mass-market pencil reaches such scale. The company describes itself as highly integrated, controlling the manufacture of many components used in its stationery products. Its current catalogue also states that it uses agroforestry wood rather than wood taken from natural forests.

That detail is important because pencil production is fundamentally a materials business.

Millions of pencils require a dependable supply of straight-grained wood, graphite mixtures, coatings, adhesives, packaging, and printing materials. At small scale, variation can be tolerated. At millions of pencils per day, small inefficiencies become large problems.

A fraction of a gram saved in coating becomes significant.

A slightly faster sharpening wood matters.

A small reduction in broken cores matters.

A package that stacks more efficiently matters.

The humble school pencil is therefore a product of industrial optimization.

This is one reason I would not compare a Nataraj 621 directly with a Hi-Uni or a Caran d’Ache Grafwood and declare a winner. They were designed around different economic and technical goals.

A Hi-Uni can justify extra attention to finish and graphite refinement because it occupies a premium position.

A 621 has to deliver acceptable performance at mass-school-stationery scale.

Its engineering target is consistency per unit cost.

That makes the pencil more interesting, not less.

For collectors, the best approach is to treat Nataraj 621 as a product family rather than a single immutable object. Look for changes in stripe patterns, logos, grade markings, box graphics, pack sizes, and variant names.

Keep the packaging whenever possible.

A loose pencil records the barrel design.

A box records the market around it.

Packaging can reveal whether a version was aimed at domestic buyers or export markets, how the company described the lead, what benefits were emphasized, and how Nataraj’s graphic identity changed.

The current global catalogue, for example, presents 621 Pearl and 621 Jade as twelve-pencil packs with universal HB grade and distinctive stripe designs. Older catalogues use different presentation language and emphasize performance claims such as bold writing and bonded lead.

Those differences create a timeline.

They also show why inexpensive pencils can be excellent collecting material. Premium pencils are often preserved deliberately. School pencils are consumed.

They are sharpened down, chewed, lost, lent to classmates, left in examination halls, and thrown away when only a stub remains.

An unused older school pencil can therefore become surprisingly informative.

It preserves an object that was never expected to survive.

India is also a useful correction to the usual geography of pencil history. European firms dominate accounts of nineteenth-century graphite manufacturing, while Japanese brands dominate many modern discussions of pencil finish. But contemporary pencil culture is much larger than those two traditions.

Hindustan Pencils represents a different story: post-independence Indian manufacturing, huge domestic educational demand, value-oriented stationery, and production at a scale that turns a small wooden object into a major industrial product.

The Nataraj 621 carries that story without looking important.

That is exactly why it belongs in a collection.

Some pencils are collectible because they were expensive.

Some because they were technically unusual.

Some because they disappeared.

The 621 is worth keeping for another reason.

It became ordinary at extraordinary scale.
