Darkness is easy to notice in a pencil test, but point retention may matter more during actual writing. It describes how well the sharpened point keeps a useful shape as the pencil moves across the page.

Softer graphite generally wears more quickly because it deposits more material. A very soft pencil can produce a rich dark line but lose its needle point after only a short passage. Harder grades usually preserve a fine point longer, although they may feel scratchier or write too lightly for some users.

Core diameter matters as well. A thick soft core may become rounded quickly but continue writing comfortably from many angles. A thinner, firmer core can maintain precise lines for small handwriting. Paper texture changes the equation: rough paper abrades the core faster than smooth paper.

This is why I never judge a pencil from one line. I write a paragraph, rotate the pencil periodically, and watch how the line width changes. A pencil that feels spectacular for the first ten words may become blunt too quickly for long notes.

The ideal point retention is personal. Artists may want fast material deposition. A student taking pages of notes may prefer a pencil that stays controlled longer. A collector should test both sensation and endurance, because the best pencil is not always the darkest or the smoothest one.
