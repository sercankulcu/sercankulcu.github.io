Most pencils disappear quietly when production ends. The Blackwing 602 did the opposite. Discontinuation turned it from a respected writing tool into one of the most recognizable cult objects in pencil collecting.

Eberhard Faber introduced the Blackwing 602 in the United States in the 1930s. Its barrel carried the memorable slogan “Half the Pressure, Twice the Speed,” a line that suggested effortless writing and became inseparable from the pencil’s identity.

The 602 looked unusual even before it touched paper. Instead of a conventional cylindrical ferrule, it used a distinctive rectangular metal eraser holder with a replaceable eraser. That small piece of hardware gave the pencil a profile that could be recognized from across a desk.

Its reputation, however, was built on more than appearance. The Blackwing became associated with writers, composers, animators, and other creative professionals who valued a dark, smooth line and a pencil that could move quickly across paper. John Steinbeck and animator Chuck Jones are among the names most often connected with the model.

The company behind the pencil changed several times. Eberhard Faber was acquired by Faber-Castell in the late 1980s, and the Blackwing continued through that transition. The line later passed to Sanford. By the late 1990s, production volume was relatively small, and manufacturing the unusual ferrule had become increasingly impractical.

Production ended in 1998.

That should have been the end of the story. Instead, scarcity changed the meaning of the pencil. People who had used Blackwings began searching for old stock. Collectors compared imprint styles, ferrule details, logos, boxes, and ownership-era variations. Individual pencils that had once been ordinary stationery began selling for surprisingly high prices.

The Blackwing had become valuable partly because it was no longer available.

This is an important lesson in pencil collecting. Rarity alone does not create significance. The 602 already had a distinctive design, a long production history, recognizable users, and a strong reputation before it disappeared. Discontinuation simply concentrated attention on qualities that had previously been easy to take for granted.

The story changed again in 2010 when California Cedar Products revived the Blackwing name. The modern Blackwing line is not simply a continuation of every technical detail of the original Eberhard Faber pencil, but it deliberately preserves the most recognizable part of the design: the rectangular ferrule and replaceable eraser.

For collectors, this creates two related but different objects. Original Eberhard Faber, Faber-Castell, and Sanford-era Blackwings belong to the historical production line that ended in 1998. Modern Blackwings belong to a revival that began more than a decade later.

That distinction matters when cataloging them. A pencil marked Blackwing 602 is not automatically the same object simply because the name is familiar. Imprints, ferrules, packaging, manufacturer names, and small design changes can reveal which period a pencil belongs to.

The Blackwing 602 is therefore more than an expensive old pencil. It is a case study in how an everyday tool can move through several identities: office supply, professional favorite, discontinued product, collector obsession, and finally revived brand.

Few wooden pencils have such a visible second life. The Blackwing 602 was sharpened away for decades, disappeared from production, and somehow became more famous after it was gone.
