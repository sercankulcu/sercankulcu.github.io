Pencil reviews often treat smoothness as the final score. I think that misses much of what makes a pencil useful.

A very smooth pencil can feel luxurious, but too little feedback may reduce control for small handwriting. A slightly firmer pencil can communicate the texture of the paper and make letterforms feel more deliberate. Artists may want another balance entirely depending on whether they are sketching, hatching, or shading.

Smoothness also interacts with darkness and point retention. A soft, heavily lubricated core may glide beautifully but wear quickly. A harder core can maintain detail while feeling less creamy. Neither is automatically better.

I therefore describe pencils using several dimensions: darkness, smoothness, feedback, point retention, smudging, core strength, sharpening quality, and comfort. The barrel itself matters too. Paint can be slippery or tactile; edges can feel sharp or rounded; diameter can affect fatigue.

The most interesting pencils are not necessarily those that win every category. They are pencils with a coherent personality. A crisp, precise pencil can be excellent precisely because it is not buttery smooth. Once quality is separated from smoothness, pencil testing becomes much more useful.
