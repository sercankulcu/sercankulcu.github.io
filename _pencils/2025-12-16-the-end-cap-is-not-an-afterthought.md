Turn an untipped pencil around and the final few millimeters often reveal more design work than expected.

Some pencils simply expose a flat painted end. Others use a contrasting dip, a stripe, a metal cap, a ring, or an elaborate multi-color pattern. These features make the pencil recognizable when only the end is visible in a cup.

End treatments can indicate grade or product family. They can also change across generations, giving collectors another clue when dating a variant.

Manufacturing them adds complexity. A clean dip requires consistent depth. Multiple stripes need additional operations. Metal caps must fit securely without damaging the wood.

Wear is common because the end hits tabletops and pencil cases. An otherwise pristine pencil may have chipped paint at the back long before the main barrel shows damage.

I always include the end cap in documentation photographs. It may occupy less than one percent of the pencil's length, but it can carry a surprisingly large share of its identity.
