A dark graphite drawing can suddenly flash silver when viewed from an angle. That sheen is a fundamental property of the material rather than a defect in the pencil.

Graphite has a layered crystalline structure. As a pencil deposits particles on paper, those particles can reflect light strongly. Heavy pressure and repeated layering tend to create smoother, denser graphite surfaces that show more shine.

The effect becomes particularly obvious in very dark passages. Trying to force graphite toward the visual black of charcoal can produce more metallic reflection instead of a deeper matte black.

Artists manage this in different ways. Some accept the sheen as part of graphite's character. Others limit pressure, build values gradually, or combine materials when they need truly dark matte areas.

Collectors can notice sheen during pencil comparison too. Different formulations and papers produce visibly different surfaces even when two swatches look equally dark head-on.

Graphite's shine is a good reminder that "black" is not a single property. A pencil mark has value, texture, reflectivity, and particle structure. Tilt the page and the material reveals itself.
