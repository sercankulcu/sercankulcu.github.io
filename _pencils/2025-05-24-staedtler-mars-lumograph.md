The blue barrel of the STAEDTLER Mars Lumograph has become one of the most recognizable forms in professional graphite drawing. It is not simply a blue pencil available in many grades. It belongs to a product line that helped make graded drawing pencils into a systematic professional tool.

The history of the Mars name reaches back more than a century.

STAEDTLER states that the Mars word mark was applied for in 1900 and registered in 1901. The name became associated with the company's premium products and eventually with the blue that still identifies the line.

By 1908, Mars pencils were already available in seventeen degrees of hardness.

That detail is important because it shows how early the pencil was being marketed as part of a graded system rather than as one general-purpose writing tool.

A wide grade range allowed artists, architects, draftsmen, and technical users to choose different balances of darkness and point retention without leaving the same product family.

The blue finish became part of that identity.

STAEDTLER later used the term “Mars blue” for the characteristic color, and the blue barrel remains central to the visual language of the range.

That continuity makes the Mars Lumograph particularly interesting to collectors.

A modern pencil is instantly recognizable, but older examples can differ in logo, typography, country marking, grade stamp, end treatment, lacquer tone, and packaging.

The basic form stays familiar while small production details change.

The current standard Mars Lumograph 100 range is unusually broad.

STAEDTLER lists twenty-four grades from 12B through 10H.

The sequence is 12B, 11B, 10B, 9B, 8B, 7B, 6B, 5B, 4B, 3B, 2B, B, HB, F, H, 2H, 3H, 4H, 5H, 6H, 7H, 8H, 9H, and 10H.

That is not merely a marketing exercise.

At the soft end, the pencil behaves almost like a compact graphite drawing stick.

At the hard end, it becomes a precision instrument capable of fine, pale, point-retentive lines.

The same blue barrel therefore covers a remarkable range of behavior.

Core diameter changes across the range.

According to STAEDTLER's current specifications, the 12B uses a core of about 4.5 mm.

Grades 11B through 6B use approximately 3.6 mm.

Grades 5B through 3B use around 2.5 mm.

Grades 2B through 10H use roughly 2.0 mm.

This is a useful example of mechanical design following material behavior.

Very soft graphite is more fragile and intended for broader deposition.

A thicker core gives it more physical presence and makes side shading easier.

Hard graphite can remain narrow because it is stronger and intended for precise lines.

The changing core diameter also means that two Mars Lumographs with identical barrel dimensions can feel very different after sharpening.

A 10H exposes a slim, hard point.

A 12B reveals a thick core that dominates the sharpened cone.

That difference affects sharpening geometry.

A long point on a hard grade can remain stable for fine work.

A long exposed 12B point is much more vulnerable and is often better treated like an artist's soft graphite tool.

The range therefore rewards matching the sharpener to the grade.

A conventional sharpener works well for ordinary middle grades.

Very soft grades may benefit from careful blade condition, shorter support, or manual refinement.

The grade range also exposes the limitations of treating H and B labels as universal measurements.

A STAEDTLER HB is STAEDTLER's interpretation of HB.

Another manufacturer may tune its HB darker, smoother, softer, or firmer.

A complete Mars range is useful precisely because it lets the user study one manufacturer's internal progression.

That progression is more meaningful than comparing isolated labels from unrelated brands.

Move from 4H to 2H to HB to 2B to 4B and the change is gradual.

Point retention decreases.

Material deposition increases.

The line darkens.

The point broadens faster.

The amount of visible graphite on the paper increases.

The sensory experience changes too.

Hard grades produce more audible and tactile feedback.

Soft grades glide more easily and cover more tooth.

This is why a full-range test is more revealing than a single HB review.

The hard grades historically align with drafting and technical traditions.

Before CAD displaced much manual drawing, architects, engineers, and draftsmen depended heavily on controlled line weight, clean reproduction, and stable points.

Hard pencils were useful for construction lines, fine notation, and precise geometry.

The softer grades served different roles.

Artists could build tone, shade large areas, and create darker expressive marks.

One family could therefore move from technical precision to pictorial drawing.

This dual identity helps explain the longevity of the line.

The Mars Lumograph is neither only an art pencil nor only a drafting pencil.

Its design sits between those traditions.

Graphite sheen becomes increasingly relevant in the softer grades.

As more graphite is deposited and compressed on smooth paper, the surface can reflect light.

This metallic lustre is a normal property of graphite rather than a manufacturing defect.

It is also why the darkest graphite does not behave like charcoal.

A heavily worked 8B or 12B graphite area can look dark from one angle and silvery from another.

STAEDTLER now offers related Mars Lumograph variants that address different drawing needs.

The broader family includes black, charcoal, aquarell, jumbo, and pure-graphite versions in addition to the classic 100 graphite pencil.

These are not merely grade extensions.

They change the material system itself.

Mars Lumograph Black is designed for darker, more matte results than ordinary graphite.

Charcoal variants introduce charcoal behavior.

Aquarell variants are water-soluble.

Pure graphite removes the conventional wooden casing and presents a much larger graphite body.

These extensions make the Mars name a platform rather than a single pencil.

For collectors, however, the classic blue wood-cased Lumograph remains the central reference point.

The barrel is hexagonal.

That shape resists rolling and provides stable faces for imprinting.

It also supports controlled rotation during drawing.

Artists often rotate the pencil to maintain a sharper edge as the point wears.

The blue lacquer has practical and historical value.

Practically, it protects the wood and provides a consistent tactile surface.

Historically, it makes production changes easier to compare because the overall color identity remains stable while smaller details evolve.

The imprint deserves close inspection.

Logo form, font, grade placement, “Made in Germany” wording, registered marks, article numbers, and other details can help separate generations.

Packaging can be even more informative.

Older boxes and tins may carry addresses, multilingual text, grade charts, and graphic styles tied to particular periods.

A collector trying to build a chronology should never rely only on the barrel.

The box may narrow the date far more effectively.

The line is also useful for studying how grade systems expanded over time.

Early broad ranges were already a feature of Mars pencils.

Modern manufacturing has extended that idea further into extremely soft grades such as 12B.

The result is a product family where the historical principle remains recognizable even as the endpoints expand.

The softest modern grades should not be interpreted as simply “more black” versions of HB.

Their larger cores, faster wear, broader side strokes, and different handling make them distinct drawing tools.

Likewise, 8H, 9H, and 10H are not ordinary writing pencils made slightly harder.

They belong to a precision end of the scale where line control and low deposition become central.

Paper choice changes the experience across the range.

Hard grades on smooth paper can feel precise and clean.

The same grades on rough paper may feel dry or scratchy.

Soft grades on toothy paper deposit graphite quickly and create visible texture.

On smooth paper they produce denser layers and more sheen.

A useful Mars Lumograph test therefore uses multiple papers.

Take one hard grade, HB, a medium-soft grade, and one extreme soft grade.

Make the same writing sample, pressure gradient, crosshatching, shading block, and erased patch.

Compare point wear after each.

The family resemblance becomes clear, but so do the limits of a single hardness scale.

Erasing also changes with grade.

Light marks from hard grades can often be removed cleanly if pressure has not embossed the paper.

Soft grades deposit more graphite and can smear or remain in the paper tooth.

Heavy pressure in any grade can create physical indentations that erasing cannot remove.

The wood matters as well.

A professional pencil should sharpen cleanly and keep the core well centered.

An excellent graphite formula is frustrating if the barrel splinters or the core wanders.

The Mars Lumograph's reputation therefore depends on the complete object, not only the grade printed near the end.

Core centering, bonding, lacquer, wood, typography, and grade consistency all contribute.

This is why the pencil works well as a reference specimen.

A collector can use it to compare other drawing pencils against a stable, widely recognized family.

That does not mean it is objectively superior to every competitor.

Faber-Castell Castell 9000, Koh-I-Noor 1500, Mitsubishi Hi-Uni, Tombow MONO 100, and other long-running ranges represent different manufacturing philosophies.

The interesting comparison is not “which famous pencil wins?”

It is how each manufacturer interprets hardness, smoothness, darkness, finish, and grade progression.

Mars Lumograph tends to make that comparison particularly clear because the range is so broad.

A single HB is useful for writing.

A box spanning H through B grades is useful for drawing.

A near-complete range becomes something else: a map of how one manufacturer translates a hardness scale into physical cores.

For collectors, older Mars Lumographs add a second map on top of the first.

The grades show technical progression.

The imprints and boxes show historical progression.

One can compare a modern HB with an older HB and ask whether lacquer, typography, core feel, centering, wood, or packaging changed.

One can compare old and new grade ranges.

One can track when particular wording appeared.

That makes the pencil both a tool and a document.

The Mars Lumograph is therefore interesting for more than its blue paint.

Its importance comes from the combination of a long-running premium identity, a systematic grade philosophy, recognizable industrial design, and continued usefulness across technical and artistic work.

A modern 12B and a 10H look like members of the same family.

Put them on paper and they behave almost like different species of tool.

That contrast is exactly what makes the Mars Lumograph one of the best pencils for understanding what the graphite hardness scale actually means in practice.
