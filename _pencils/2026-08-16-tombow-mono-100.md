Some pencils are remembered because they changed shape, introduced a new mechanism, or became inexpensive enough to appear everywhere. The Tombow MONO 100 is interesting for a different reason: it represents an attempt to perfect a familiar object without changing what that object fundamentally is.

Tombow introduced the original MONO pencil in 1963 as a high-grade drawing pencil. Four years later, in 1967, the company released the MONO 100 to mark its 55th anniversary. It was positioned above the earlier MONO and became the premium model of the series.

The name tells part of the story. Tombow had been working on improving pencil cores since 1949, paying particular attention to the fineness and density of the particles inside the lead. The company says the MONO 100 name referred to a core refined to roughly ten billion particles per cubic millimeter. Whether or not a writer ever thinks about particles while taking notes, the engineering goal is easy to understand: a line that is dark and smooth while the core remains strong enough for controlled work.

That balance is what makes a premium graphite pencil difficult to design. Darkness alone is easy to notice, but a very soft core can wear quickly. Hardness gives a longer-lasting point, but taken too far it can make the pencil feel pale or resistant on paper. A good drawing pencil has to manage several qualities at once rather than simply maximize one of them.

The MONO 100 makes that range visible. It is produced in seventeen grades from 9H to 6B. At the hard end it can make fine, controlled marks; toward the soft end the line becomes darker and broader. The barrel remains recognizably the same while the behavior of the core changes dramatically.

Its appearance is unusually restrained for something sold as a top-tier product. The glossy black hexagonal body carries gold lettering and a gold ring near the end. There is no ferrule and no attached eraser. The result feels closer to a traditional drawing instrument than to a school pencil.

Yet an eraser is an important part of the MONO 100 story. When Tombow introduced the pencil in 1967, boxes included a plastic eraser as a promotional item. Plastic erasers were still unusual in Japan at the time, and the accessory became popular enough that Tombow began selling the MONO eraser as its own product in 1969. The eraser eventually became one of the most recognizable objects in the company's catalog.

That connection makes the MONO 100 particularly interesting to collectors. One pencil helped establish not only a long-running pencil model but also an entire visual identity that expanded far beyond graphite. Boxes, logos, grade markings, packaging, and accompanying erasers can all become clues to different periods of the MONO brand.

The pencil itself, however, has remained remarkably conservative. Its appeal does not depend on novelty. It is still a hexagonal wooden pencil that must be sharpened, becomes shorter with use, and eventually disappears.

That is exactly why the MONO 100 belongs in a pencil collection. It shows what happens when a manufacturer treats the ordinary wooden pencil not as a cheap object waiting to be replaced, but as a mature design worth refining in very small increments.
