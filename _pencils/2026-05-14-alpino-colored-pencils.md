Some pencil companies begin with graphite, machinery, or a famous inventor. Alpino began with leftover wood.

The story starts in 1933 in Anglès, a town in Girona, where the Masats brothers were working around a family toy-making business. Instead of discarding the pieces of wood left over from toy production, they began using them to make pencils and colored leads. The first experiments were small and improvised, but the idea eventually developed into one of Spain’s most recognizable school-stationery brands.

The business took time to stabilize. Alpino’s own history says that the operation began to succeed in 1942 and that the Alpino brand was formally founded in 1943. Three years later the company moved to Barcelona, where design, branding, and communication became increasingly important parts of the business.

That sequence makes Alpino different from many older European pencil companies. It did not emerge from an eighteenth- or nineteenth-century graphite-making district. It grew from a twentieth-century family workshop and from the practical reuse of wood already available for another product.

Colored pencils became the center of the identity.

Alpino still describes itself as a benchmark Spanish stationery brand and continues to sell colored pencils as one of its defining products. The current range includes standard school pencils, watercolor pencils, triangular pencils, erasable versions, pastel and fluorescent sets, and products intended for younger children.

The brand’s packaging became almost as important as the pencils themselves.

Across generations, Alpino boxes have retained a recognizable visual vocabulary built around mountains, a young deer, and strong color. The design has been modernized repeatedly, but the company has deliberately kept familiar elements so that parents who used Alpino at school can still recognize the brand when buying supplies for their children.

That kind of continuity is valuable to collectors because packaging changes provide a usable timeline.

The pencil itself may remain broadly similar while the box reveals shifts in typography, printing technology, safety marks, logos, language, barcode use, and marketing style. A group of Alpino boxes from different decades can therefore show the evolution of Spanish school design more clearly than a handful of loose pencils.

The brand name also works unusually well with the imagery.

“Alpino” immediately suggests mountains, and the mountain landscape became a recurring part of the package. The deer reinforces the outdoor image. Together they created a visual identity that could be understood by a child before the child was old enough to care about company history.

For collectors outside Spain, that cultural familiarity can be easy to underestimate.

An Alpino box is not rare simply because it is old. Millions of children used these pencils. Their importance comes from the opposite quality: they were ordinary enough to become part of shared school memory.

That makes wear interesting rather than merely undesirable.

A used Alpino pencil can show tooth marks, shortened length, uneven sharpening, name labels, and the particular colors that were consumed first. A school set with the red, blue, and green pencils reduced to stubs while less popular colors remain nearly full records actual behavior in a way an unopened box cannot.

Unopened sets preserve something different.

They keep the original assortment, packaging order, printed color names, factory sharpening, and graphic design together. For dating and comparison, a complete box is usually more informative than a single unused pencil.

The early history gives the wood an additional meaning.

Many pencil companies talk about wood mainly as a technical material: cedar for sharpening, basswood for economy, or plantation wood for supply stability. Alpino’s origin story begins before any of those questions. The first wood was valuable because it was available as waste from toy production and could be given another purpose.

That makes the company’s beginning an early example of material reuse, although it should not be confused with modern sustainability certification or industrial recycling systems. The Masats brothers were solving a practical workshop problem, not advertising an environmental program.

The brand also shows how colored pencils can build a stronger emotional identity than ordinary graphite pencils.

A graphite school pencil is often remembered by feel, grade, or yellow lacquer. A colored-pencil set is remembered as a group: the box, the arrangement of colors, the missing favorite pencil, the first drawing made with it, and the way the tips looked after repeated sharpening.

Alpino benefited from that collective memory.

For a collector, the most useful details to record are the exact box design, number of colors, logo form, deer and mountain artwork, company wording, country markings, pencil shape, barrel finish, and whether the pencils are factory sharpened. These details can separate decades that otherwise look superficially similar.

The company’s long survival also makes it useful for studying continuity.

A brand founded in 1943 can be compared across wartime scarcity, postwar consumer growth, the rise of mass advertising, barcode-era retail, modern safety regulation, and contemporary school-supply marketing.

The pencils remain simple.

The context around them changes.

That is the real attraction of Alpino for a pencil collection.

It is not only a Spanish colored-pencil brand.

It is a record of how a small workshop idea built from leftover toy wood became a school object recognized across generations.
