Colored pencils share the wooden form of graphite pencils, but the resemblance can hide a major material difference.

A graphite pencil relies on a graphite-based core. A colored pencil uses pigments with binders and other ingredients designed to carry color onto the paper. There is no hardness scale equivalent to the familiar H-to-B graphite system.

That difference affects sharpening and use. Colored cores may be softer or more vulnerable to breakage depending on formulation. Layering, blending, opacity, and lightfastness become more important concerns than graphite sheen or HB grading.

The wood casing still matters. Centering, bonding, and clean sharpening remain signs of good manufacturing. But the collector's questions change: pigment range, discontinued colors, lightfast ratings, set composition, and packaging become important.

I keep graphite and colored pencils as neighboring but distinct collections. Their shared shape makes comparison irresistible, yet the technologies inside are solving different problems.

A wooden barrel is a platform. What manufacturers place inside it can turn the same basic object into a writing pencil, drawing pencil, colored pencil, copying pencil, grease pencil, or specialty marking tool.
