Portugal has one surviving pencil factory, and its story begins before the name Viarco existed.

In 1907, Figueiredo Faria and the French engineer Jules Cacheux established a pencil-making operation in Vila do Conde. The company became known as Portugália, or Faria, Cacheux & Cª, and produced writing materials at a time when Portugal depended heavily on imported manufactured goods. The factory survived the disruptions of the First World War and the difficult economic years that followed, but by the early 1930s it needed new ownership and direction.

Manoel Vieira de Araújo, an industrialist from São João da Madeira with interests in hat manufacturing, acquired the pencil business. In 1936 the Viarco name was registered. The word was formed from Vieira Araújo & Companhia, linking the pencil operation to the family company while giving it a distinct identity.

That change was more than a new label. António Vieira Araújo, Manoel's son, took responsibility for rebuilding the pencil business. The company spent its early Viarco years improving formulas, machinery, production methods, and product quality. In 1941 the entire operation moved from Vila do Conde to São João da Madeira, taking equipment and a number of workers with it.

The factory still occupies that industrial landscape.

This is what makes Viarco unusual in a pencil collection. Many historic pencil names survive only as trademarks. Their original factories are gone, their machinery has been replaced, or production has moved across borders. Viarco remains tied to a specific Portuguese city and to manufacturing equipment whose history is visible inside the plant.

São João da Madeira treats the factory as part of its industrial heritage. Visitors can enter through the city's industrial-tourism program and see pencil production as a working process rather than as a museum reconstruction. The factory is old, but it is not a static exhibit.

Graphite still becomes pencils there.

The basic sequence would be recognizable to pencil makers from a century ago. Graphite and clay are prepared for the core. Wooden slats are grooved. Cores are placed between paired slats. The sandwich is glued, shaped into pencils, finished, printed, and packaged.

Modern pencil manufacturing elsewhere can hide much of this behind high-speed automated equipment. At Viarco, the age and scale of the factory make the individual stages easier to understand.

For a collector, this matters because the pencil stops looking like one object.

It becomes a chain of operations.

The graphite mixture determines darkness and hardness. The groove determines core position. The slat determines sharpening behavior. Shaping creates the familiar round or hexagonal body. Lacquer changes both appearance and surface feel. Foil printing adds identity. A painted end, eraser, or other finish can separate one product family from another.

Viarco's history also preserves the connection between ordinary pencils and specialized trades.

Over the decades the company produced graphite and colored pencils as well as pencils for carpenters, commerce, industry, copying, and other professional uses. Products such as the violet copying pencil, carpenter pencil, bicolor pencils, and technical grades reflect a period when different occupations regularly demanded different pencil formulations.

That variety is historically important.

Today, a general-purpose HB or No. 2 pencil dominates how many people imagine the category. Earlier stationery catalogs were far more specialized. A pencil could be designed for ledgers, copying systems, workshop surfaces, school writing, technical drawing, or marking particular materials.

A factory such as Viarco had to understand not simply how to make a pencil, but how to alter a pencil for the job.

The company also preserves a specifically Portuguese visual history. Old Viarco boxes, school pencils, logos, and advertising materials belong to generations that learned to write with the brand. The objects are valuable because they are local.

A German pencil can tell the history of Nuremberg manufacture.

A Japanese pencil can show postwar precision and finishing.

A Viarco pencil records Portugal's much smaller pencil industry.

That makes packaging especially worth collecting. A loose pencil gives you the imprint, grade, and finish. A box can reveal older logos, Portuguese wording, product numbers, intended professions, and the way the company presented itself to domestic customers.

The exact Viarco name also helps place a pencil within the company's history. During the 1970s the pencil business became more independent from the wider Vieira Araújo group and operated as Viarco – Indústria de Lápis, Lda. Older material can therefore preserve company descriptions that belong to earlier corporate phases.

These changes are easy to miss if every pencil is cataloged only as “Viarco.”

The factory's survival was not guaranteed.

Like many European stationery manufacturers, Viarco faced intense competition from lower-cost international production. A small Portuguese plant could not win by trying to manufacture anonymous commodity pencils at the same scale as the largest global factories.

Instead, the company increasingly treated its history and manufacturing knowledge as strengths.

One result was a closer relationship with artists and designers. Viarco opened the factory to creative collaboration and developed products that use graphite in less conventional forms. The ArtGraf line grew from this direction, exploring graphite as a material for painting and expressive drawing rather than only as the core of a wooden pencil.

That development is interesting because it does not reject the old factory.

It uses it differently.

The same company that preserves traditional school and trade pencils can also produce water-soluble graphite and experimental drawing materials. Heritage becomes useful when it remains connected to manufacturing rather than being reduced to nostalgia.

There is also something important in the scale of the operation.

Large pencil companies build identity through worldwide distribution. Viarco's identity is almost the reverse. Its value comes from remaining unusually specific.

One country.

One city.

One working factory.

A long sequence of machinery, recipes, packaging, and local memory.

This makes Viarco particularly rewarding for chronological collecting. Rather than buying one modern example, look for pencils and boxes from different decades. Compare the typography. Record product numbers. Note whether a pencil was intended for school, drawing, copying, or industry. Photograph end treatments and barrel markings.

The differences can reveal how the Portuguese market changed.

A school pencil reflects education.

A copying pencil reflects office technology.

A carpenter pencil reflects manual trades.

An experimental graphite block reflects the contemporary art market.

The factory connects them all.

Visiting Viarco would therefore be different from visiting a pencil museum. A museum normally begins after production has ended. Viarco allows industrial history and current manufacture to occupy the same building.

Machines become artifacts without ceasing to be tools.

That is rare.

It also changes how I would evaluate a Viarco pencil. I would not begin by asking whether its graphite is smoother than a Hi-Uni or darker than a Grafwood of the same nominal grade. Those comparisons can be useful, but they miss the most distinctive part of the object.

The better question is what manufacturing tradition the pencil carries.

A Viarco pencil connects a 1907 Portuguese writing-material factory, a 1936 brand, a 1941 move to São João da Madeira, decades of school and industrial products, and a factory that still participates in the city's working industrial landscape.

That is a remarkable amount of history for one wooden pencil.

Most pencils disappear as they are used.

Viarco survived by continuing to make them.
