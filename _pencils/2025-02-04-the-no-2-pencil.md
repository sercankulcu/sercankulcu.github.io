In the United States, "No. 2 pencil" became almost synonymous with school and testing.

The numbering system describes hardness, with No. 2 occupying a practical middle range comparable in general use to HB. It is dark enough for ordinary writing and machine-readable forms while still retaining a useful point.

The interesting part is that different grading traditions coexist. A pencil may be marked No. 2, HB, or even both, depending on brand and market. These systems are useful guides, but exact equivalence is not absolute because graphite formulations differ between manufacturers.

The No. 2 also became culturally durable because institutions standardized around it. Teachers requested it. Test instructions named it. Office supply stores stocked it by the box. Repetition turned a technical grade into a social expectation.

For a collector, this creates a wonderfully broad category. Premium pencils, cheap school pencils, advertising pencils, government issue pencils, and novelty products can all carry the same No. 2 designation while differing enormously in construction.

I like that contradiction. "No. 2" sounds standardized, but a drawer full of No. 2 pencils quickly demonstrates how much variety can exist inside a standard.
