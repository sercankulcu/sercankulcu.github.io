Long before yellow became the default visual language of the school pencil, one yellow pencil was already being used as a statement of quality.

The KOH-I-NOOR 1500 entered production in 1888 under Franz Hardtmuth. By then, the Hardtmuth family had already spent generations turning graphite and clay into a more controlled writing material. The company itself traced its origins to Vienna in 1790, when Joseph Hardtmuth founded the business that would eventually become one of the most important names in pencil manufacturing.

The factory moved from Vienna to České Budějovice in 1848. That location would become important not only to the company but, according to KOH-I-NOOR's own historical account, to the language used for pencil grades.

The letters H, B, and F are now so familiar that they can look like abstract technical symbols. In the Hardtmuth tradition, however, they were tied to names and places: H for Hardtmuth, B for Budweis—the German name for České Budějovice—and F for Franz.

The 1500 was launched in seventeen gradations. That was a remarkable idea for the late nineteenth century. Instead of treating a pencil simply as hard or soft, the manufacturer could offer a controlled family of tools with predictable differences in darkness and hardness.

That system helped turn pencil grading into something more precise. Artists, engineers, draftsmen, and writers could choose a grade for a task instead of adapting every task to one general-purpose pencil.

The yellow barrel also mattered.

Color on a pencil is easy to dismiss as decoration, but on the 1500 it became part of the product's identity. The bright finish made the pencil recognizable in shops and catalogs, and it helped separate a premium manufactured object from the plain wooden pencils that surrounded it.

The name KOH-I-NOOR reinforced the same idea. It referred to the famous Koh-i-Noor diamond and presented the pencil as something refined rather than ordinary. The branding was successful enough that the company eventually incorporated KOH-I-NOOR into its own name.

In 1900, the yellow 1500 received a Grand Prix award in Paris. By the years before the First World War, KOH-I-NOOR HARDTMUTH had grown into one of the largest pencil manufacturers in the world.

For collectors, the 1500 is especially useful because its history is visible in details.

Older pencils may differ in imprint wording, logos, country markings, grade typography, lacquer tone, end treatment, or packaging. Political borders and company identities changed repeatedly across Central Europe during the twentieth century, so even a small line of text on a pencil can provide clues about when it was made.

That makes the 1500 more than an old drawing pencil. It can also be read as a small industrial document.

There is another reason it belongs in a collection beside pencils such as the Castell 9000, Mars Lumograph, Hi-Uni, MONO 100, and Blackwing 602. Each represents a different idea of what a premium pencil should be.

Some emphasize smoothness. Some emphasize precision. Some become famous through cultural associations or unusual design.

The KOH-I-NOOR 1500 represents standardization.

Its importance lies in the idea that a pencil could belong to a carefully ordered system of grades, each manufactured to perform differently while still remaining part of one recognizable family.

More than a century later, the object itself still looks almost disarmingly simple: yellow paint, black lettering, wood, and graphite.

That simplicity hides a large historical footprint.

The 1500 is a reminder that even the most familiar conventions of the pencil—the grade on the barrel, the expectation of consistency, and even the visual power of yellow—had to be invented, refined, and sold to the world.
