---
title: 'Silence Does Not Explain Itself'
date: 2023-03-13
tags:
  - relationships
  - endings
  - uncertainty
---

When someone leaves without saying goodbye, the mind does not tolerate the blank space for long.

It begins writing.

I must have done something wrong.

They never cared.

They were angry.

They found someone else.

The silence becomes evidence for whichever explanation hurts most.

That is the difficult thing about unexplained endings: absence contains almost no information, but it creates enormous interpretive pressure.

People leave quietly for many reasons. Fear. Shame. Exhaustion. Avoidance. Confusion. Cowardice. A wish to reduce conflict. A situation they do not know how to describe.

Some reasons are understandable.

Some are not admirable at all.

But silence alone does not tell us which one we are dealing with.

This is where I think two questions need to be separated.

**Was the way they left acceptable?**

and

**Do I know why they left?**

The answer to the first can be no while the answer to the second is still no.

That matters because understanding and approval are often confused. If I admit that another person may have reasons I cannot see, it can sound as if I am excusing the hurt they caused.

I am not.

A person can deserve an explanation and never receive one.

A relationship can matter and still end badly.

Someone can have complicated reasons and still behave irresponsibly.

All of these can be true at the same time.

The temptation is to solve uncertainty by converting it into judgment.

If they will not explain themselves, I will explain them for them.

The problem is that invented certainty can keep the relationship psychologically active long after the person is gone. We replay scenes, reinterpret messages, build motives, test theories against evidence that cannot answer back.

At some point, the search for closure can become another form of attachment.

Closure is usually imagined as information: one final conversation that makes the ending understandable.

Sometimes that happens.

Sometimes it does not.

Then closure has to mean something less satisfying: accepting that the story ended with missing pages.

That is not the same as saying the missing pages do not matter.

It is deciding that they do not get to hold the rest of the book hostage.

Silence can hurt.

It can be unfair.

It can even reveal something about how a person handles difficult endings.

But silence does not explain itself.

And we should be careful about turning what we do not know into the certainty that wounds us most.
