---
title: "When Growing Older Means Missing the Old Things"
date: 2009-12-26
tags:
   - aging
   - childhood
   - memory
   - change
   - reflection
---

When I was a child, getting something new could feel urgent.

A new pair of shoes.

A new school bag.

Something cleaner, newer, less worn than the thing I already had.

If everyone else had replaced theirs and I was still using the old one, the difference could become painfully visible.

Children notice these things.

They notice brands.

Colors.

Scratches.

Wear.

Who has the newest version.

Who is still carrying last year's bag.

And when you are young, the old object can feel like the problem.

You want it gone.

You want the new one.

You want to catch up.

In December 2009, I wrote something about aging that began from exactly this place.

I asked whether you had ever had to keep using something from childhood — a pair of shoes or a school bag, for example — even after your friends had replaced theirs.

Had you argued with your parents because your own things looked old and worn?

Then I wrote the part that still works for me:

Maybe growing older is what happens when, instead of trying to replace your old things, you begin wondering where all the old things your friends used to have have gone.

It is a small reversal.

But a powerful one.

As a child, the old object embarrasses you.

As an adult, its disappearance saddens you.

Nothing about the object itself has changed.

Only your relationship with time.

This may be one of the strangest parts of aging.

You spend the first part of life waiting for replacement.

Then, slowly, you begin mourning it.

You want the next thing.

The next class.

The next phone.

The next car.

The next apartment.

The next stage.

You want to stop being the person with the old version.

Then one day, without noticing exactly when it happened, you begin looking backward.

Where did that bag go?

Where did those shoes go?

Where did that classroom go?

Where did the desk go?

Where did the people go?

The object is often only the first question.

Behind it is a larger one.

Where did that version of life go?

This is why old objects can become emotionally dangerous.

They look harmless.

A school notebook.

A broken watch.

A cheap toy.

A jacket.

A key that no longer opens anything.

But they are not only objects.

They are containers.

They hold rooms.

Voices.

Routes.

A certain age.

A certain body.

A certain way of thinking.

A certain group of people who once seemed permanent.

That is what nostalgia does.

It attaches time to matter.

Then the matter survives longer than expected.

Or disappears too early.

When an old object survives, it can suddenly become precious.

Something you once wanted to throw away becomes impossible to discard.

Not because it is useful.

Because it is one of the few physical witnesses left.

You may not remember every day of childhood.

But you remember the bag.

You may not remember every conversation with your friends.

But you remember the shoes everyone wore.

You may not remember exactly how the classroom smelled or what was written on the walls.

But one object can open the whole room.

Memory likes handles.

Objects give it something to hold.

This is why the disappearance of ordinary things can feel so strange.

Nobody announces it.

There is no ceremony.

One year, a friend has a particular school bag.

The next year, something else.

The old one is probably thrown away, given to someone, put into storage, lost, damaged, forgotten.

At the time, nobody cares.

Why would they?

It is only a bag.

Years later, that "only" becomes the problem.

Because now the bag is not replaceable.

You can buy another one that looks similar.

That is not the same thing.

The original object belonged to the original time.

Its scratches came from those days.

Its wear came from those corridors.

A reproduction cannot inherit history.

This is one of the reasons nostalgia can become expensive.

People try to buy back objects from their childhood.

Old game consoles.

Toys.

Cars.

Cassette players.

Furniture.

Books.

Even packaging.

At first glance it looks like collecting.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes it is an attempt to rebuild access to a vanished period.

You are not really buying the object.

You are buying the possibility of recognition.

There it is.

I remember this.

I touched one like this.

We had this.

That sentence has enormous emotional power.

It reconnects present self and past self for a moment.

The object becomes a bridge.

But the bridge is incomplete.

You can recover the toy.

Not the afternoon.

You can recover the model of the school bag.

Not the classmates.

You can find the same pair of shoes online.

Not the body that wore them.

That is where nostalgia becomes both comforting and cruel.

It can return the surface.

Not the time.

The 2009 entry is interesting to me because it captures the beginning of that change in perspective.

The child is focused on what is missing from the present.

I do not have the new thing.

The adult becomes focused on what is missing from the past.

Where did the old thing go?

Those are two very different kinds of lack.

The first is comparative.

My friends have something newer.

I do not.

The second is historical.

We all had things once.

Now they are gone.

In childhood, other people make you aware of what you do not have.

In adulthood, memory makes you aware of what nobody has anymore.

That may be one of the reasons nostalgia becomes stronger as peer groups age together.

At first, everyone is moving toward acquisition.

New clothes.

New devices.

New jobs.

New homes.

New cars.

Later, conversations slowly begin filling with disappearance.

Do you remember that place?

It closed.

Do you remember that teacher?

They retired.

Do you remember that shop?

Gone.

Do you remember that car your father had?

Sold years ago.

Do you remember that friend?

I haven't heard from him in ages.

The vocabulary changes.

"New" becomes less exciting.

"Still there" becomes more precious.

This can happen with cities too.

A young person sees an old building and thinks it needs renovation.

An older person sees it and thinks:

At least that is still there.

The building may be ugly.

The café may not even have been good.

The street may have been inconvenient.

But persistence acquires emotional value.

Survival becomes a form of beauty.

Because so much else did not survive.

This does not mean we should preserve everything.

Cities need to change.

Homes need renovation.

Broken things need replacing.

Life cannot become a museum.

But it helps explain why change begins to hurt differently with age.

When you are young, change feels like arrival.

Later, change also contains departure.

Every new thing replaces something.

Every renovation removes a version.

Every move closes a room.

Every technological improvement quietly makes another habit impossible.

Progress is real.

Loss is also real.

Maturity may involve being able to hold both at once.

I would not want to return to every inconvenience of childhood.

I would not want the old shoes merely because they were old.

I would not want to freeze a city in 1995.

That would be absurd.

But I can still understand why the vanished object matters.

We do not miss everything about the past.

We miss continuity.

We miss evidence that the past really happened.

An old object says:

Yes.

That version of you existed.

These people were around.

This place was real.

You did not imagine it.

That assurance becomes more important as memory becomes less complete.

Time erodes detail.

Faces remain but dates blur.

Rooms remain but layouts change.

Stories remain but sequences become uncertain.

Physical objects resist that erosion.

They say:

Here is something from then.

That can feel almost miraculous.

This is one reason family storage rooms are emotionally complicated places.

They are full of objects nobody uses.

Old clothes.

Schoolbooks.

Appliances.

Documents.

Boxes.

Things kept because throwing them away feels too final.

A practical person looks and sees clutter.

A sentimental person looks and sees evidence.

Both are right.

This conflict becomes harder when parents age.

An object may mean nothing to one generation and everything to another.

A cupboard can contain a whole biography in things that have no market value.

The difficulty is deciding what deserves preservation.

Everything cannot.

If every object becomes sacred, life becomes storage.

So we choose.

Poorly.

We throw away something we later wish we had kept.

We keep something for twenty years and never look at it once.

There is no perfect system.

Memory does not tell us in advance which object will become important.

This is why photographs often become substitutes.

You do not keep the school bag.

But perhaps there is a photograph where it appears in the corner.

Years later, the photograph is not only about the person.

You zoom in.

There it is.

The bag.

The wallpaper.

The television.

The chair.

The old car outside.

Secondary objects become primary memories.

The thing nobody intended to photograph becomes the reason the photograph matters.

This is one of the unexpected gifts of old images.

Backgrounds become archives.

The center of the image was important then.

The edges become important later.

A birthday photograph becomes a record of furniture.

A graduation photograph becomes a record of a building.

A family photograph becomes a record of clothes nobody thought were special.

Time changes the subject.

The same happens with writing.

A short entry written casually in 2009 can become more interesting years later because it preserves what I was beginning to notice.

At the time, I probably thought I was writing a clever definition of aging.

Now I see something more personal in it.

The person who wrote it had started noticing disappearance.

That shift matters.

Aging is often described through the body.

Less energy.

Less sleep.

Grey hair.

Pain.

Slower recovery.

These are obvious markers.

But some of the earliest signs are psychological.

You begin relating differently to time.

You notice anniversaries.

You calculate how long ago something happened and are surprised by the answer.

You meet someone younger who was born after an event that still feels recent to you.

A song from your university years is called "old."

A computer you once considered advanced appears in a museum or retro collection.

A childhood object becomes antique enough to be sold as nostalgia.

None of this physically hurts.

Still, something shifts.

Your own lifetime becomes historical.

That may be one of the true thresholds.

As children, history is something that happened before us.

As adults, eventually, parts of our own lives become history.

Not metaphorically.

Literally.

Technology changes.

Buildings disappear.

People retire.

Cultural references stop being understood automatically.

You have to explain something that once needed no explanation.

That experience is surprisingly disorienting.

You mention a device.

A younger person asks what it was.

You mention how internet access worked.

They look at you as if you are describing a steam engine.

You say:

No, really, this was normal.

That sentence marks age beautifully.

This was normal.

Because normality has changed.

And with it, old objects stop being merely old.

They become proof of another normal.

That is why a school bag can matter.

Not because of the bag.

Because of the world around it.

Maybe the most touching part of the 2009 entry is that it uses friends' possessions rather than only my own.

I wrote about wondering where my friends' old things had gone.

That widens the nostalgia.

The lost world was collective.

Everyone had objects.

Everyone replaced them.

Nobody knew they were dismantling a shared landscape.

One friend's shoes disappeared.

Another friend's bag.

Another person's bicycle.

Someone's cassette collection.

Someone's first computer.

One by one, the common world became inaccessible.

This is how eras end.

Not on a specific day.

Through thousands of small replacements.

One new object at a time.

Nobody wakes up and says:

Today childhood is over.

Instead, the objects change.

The routines change.

The meeting places change.

The people become busy.

Then years later, you look around and realize the old version has almost entirely disappeared.

The transformation was gradual.

The recognition is sudden.

That may be why nostalgia can arrive without warning.

You see an object in a shop window.

Hear a sound.

Smell something.

Find an old photograph.

And suddenly a whole period returns.

Not because you were thinking about it.

Because the right key appeared.

The older I get, the more I think memory is less like a continuous film and more like a collection of locked rooms.

Most of the time, we do not know where the keys are.

Then an object unlocks one.

You step inside.

For a few seconds, details come back with surprising clarity.

Then the door closes again.

This is one reason I no longer dismiss old objects as easily as I probably once did.

I still do not want a house full of everything I have ever owned.

But I understand the emotional logic of keeping a few witnesses.

One or two school items.

An old computer part.

A ticket.

A photograph.

A notebook.

Something that has touched the time directly.

Not because the object is valuable.

Because memory is fragile.

And matter sometimes helps.

There is another reversal hidden here.

As a child, I may have argued with my parents because they would not replace something quickly enough.

As an adult, I can imagine the parents' perspective differently.

Perhaps the object was still usable.

Perhaps money mattered.

Perhaps replacement seemed unnecessary.

The child sees embarrassment.

The parent sees function and cost.

Both perspectives are real.

Age changes which one is easier to understand.

This is another way growing older rewrites childhood.

Not by changing what happened.

By adding viewpoints that were unavailable at the time.

You begin to understand why adults made decisions that once felt arbitrary.

Not all of them.

Some still seem wrong.

But many become legible.

The old bag becomes part of that process.

At age ten:

Why can't I have a new one?

At age thirty:

It was probably fine.

At age forty:

I wish I still had it.

That is almost comic.

The object survives three different moral systems.

First injustice.

Then practicality.

Then nostalgia.

The bag did nothing.

We changed around it.

Objects are patient like that.

They wait while we assign meaning.

Then eventually, most disappear before we are finished.

Perhaps that is the real lesson of the entry.

Not that aging means becoming sentimental about old things.

Something subtler.

Aging means understanding that replacement is not neutral.

When something leaves, a version of the world leaves with it.

Sometimes that is good.

Sometimes necessary.

Sometimes welcome.

But not meaningless.

The child thinks only about what the new object will add.

The older person also sees what the old object's disappearance removes.

Both perspectives are needed.

Without the first, life stagnates.

Without the second, life becomes disposable.

Maybe wisdom sits somewhere between them.

Replace what needs replacing.

But notice what is leaving.

Take a photograph.

Keep one thing.

Ask a parent about the story before the story is gone.

Write down the name of the place.

Save the small detail.

Because the strange thing about ordinary life is that it never tells you which parts will later become precious.

The school bag does not announce itself as memory.

It is just a school bag.

Until one day, many years later, you find yourself wondering where it went.

And by then, you are not really asking about the bag anymore.
