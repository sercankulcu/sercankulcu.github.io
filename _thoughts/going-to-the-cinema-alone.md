There was a time when going to the cinema alone felt like something that needed an explanation. Not a legal explanation—a social one. Why are you here alone? Could you not find anyone? Are you all right? Nobody necessarily asked these questions aloud, which was almost worse because you could imagine them.

Years ago, I wrote a very short entry: going to the cinema alone can be done to watch *Kaybedenler Kulübü*. That was all. One sentence. But I still remember the feeling hiding inside it. Some films do not require company. Some evenings do not require company either. The film is showing, you have time, you buy a ticket, and for two hours nobody needs anything from you.

This should not be remarkable. Yet for a long time, doing things alone was treated as evidence that something had gone wrong. Restaurants were for two or more people. Cafés were for meeting someone. Cinema was a social plan. Even walking somewhere could look incomplete if nobody walked beside you. The activity itself was not enough; it needed witnesses.

Maybe this is partly a young person's problem. When you are younger, social life can become proof that you belong somewhere, that people call you, that your weekend contains events. "I'm going to the cinema" sounds normal. "With whom?" is the question that quietly creates a hierarchy.

Cinema is a strange activity to attach that hierarchy to. You enter a dark room, sit facing forward, and are expected not to speak. For most of the experience, the person beside you may as well not exist. Still, company feels important because the ritual begins before the film: meeting, walking there, buying something, waiting, talking afterward.

When you go alone, the ritual changes. You arrive when you want, choose the seat you want, and leave when you want. If the film is terrible, there is nobody to blame for choosing it. If it is excellent, there is nobody beside you whose reaction can confirm yours. That last part is more important than it sounds.

We constantly check reality against other people. A joke lands and we turn. A surprising scene happens and we glance sideways. A song begins and someone smiles. We ask without speaking: *Did you see that too? Was that funny? Was that sad? Was that good?* When you are alone, the experience belongs more completely to you.

That can feel empty. It can also feel clean. There is something honest about liking a film without needing the person beside you to like it too. No post-film diplomacy. No saying "It was okay" when you loved it or "Yes, it was great" when you spent the last hour wondering whether falling asleep would be rude.

I think that is part of what I learned from going alone: solitude removes some performance. Not all of it—we can perform even for ourselves—but enough to notice the difference. If a scene affects you, there is no reason to hide it except habit. If the film bores you, you are free to admit it. Freedom sometimes looks very small: one ticket, one seat, no coordination.

This became more valuable as life became more complicated. Getting several adults to do something together can require project management: which day, what time, who has work, who has family obligations, who dislikes the genre, who already saw the film. At some point, the effort required to organize the experience becomes larger than the experience itself.

The younger version of you waits. Maybe next week. Maybe someone will be free. Then the film disappears. You did not miss it because you were busy. You missed it because nobody accompanied you.

Waiting for company can become waiting for permission.

A surprising number of things become possible once another person's participation stops being a requirement. You can eat somewhere because you want to eat there, attend a concert because you like the band, visit a museum, sit in a café, travel, walk around a city, watch a film. None of these actions announces that you have no friends. It announces only that the activity was worth doing.

Before learning this, solitude can feel public. You imagine everyone noticing: the waiter, the ticket clerk, the couple beside you. They probably are not. Most people are too busy wondering what other people think of them. The audience is mostly imaginary.

There is also a difference between being alone and being lonely. I did not always understand it. Loneliness is absence felt as pain; solitude can be absence felt as space. The same empty seat can mean different things on different nights. Sometimes you genuinely want another person there. That is not weakness. Sometimes you do not need one. That is not failure.

The film in my old entry made the idea funnier to me at the time, but the film eventually became less important than the habit. Once you do something alone a few times, the psychological barrier disappears. The first time feels like an announcement. The fifth time feels like Tuesday.

Eventually you stop thinking, *I am going to the cinema alone.* You think, *I am going to the cinema.* The word "alone" becomes unnecessary. That may be the best sign: independence stops feeling like an identity and becomes simply one available way of doing things.

I do not think everything is better alone. Some films become permanent memories because of who sat beside you. A conversation afterward can matter more than anything on screen. Human connection adds things solitude cannot.

But connection should add to an experience, not authorize it.

If someone comes with you, good. If nobody can, decide whether the thing itself is still worth doing. If it is, go.

There is no prize for missing your own life politely.

Years ago, I reduced all of this to one line: going to the cinema alone? Sure. For *Kaybedenler Kulübü*, it can be done.

Today I would remove the condition.

It can simply be done.
