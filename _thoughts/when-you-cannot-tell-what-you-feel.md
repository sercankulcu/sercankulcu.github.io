title: "When You Cannot Tell What You Feel"
date: 2010-04-24

There is a point in some relationships when the category stops being clear.

Friend.

Close friend.

Someone you protect.

Someone you miss.

Someone you are attracted to.

Someone whose attention matters more than it probably should.

The difficulty is not always that you feel too little.

Sometimes you feel several things at once.

In April 2010, I wrote about that state.

There was a woman I had become very used to having around.

She was beautiful.

She had a boyfriend.

Other men around us were obviously interested in her.

And yet I felt that she gave me more attention than she gave many of them.

She trusted me.

She seemed comfortable with me.

The problem was that I could not decide what that meant for me.

Was it love?

Friendship?

Something protective, almost brotherly?

Every time I moved mentally toward one explanation, another one appeared and contradicted it.

The most honest part of the old entry was the last sentence:

What I really wanted was for her attention to never end.

That may have been the clearest feeling in the whole situation.

Not romance.

Not friendship.

Not possession.

Continuity.

I wanted whatever existed between us to remain.

That desire can be surprisingly powerful.

We often think emotional uncertainty means we do not know whether we want a relationship.

But sometimes the deeper uncertainty is simpler:

Do I want this person differently, or do I just not want to lose the way they are already present in my life?

Those are not the same question.

When someone becomes familiar, their presence begins to disappear into the structure of the day.

A message.

A conversation.

A look.

A joke.

A small confidence shared privately.

Nothing individually dramatic.

Together, they create expectation.

Then expectation becomes attachment.

You do not notice the transition because nothing announces it.

There is no day when friendship officially crosses a line.

There is no alarm.

No system message.

You simply realize that their absence changes the day.

That is when classification begins.

What is this?

We like labels because labels promise control.

If it is friendship, behave like a friend.

If it is love, confess it.

If it is attraction, understand it as attraction.

If it is protective affection, keep the boundary.

The problem is that emotions do not always arrive separated into clean folders.

They overlap.

You can be attracted to someone and also genuinely care about the friendship.

You can feel protective without believing the other person needs protection.

You can enjoy being trusted and also become dependent on that trust.

You can want closeness without knowing what form that closeness should take.

This is where the mind starts doing something dangerous.

It turns feelings into a logic problem.

Evidence for love:

I think about her.

Evidence against:

She has a boyfriend.

Evidence for friendship:

We talk easily.

Evidence against:

Her attention affects me too much.

Evidence for something brotherly:

I care about what happens to her.

Evidence against:

I am attracted to her.

Then you sit there trying to solve yourself.

As if the correct category is hidden somewhere in the data.

I did this often when I was younger.

Feel first.

Analyze immediately afterward.

Sometimes even analyze instead of feeling.

I suspect engineers are especially vulnerable to this.

Ambiguity is uncomfortable.

We want state.

True or false.

Friend or lover.

Proceed or stop.

But human relationships often remain undefined for long periods without being broken.

The ambiguity itself can be the reality.

That does not mean ambiguity is always healthy.

It can become a way of avoiding decisions.

If one person wants a romantic relationship and the other does not, staying indefinitely in an undefined emotional space can hurt both people.

But not every unclear relationship needs to be forced into a declaration immediately.

Sometimes you really are still learning what the person means to you.

The difficulty in my old entry was made stronger by the fact that she already had a relationship.

That should have simplified the practical part.

There was a boundary.

Whatever I felt, I did not have the right to treat her existing relationship as an obstacle to solve.

That is much clearer to me now than some of my younger writing made it sound.

A person is not a prize sitting behind another person.

Attention is not a promise.

Trust is not romantic evidence.

Closeness is not an invitation.

These distinctions matter.

At twenty-something, however, attention can feel enormously meaningful.

Especially selective attention.

If someone seems to trust you more than others, your brain begins assigning significance.

Why me?

Does this mean something?

Am I different?

Maybe.

But "different" does not automatically mean "romantic."

People create many kinds of intimacy.

Some friendships are emotionally deeper than mediocre romantic relationships.

Some people tell one friend things they do not tell their partner.

That may reflect history, personality, safety, timing, or simply the specific shape of that friendship.

Not every intimacy is waiting to become romance.

This is an important lesson because popular stories teach the opposite.

Films often treat friendship between a man and a woman as an unfinished love story.

If they are close enough, one of them must secretly want more.

If they understand each other deeply, romance is presented as the logical conclusion.

Real life is less obedient.

Deep friendship can remain friendship.

Romantic attraction can exist without compatibility.

Trust can exist without desire.

Desire can exist without a future.

The categories overlap but do not collapse into one another.

The old version of me was struggling because I had several signals inside myself and wanted one answer.

Maybe there was no single answer.

Maybe I loved the attention.

Maybe I cared about her.

Maybe I found her attractive.

Maybe I valued being trusted.

Maybe I enjoyed feeling important to someone I admired.

All of those could be true at once.

The more interesting question is what one does with that mixture.

Feelings do not automatically create obligations.

You can feel something without acting on it.

That sentence took me a long time to appreciate.

When younger, I often treated a strong feeling as a problem requiring resolution.

If I liked someone, something needed to happen.

If I was confused, clarity needed to be produced.

If I was jealous, the jealousy must contain information.

Not necessarily.

A feeling can simply report the state of the person feeling it.

Jealousy may tell you that you are attached.

It does not tell you that you are entitled to anything.

Longing may tell you that someone matters.

It does not tell you that they should choose you.

Confusion may tell you that the relationship occupies several emotional roles.

It does not guarantee a hidden romantic truth.

This is where emotional maturity becomes less dramatic than romantic stories suggest.

Maturity is often the ability to experience an emotion without immediately converting it into a claim on another person.

I wish I had understood that earlier.

The line in my old entry that still feels most revealing was not about her beauty or the other men around her.

It was the fear that her attention might stop.

That fear says something about dependency.

Once a person's attention becomes part of your emotional stability, small changes become large.

A delayed message feels different.

A conversation that is shorter than usual feels different.

They spend more time with someone else.

They seem distracted.

Nothing objectively terrible has happened.

But the internal system detects loss.

This can lead to strange behavior.

You become more observant.

Too observant.

You notice frequencies.

Who wrote first.

How long the conversation lasted.

Whether the tone changed.

Emotional bookkeeping begins.

This is rarely healthy.

Human attention fluctuates.

People become busy.

Relationships shift.

A friendship cannot remain alive if one person secretly requires it to stay identical forever.

That was the contradiction in my wish.

I wanted her attention never to end.

But any real relationship changes.

Friendships become closer.

Then more distant.

Then close again.

People fall in love.

Move.

Change jobs.

Marry.

Become parents.

Disappear for months.

Return.

If closeness must remain constant to feel safe, it becomes fragile.

The desire for permanence can damage the very relationship it tries to preserve.

This is true beyond romance.

We do it with friends.

Colleagues.

Family.

Places.

Even versions of ourselves.

Do not change.

Stay like this.

Keep giving me the same thing.

Life does not sign that contract.

Maybe the real skill is not keeping every form of attention forever.

It is learning to value what exists without demanding that it remain unchanged.

That sounds easy when written calmly years later.

It is much harder when you are inside the feeling.

At the time, I was not philosophizing.

I was confused.

There was someone I cared about.

She was close to me.

She trusted me.

She mattered.

I could not tell what category to put that in.

And the possibility of losing the closeness frightened me.

That is a very human state.

It does not need to be mocked.

It only needs to be understood more carefully than I understood it then.

There is another subtle trap in relationships like this.

Being trusted can become part of your identity.

You become the one she tells things to.

The one who listens.

The reliable one.

The safe one.

That role feels good.

Not because it is manipulative.

Because being trusted feels meaningful.

But if your sense of value begins depending on remaining indispensable, care can quietly become possessive.

You may resent new relationships.

New friends.

New confidants.

Not because the other person has done anything wrong.

Because your role feels threatened.

That is the point where affection requires self-examination.

Do I want this person to be happy?

Or do I want to remain necessary?

Those answers are not always identical.

Healthy closeness needs room for the other person to have a life that does not revolve around us.

Again, obvious in theory.

Much harder in practice.

The old entry also mentioned that many people around her seemed interested in her.

That detail probably intensified everything.

Scarcity changes perception.

If many people want someone's attention and you receive more of it, the attention can feel more valuable.

Competition enters the emotional picture even if nobody agreed there was a competition.

This is another bad habit worth unlearning.

People are not rankings.

Someone else's interest does not increase or decrease another person's worth.

And intimacy is not a leaderboard.

Yet young social environments can make it feel that way.

Who is she talking to?

Who does she trust?

Who has a chance?

Who does not?

The language itself turns a person into a contested territory.

I would not describe it that way now.

The more useful perspective is simpler:

She had her own choices.

I had my feelings.

Those two realities were related but not identical.

My task was to understand and manage mine.

Not decode hers into an answer I preferred.

This is probably the central lesson I take from the entry now.

When you cannot tell what you feel, do not begin by interrogating the other person's behavior.

Begin with yourself.

What do I actually want?

A relationship?

More time together?

Reassurance?

Attention?

Exclusivity?

Physical attraction?

Emotional closeness?

To be chosen?

To avoid losing a friendship?

These are very different needs.

If you do not distinguish them, you may ask another person to solve a problem you have not even defined.

Sometimes the answer turns out to be uncomfortable.

You do not want a relationship.

You want certainty.

Or you do not want love.

You want to keep feeling important.

Or you do not want to be with the person.

You simply do not want someone else to become closer to them.

Those realizations are not flattering.

They are useful.

Self-knowledge is not always discovering noble motives.

Sometimes it is catching yourself being ordinary.

Jealous.

Insecure.

Dependent.

Afraid.

That is fine.

What matters is what you do after recognizing it.

The strangest part of rereading old emotional entries is seeing how urgent everything once felt.

A glance.

A message.

A morning greeting.

A change in attention.

Entire internal worlds built around tiny signals.

It would be easy to laugh at the younger version of myself.

I do not want to.

Those signals mattered because the relationships mattered.

The scale was different then.

Life had fewer layers.

One person's attention could occupy a larger portion of the emotional landscape.

As life expands, individual moments sometimes become smaller.

Not less meaningful.

Better contextualized.

That may be one definition of growing older.

Not feeling less.

Having more places to put the feeling.

Work.

Family.

Friendship.

Responsibility.

Memory.

Perspective.

The emotion no longer needs to explain the entire world.

So if I could answer the question hidden in my 2010 entry now—

Was it love, friendship, or something brotherly?—

I would probably say:

It does not matter as much as I thought.

It was attachment.

Care.

Attraction.

Trust.

Habit.

Fear of losing closeness.

Several things together.

A relationship does not become less real because it cannot be summarized in one word.

And a feeling does not become more legitimate because we give it a romantic name.

Sometimes the honest answer is simply:

This person matters to me, and I do not yet know in what way.

That is not failure.

It is a state.

The important part is what comes next.

Respect the other person's boundaries.

Do not turn attention into entitlement.

Do not confuse trust with a promise.

Do not demand permanence from something alive.

And do not be so frightened by ambiguity that you force a beautiful friendship into a shape it was never meant to have.

In 2010, the thing I feared most was that her attention might end.

Years later, I think the healthier wish would have been different:

Whatever this is,

let me appreciate it without needing to own its future.
