title: "A Feeling Does Not Require a Category"

In April 2010, I wrote about a woman whose place in my life I could not quite name. She had a boyfriend. We had become close, she trusted me, and I felt that she gave me more attention than she gave many of the other men around us.

I kept trying to decide what I felt. Friendship? Attraction? Something protective, almost brotherly? Each answer explained part of the experience and left something else out.

The clearest sentence came at the end: I wanted her attention never to end.

Looking back, that wish tells me more than the categories did. I valued the closeness and feared losing it. I did not yet know whether I wanted a different relationship or simply wanted the existing one to remain exactly as it was.

Those are different wishes, although they can feel similar from inside them. A familiar conversation becomes part of a day. A private joke gives the friendship a small history. Being trusted makes you feel that you have a particular place in someone's life. None of this needs to be dramatic to become important.

Then the person's absence changes the day, and the mind begins classifying.

I used to approach that uncertainty as a problem to solve. I think about her, so perhaps it is love. We talk easily, so perhaps it is friendship. I want to protect her, but I also find her attractive. I could keep organizing the evidence without getting any closer to an answer that helped me behave well.

The difficulty was partly in the question. Feelings do not always arrive separately. Attraction can coexist with affection, habit, admiration, and the pleasure of being important to someone. Finding one name does not necessarily make that mixture clearer.

There was, however, a practical boundary that did not depend on solving it: she was already in a relationship. Her trust did not create an obligation to return my feelings. The attention I enjoyed was not a promise that something romantic would follow.

That seems obvious when written calmly years later. At the time, selective attention could feel full of meaning. Why does she tell me this? Why does she seem comfortable with me? The temptation was to treat every answer as evidence for the outcome I hoped to find.

But intimacy has more than one form. A friendship can contain real trust and remain a friendship. Its value does not depend on becoming a stage in a romantic story. If I treated it that way, I risked missing what was already there while waiting for something else.

This is why I find the wish for permanent attention revealing. Once someone's attention becomes a measure of your own importance, small changes can feel large. A shorter conversation, a delayed message, or a new confidant begins to look like a loss of position. You start keeping accounts that the other person never agreed to keep.

That makes a living relationship answer to an impossible standard. People become busy, fall in love, change jobs, move, and find new friends. Even a close friendship cannot promise an identical amount of attention forever.

Being trusted can also become a role we are reluctant to release. The listener. The reliable one. The person who understands. Those can be generous ways to care for someone. They become harder to sustain generously when we need to remain indispensable.

The uncomfortable question is whether we want the other person to flourish or need their life to keep confirming our place in it. Usually our motives are mixed. Recognizing that mixture is more useful than giving it a flattering name.

I do not want to mock my younger self for being confused. The friendship mattered, and the possibility of losing it frightened him. He was trying to understand an experience for which his familiar categories were inadequate. There is nothing unusual about that.

What I would change is the direction of the inquiry. Instead of decoding her behavior for an answer about my future, I would ask what I actually wanted: a relationship, reassurance, exclusivity, or simply more time together. Each desire would require a different response. None could be settled by treating her choices as clues in a puzzle designed for me.

A feeling can be real before it is fully understood. It can also remain unacted upon. Jealousy may reveal attachment without revealing any entitlement. Longing may tell us that someone matters without telling us what they should do about it.

Sometimes clarity does require a conversation. Sometimes distance is needed because one person wants more than the friendship can offer. The point is not to preserve ambiguity indefinitely. It is to avoid making another person responsible for resolving every uncertainty inside us.

I can now leave the old question partly open. There was care, attraction, trust, habit, and a fear of losing closeness. A single label would make the account shorter, but perhaps less accurate.

I would ask less of that friendship's future. Whatever it was, I would want to appreciate it without requiring it to stay unchanged.
