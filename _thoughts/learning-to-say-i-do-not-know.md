---
title: 'I Do Not Know Is a Professional Skill'
date: 2014-10-19
tags:
  - learning

---

There was a time when saying "I do not know" felt like a small professional failure.

Technical work encourages this feeling. Someone asks a question in a meeting. A student waits for an answer. Experience creates expectations. The longer we have worked in a field, the more tempting it becomes to protect the appearance of competence.

So we improvise.

We answer too quickly. We hide uncertainty behind jargon. We speak with more confidence than the evidence deserves.

The immediate cost is small. The room keeps moving.

The later cost can be much larger.

A false answer enters a design decision. A student learns the wrong thing. A team spends hours investigating an assumption nobody was willing to label as an assumption.

This changed how I think about "I do not know."

It is not merely a confession of missing knowledge. In many situations, it is a way of protecting the quality of the next decision.

There is an important difference between ignorance and uncertainty.

Sometimes I truly do not know and need to learn. Sometimes I know several possibilities but cannot yet distinguish between them. Sometimes I remember something vaguely enough that I should verify it before treating memory as evidence.

Those states deserve different sentences.

"I do not know."

"I am not sure."

"I think it is X, but I want to check."

Precision about uncertainty is itself a form of competence.

The more experienced I become, the less impressive instant answers seem to me. A person who can say "I do not know" without defensiveness is often easier to trust than someone who always has an explanation ready.

This does not mean uncertainty should become a habit of avoidance. If the answer matters, the next sentence should usually be: "I will find out."

That is the part I once missed.

Professional confidence is not knowing everything.

It is being reliable about the boundary between what you know and what you do not.
