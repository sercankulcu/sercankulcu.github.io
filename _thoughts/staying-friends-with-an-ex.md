title: "Staying Friends with an Ex"
date: 2009-01-03

People often say it is mature to stay friends with an ex.

Maybe sometimes it is.

Maybe sometimes it works perfectly.

But there is a version of this arrangement that looks much better in theory than it feels in real life.

Imagine this.

You are in the same place.

Your ex is there.

Their new partner is there too.

Everybody is being civilized.

Everybody is behaving like adults.

Then the two of them start kissing in front of you.

That is a useful moment.

Not pleasant.

Useful.

Because suddenly all the abstract ideas disappear.

"We are still friends."

"I am completely over it."

"The past is the past."

"I only want them to be happy."

All of those sentences are tested at once.

The body usually answers faster than the mind.

Jealousy does not care about your carefully prepared philosophy.

Anger does not care that you promised yourself to be reasonable.

Memory does not care that the relationship officially ended months ago.

There is a difference between accepting that someone has moved on and watching them move on two meters away from you.

That difference can be large.

When a relationship ends, people like clean language.

We broke up.

We are friends now.

It sounds orderly.

A relationship was in one category.

Now it has been moved to another.

Like changing the label on a folder.

But people do not reorganize themselves that neatly.

History remains.

Habits remain.

Private jokes remain.

Old expectations remain.

The memory of touch remains.

Even if love is gone, familiarity can remain in the body much longer than expected.

That is why "friendship" after a relationship can become confusing.

Friends do not usually have to forget what it felt like to kiss each other.

Friends do not usually know exactly how the other person behaves in bed.

Friends do not usually carry a catalog of old arguments, reconciliations, promises and disappointments.

An ex is not simply a friend you happen to know very well.

There is a past attached.

Sometimes that past has become harmless.

Sometimes it has not.

The difficult part is that you may not know which one is true until something tests it.

You can believe you are fine for months.

Then you see them holding somebody else's hand.

Or hear them using a word they once used with you.

Or watch them laugh in a way that used to belong to your shared life.

Nothing objectively terrible has happened.

But suddenly the past is back in the room.

Years ago, my solution was not especially sophisticated.

Better to call the past the past and move on.

There are other people in the world.

Why insist on turning every former relationship into a lifelong friendship?

I still think there is something sensible in that.

Not every person who was important to you has to remain permanently present.

We sometimes confuse preserving contact with preserving meaning.

They are not the same thing.

A relationship can have mattered enormously and still end completely.

You do not need to exchange birthday messages forever to prove that the years were real.

You do not need occasional coffee to honor the past.

Sometimes distance is not bitterness.

It is structure.

It creates enough space for two people to become separate again.

That can be necessary.

Especially when one person moves on faster than the other.

"Let's stay friends" sounds fair when both people mean the same thing.

But often they do not.

For one person it may mean:

I care about you and would genuinely like a different kind of relationship.

For the other it may mean:

I am not ready to lose you completely.

Those are very different arrangements using the same word.

That is where trouble begins.

One person starts dating.

The other is still emotionally waiting.

One person shares details about the new relationship because friends share things.

The other listens because friends are supposed to listen.

Everyone follows the rules.

Only one person is getting hurt.

Friendship is not automatically kinder than distance.

Sometimes distance is the kinder option.

It lets expectations die.

It removes accidental hope.

It prevents ordinary friendliness from being interpreted as a possibility.

A message is just a message when enough time has passed.

Too early, it can become evidence.

"She wrote to me."

"He asked how I was."

"Maybe..."

That word can keep a finished relationship alive for a very long time.

Maybe.

Maybe they miss me.

Maybe the new person will not last.

Maybe we will try again.

Maybe this friendship is actually the beginning of something else.

Hope is not always positive.

Sometimes it simply delays an ending.

Of course, some people really do become friends.

Enough time passes.

The romantic part disappears.

Both people build other lives.

The history becomes something they can look at without wanting to return to it.

Then friendship may become possible.

But I think the sequence matters.

First become separate people.

Then see whether friendship still makes sense.

Trying to skip that separation can make the transition unnecessarily painful.

There is also pride.

Nobody wants to look jealous.

Especially after saying:

"I am happy for you."

So people perform calmness.

They smile.

They behave politely.

They insist that nothing bothers them.

Then go home and discover that the performance ended but the feeling did not.

There is no prize for being the most civilized person in a room that is hurting you.

Leaving can be mature too.

So can deciding not to put yourself in that situation again.

We often think maturity means tolerating discomfort without complaint.

Sometimes maturity means recognizing unnecessary discomfort and removing it.

The old version of me expressed this much more aggressively.

If your ex and their new partner start making out in front of you, friendship can quickly become something capable of ending in broken noses and family feuds.

That was exaggeration.

Mostly.

But the emotion behind it was real.

Some situations ask too much from people.

Why create them if you do not need to?

The phrase "stay friends" sounds beautiful because it suggests nothing was lost.

Love becomes friendship.

Nobody disappears.

Everybody remains in each other's lives.

It is a comforting idea.

Real endings are less comfortable.

Sometimes a person simply leaves your daily life.

You stop knowing what they ate for dinner.

You stop knowing where they went at the weekend.

You stop hearing about their problems.

Then one day you realize you no longer know who they are dating.

Later, you may not even care.

That distance can feel cruel at first.

But it is also how lives separate.

And sometimes separation is exactly what a breakup needs.

Not hatred.

Not revenge.

Not dramatic final speeches.

Just enough distance for the word "ex" to become a description of the past instead of a strange form of the present.

I do not think everyone should cut all contact with every former partner.

There are no universal rules for relationships.

But I do think friendship should be real friendship.

Not surveillance.

Not waiting.

Not guilt.

Not an excuse to remain nearby.

Not a way to avoid admitting that something ended.

If seeing them happy with someone else makes you want to destroy the furniture, perhaps friendship can wait.

There are other people to be friends with.

And there is nothing wrong with allowing the past to become the past.
