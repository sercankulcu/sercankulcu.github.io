People often say it is mature to stay friends with an ex. Maybe sometimes it is. Maybe sometimes it works perfectly. But there is a version of this arrangement that looks much better in theory than it feels in real life.

Imagine being in the same place with your ex and their new partner. Everybody is civilized. Everybody behaves like an adult. Then the two of them start kissing in front of you. That is a useful moment—not pleasant, useful—because all the abstract sentences are tested at once: *We are still friends. I am completely over it. The past is the past. I only want them to be happy.*

The body often answers faster than the mind. Jealousy does not care about your carefully prepared philosophy. Memory does not care that the relationship officially ended months ago. There is a difference between accepting that someone has moved on and watching them move on two meters away from you.

When a relationship ends, people like clean language. *We broke up. We are friends now.* It sounds orderly, as if a relationship has simply been moved from one folder to another. But people do not reorganize themselves that neatly. History remains. Habits remain. Private jokes, old expectations, the memory of touch, and the reflex of caring may remain long after the label has changed.

That is why an ex is not simply a friend you happen to know very well. There is a past attached, and sometimes you do not know whether that past has become harmless until something tests it. You can believe you are fine for months and then see them holding somebody else's hand, hear them use a familiar word, or watch them laugh in a way that belonged to your shared life. Nothing objectively terrible has happened, but suddenly the past is back in the room.

Years ago, my solution was not especially sophisticated: better to call the past the past and move on. I still think there is something sensible in that. Not every person who was important to you has to remain permanently present. We sometimes confuse preserving contact with preserving meaning, but they are not the same thing. A relationship can have mattered enormously and still end completely.

You do not need birthday messages forever to prove that the years were real. You do not need occasional coffee to honor the past. Sometimes distance is not bitterness; it is structure. It creates enough space for two people to become separate again, especially when one person moves on faster than the other.

"Let's stay friends" also becomes dangerous when the two people mean different things by it. For one person it may mean, *I care about you and would genuinely like a different kind of relationship.* For the other it may mean, *I am not ready to lose you completely.* Those are very different arrangements using the same word.

That is where ordinary friendliness can become evidence. A message is just a message when enough time has passed. Too early, it can become: *She wrote to me. He asked how I was. Maybe...* That word can keep a finished relationship alive for a long time. Hope is not always positive. Sometimes it simply delays an ending.

Of course, some people really do become friends. Enough time passes. The romantic part disappears. Both people build other lives, and the history becomes something they can look at without wanting to return to it. Then friendship may become possible. But I think the sequence matters: first become separate people, then see whether friendship still makes sense.

There is also pride. Nobody wants to look jealous after saying, "I am happy for you." So people perform calmness. They smile, behave politely, insist that nothing bothers them, and then go home and discover that the performance ended but the feeling did not. There is no prize for being the most civilized person in a room that is hurting you. Leaving can be mature too.

The phrase "stay friends" sounds beautiful because it suggests nothing was lost. Love becomes friendship, nobody disappears, everybody remains in each other's lives. Real endings are less comfortable. Sometimes a person simply leaves your daily life. You stop knowing what they ate for dinner, where they went at the weekend, and eventually who they are dating. Later, you may not even care.

That distance can feel cruel at first, but it is also how lives separate. Sometimes separation is exactly what a breakup needs: not hatred, not revenge, not a dramatic final speech, just enough distance for the word *ex* to become a description of the past instead of a strange form of the present.

I do not think everyone should cut all contact with every former partner. There are no universal rules for relationships. But friendship should be real friendship—not surveillance, waiting, guilt, accidental hope, or an excuse to remain nearby.

If seeing them happy with someone else still makes the room feel dangerous, perhaps friendship can wait. There is nothing wrong with allowing the past to become the past.
