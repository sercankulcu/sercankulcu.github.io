Why do we find one person beautiful and remain almost indifferent to another person who may be equally beautiful by ordinary standards?

This question looks simple until we try to answer it honestly.

We can talk about symmetry, eyes, hair, skin, height, body shape, age, style, or facial proportions. We can create categories and measure similarities. Yet attraction often refuses to behave like a mathematical formula. Two women can look very different, and still create the same kind of interest in the same observer. At the same time, two women who seem physically similar may produce completely different reactions.

I started thinking about this after looking at a group of Turkish actresses whom I find attractive: Selma Ergeç, Ayça Ayşin Turan, Sera Tokdemir, Burcu Özberk, Pelin Akil, Hilal Altınbilek, Funda Eryiğit, Hazal Subaşı, Merve Dizdar, Gökçe Bahadır, Pınar Deniz, Cansu Dere, Burçin Terzioğlu, Selin Şekerci, Gizem Karaca, Devrim Özkan, Berfin Nilsu Aktaş, Melisa Aslı Pamuk, İlayda Akdoğan, Aslı Enver, Gonca Vuslateri, Aybüke Pusat, Sedef Avcı, Pelin Karahan, Alina Boz, Hayal Köseoğlu, Damla Sönmez, Ebru Şahin, Aslıhan Gürbüz, Şükran Ovalı, Seray Kaya, Melisa Döngel, Biran Damla Yılmaz, and Tülin Özen.

At first sight, this is too wide a group to describe as one physical type. That is exactly what makes it interesting.

If all the women looked almost the same, there would be little to examine. We could simply say that a certain hair color, face shape, or body type creates attraction. But this list contains women with very different faces, ages, styles, proportions, expressions, and public images. The common ground is not a single physical feature. It is something more difficult to name.

Perhaps a personal idea of beauty is not a picture.

Perhaps it is a pattern.

## The Classical and Composed Face

Some women in this group are close to what we may call classical beauty. Their faces create a strong sense of balance. Selma Ergeç, Cansu Dere, Sedef Avcı, Melisa Aslı Pamuk, Gizem Karaca, and Aybüke Pusat belong partly to this group.

They do not look identical.

Selma Ergeç has a refined and unusual elegance. Cansu Dere often gives a cooler and more distant impression. Sedef Avcı has a softer, calmer form of beauty. Melisa Aslı Pamuk has a more glamorous presence. Gizem Karaca has a bright and polished appearance, while Aybüke Pusat combines elegance with a more athletic and modern energy.

What connects them is not the exact shape of the nose or the distance between the eyes.

It is the feeling of visual order.

There is a kind of beauty that makes the eye rest. The parts seem to belong together. Nothing has to fight for attention. The face feels complete before we begin to study its details.

This is close to an old philosophical idea. For many thinkers in the classical tradition, beauty was connected with proportion, order, and harmony. A beautiful object was beautiful because its parts formed a meaningful whole.

That idea still explains part of attraction.

We often notice harmony before we consciously notice individual features.

But it cannot explain the whole list.

If harmony were enough, people with the most regular faces would always be the most attractive.

They are not.

## Beauty with Force

Another group has a different quality.

Their beauty is not quiet.

It has force.

Hilal Altınbilek, Ebru Şahin, Pınar Deniz, Devrim Özkan, Hazal Subaşı, Seray Kaya, Biran Damla Yılmaz, and Berfin Nilsu Aktaş often create this stronger visual impression.

Again, they are not the same type.

Hilal Altınbilek has a highly defined, almost dramatic form of femininity. Ebru Şahin can give a more earthy and powerful impression. Pınar Deniz has a direct, intelligent intensity in her face. Devrim Özkan often combines softness with a clear and confident gaze. Hazal Subaşı can appear delicate in one image and very sharp in another. Seray Kaya carries a more mature strength. Biran Damla Yılmaz has a highly expressive face, while Berfin Nilsu Aktaş often has a younger but still striking presence.

These women suggest that beauty is sometimes connected with visual tension.

A face can be attractive because it is harmonious, but it can also be attractive because something in it refuses to disappear into harmony.

Strong eyebrows, unusual eyes, a sharper jaw, a serious expression, or simply an intense gaze can create this effect.

This is an important difference between being pretty and being memorable.

A pretty face can be pleasant.

A memorable face stays in the mind.

The observer does not only see it.

He returns to it mentally.

## Warmth and Familiar Femininity

Burcu Özberk, Pelin Akil, Aslı Enver, Pelin Karahan, Sera Tokdemir, and Selin Şekerci suggest another side of the pattern.

There is warmth in this group.

Burcu Özberk can move easily between polished beauty and an open, lively expression. Pelin Akil often has a strong face, yet her expressions can make that strength feel familiar rather than distant. Aslı Enver has a softness that makes beauty feel less formal. Pelin Karahan carries a calm and traditional femininity. Sera Tokdemir can combine a strong appearance with emotional warmth. Selin Şekerci has a more playful and energetic face.

The attraction here seems to depend partly on movement and expression.

This matters because photographs often mislead us about beauty.

A face is not really a still object.

In ordinary life it moves.

It changes while listening, smiling, becoming serious, feeling embarrassed, thinking, laughing, or becoming angry.

Some people are more attractive in motion than in a perfectly controlled photograph. Their beauty lives in the transition between expressions.

This may be one reason why the eyes are so important in attraction. We do not only look at their color or shape.

We read them.

We try to understand whether the person behind them is amused, curious, tired, distant, kind, suspicious, confident, or vulnerable.

A face becomes more attractive when it appears to contain a life.

## The Expressive and Unconventional Group

The most revealing names in the list may be Funda Eryiğit, Merve Dizdar, Tülin Özen, Aslıhan Gürbüz, Gonca Vuslateri, Hayal Köseoğlu, Damla Sönmez, and Şükran Ovalı.

Why are they revealing?

Because they make a simple theory of conventional beauty impossible.

Funda Eryiğit has a face with unusual depth and seriousness. Merve Dizdar is highly expressive; the personality of the face can be more important than classical perfection. Tülin Özen has an intelligent and mature presence that becomes more interesting the longer one looks. Aslıhan Gürbüz has strength, confidence, and a distinctive facial character. Gonca Vuslateri has an expressive and sometimes unpredictable visual energy. Hayal Köseoğlu can move between softness, eccentricity, and intensity. Damla Sönmez has a delicate but very recognizable face. Şükran Ovalı has a natural, grounded quality that does not depend on looking artificial or perfectly arranged.

These women are important because they show that difference itself can be attractive.

A face does not need to approach an ideal model in order to be beautiful.

Sometimes the feature that prevents a face from becoming generic is the same feature that gives it power.

An unusual nose can create character.

A strong jaw can create presence.

Slight asymmetry can make expressions more interesting.

A face that changes greatly according to emotion can become much more attractive than a face that remains perfect from every angle.

There is a useful paradox here:

Perfection can increase beauty, but too much perfection can reduce identity.

If every feature moves toward the same ideal, individual faces may begin to look more similar. The observer receives beauty, but less information about the person.

This does not mean that conventional beauty is empty.

It means that attraction may need both order and difference.

## Youthful and Delicate Beauty

Ayça Ayşin Turan, Alina Boz, İlayda Akdoğan, and Melisa Döngel show another direction in the list.

Their appeal can be lighter, younger, or more delicate compared with the stronger and more mature presence of Cansu Dere, Funda Eryiğit, Gökçe Bahadır, Tülin Özen, or Burçin Terzioğlu.

Ayça Ayşin Turan often has a soft and almost dreamlike quality. Alina Boz can combine delicacy with a clear facial structure. İlayda Akdoğan has a natural, youthful individuality. Melisa Döngel has a brighter and more openly glamorous form of young femininity.

This group prevents another simple conclusion.

The preference is not only for mature or severe faces.

There is room for softness.

But even here, complete innocence does not appear to be the main theme. Each of these women has some visual feature or expression that gives the face a recognizable identity.

Softness alone is not enough.

It still needs character.

## Mature Presence

Gökçe Bahadır and Burçin Terzioğlu deserve special attention because they show what happens when beauty is joined by maturity.

Their attraction cannot be reduced to youth.

Their faces carry time, experience, confidence, and self-possession. They can look elegant without looking fragile. They can look feminine without needing to appear innocent.

Pelin Akil, Tülin Özen, Funda Eryiğit, Aslıhan Gürbüz, Selma Ergeç, and Şükran Ovalı also fit this broader category in different ways.

This suggests an important idea:

Youth and beauty are not the same thing.

Modern visual culture often treats youth as if it were the main source of female beauty. Smooth skin, small signs of age, and a certain kind of freshness are presented as goals.

But attraction is more complicated.

A face can gain something as it loses youth.

Confidence may replace innocence.

Character may replace smoothness.

Presence may become stronger.

Time does not only take things from a face.

It also writes things into it.

For someone attracted to this list, beauty seems able to survive change.

It may even become more interesting through change.

## A Face Is Not a Collection of Parts

It is tempting to turn attraction into a checklist.

Dark hair: yes.

Large eyes: yes.

Strong eyebrows: yes.

Certain height: yes.

Certain body type: yes.

But the list does not support such a simple model.

The women differ too much.

The better explanation is holistic.

A face is attractive because of the relationship between its parts, not because each part passes an independent test.

A large nose may look beautiful on one face and unsuitable on another. The same is true for lips, eyebrows, cheekbones, jawlines, and even hairstyles.

A feature has no complete meaning outside the face that contains it.

This is similar to music.

A single note is neither beautiful nor ugly in the same way that a melody is.

Its meaning depends on what comes before it, what comes after it, and what sounds around it.

A face is also a composition.

This is why attempts to create beauty by copying isolated features can sometimes fail.

A fashionable nose, fashionable lips, fashionable eyebrows, and fashionable cheekbones do not automatically create a more attractive whole. They may even remove the differences that made the original face interesting.

Beauty does not always come from having the best individual parts.

It often comes from having parts that belong to each other.

## The Importance of Contrast

Looking across these women, another pattern appears:

contrast.

Softness beside strength.

Warmth beside distance.

Symmetry beside irregularity.

Elegance beside earthiness.

Youth beside maturity.

This may explain why very different women can belong to the same personal map of attraction.

Consider Cansu Dere and Merve Dizdar.

They are not obvious members of one physical category.

Yet both can create a powerful presence.

The route is different, but the result is similar.

Consider Burcu Özberk and Funda Eryiğit.

One may appear closer to bright, conventional femininity; the other may feel more serious, intellectual, or unconventional.

Yet both can hold attention because their faces are not empty surfaces.

They communicate.

Consider Hilal Altınbilek and Tülin Özen.

One can represent highly visible glamour; the other a quieter and more mature individuality.

Again, the physical language differs, but both have identity.

Consider Ayça Ayşin Turan and Aslıhan Gürbüz.

One can feel delicate, the other powerful.

If both are attractive to the same person, the preference cannot simply be "soft women" or "strong women."

The deeper preference may be for women whose appearance has a clear internal logic.

This is why personal taste can look inconsistent from outside while remaining very consistent from inside.

The observer is not choosing one type of face.

He is responding to one type of effect.

## Beauty and Presence

I think the central word is presence.

Presence is difficult to measure because it is not one physical feature.

It is the feeling that a person occupies visual and emotional space.

Some faces are technically beautiful but strangely easy to forget.

Others may contain obvious irregularities yet remain in memory for years.

Presence can come from the eyes.

It can come from posture.

It can come from the way someone smiles only slightly rather than completely.

It can come from confidence, reserve, humor, seriousness, or the impression of intelligence.

Of course, we should be careful here.

We cannot know a person's real personality simply by looking at her face.

Public figures are also seen through acting roles, interviews, styling, photography, lighting, and cultural expectations. What we call "presence" is partly created by the observer.

But that does not make it unreal.

Attraction always happens between what is there and what we perceive.

## Plato's Problem: Is Beauty Something We Recognize?

Plato often treated visible beauty as a sign of something beyond the individual beautiful object.

In a simplified reading, we recognize beautiful things because they participate in a more general idea of Beauty.

There is something attractive about this thought.

When we say that many very different people are beautiful, we seem to assume that there is some invisible quality they share.

We cannot point to one nose, one eye shape, or one body type.

Yet we still use the same word.

Perhaps this is why beauty feels like recognition.

We see a face and sometimes react before we can explain why.

But the list also creates a problem for Plato.

If there is one perfect form of beauty, why is individual taste so different?

Why can one person's extraordinary beauty leave another person almost unmoved?

The answer may be that beauty is partly universal and partly personal.

Humans may share some preferences for health, balance, expression, and proportion.

But experience adds another layer.

Memory, culture, past relationships, childhood, films, social environment, and personal associations slowly create an individual map.

We do not meet beauty with an empty mind.

We bring our history with us.

## Hume's Problem: Is Beauty in the Observer?

David Hume argued that beauty is not simply a quality inside objects.

It exists in the mind that experiences them.

This idea explains personal taste much better.

The same face can be ordinary to one person and unforgettable to another.

Neither reaction can be fully understood by measuring the face.

Yet saying "beauty is subjective" is also too easy.

It ends the discussion exactly where the interesting questions begin.

Subjective does not mean random.

This list is evidence of that.

The women are different, but the choices form patterns: expressive eyes, recognizable faces, a balance between femininity and strength, attraction to both mature presence and youthful softness, and an unusual tolerance — perhaps even a preference — for features that create individuality.

Taste has structure even when it has no strict rules.

A useful comparison is language.

We speak freely, but not randomly.

There are patterns below the surface.

We may not consciously know the grammar, yet we follow it.

Attraction may have a private grammar too.

## Kant's Distance and the Problem of Desire

Kant made an important distinction between appreciating beauty and simply wanting something.

This distinction matters when thinking about physical attraction.

To call someone attractive can mean, "I desire this person."

But to call someone beautiful can also mean, "There is something in this appearance that I enjoy observing even without any practical goal."

The two experiences often overlap, but they are not identical.

A person may recognize that someone is conventionally beautiful without feeling attraction.

Another person may create strong attraction without fitting the observer's abstract idea of perfect beauty.

This list seems to live in the space between the two.

The women are not attractive merely because they satisfy a standard.

They produce attention.

The eye wants to return.

That return is important.

Perhaps attraction is less about immediate perfection and more about the desire to look again.

A face that reveals everything in one second may be beautiful.

A face that seems slightly different each time can become fascinating.

## Beauty as a Family Resemblance

The philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein used the idea of "family resemblance" to describe categories whose members do not all share one single feature.

Games are a famous example.

Chess, football, children's games, card games, and video games are all called games, but there may be no single property shared by every one of them.

Instead, there is a network of overlapping similarities.

Personal beauty may work in the same way.

There is no one feature shared equally by Selma Ergeç, Burcu Özberk, Merve Dizdar, Pınar Deniz, Alina Boz, Funda Eryiğit, Cansu Dere, Ebru Şahin, Aslı Enver, and Tülin Özen.

But there are overlapping chains.

One woman shares elegance with another.

Another shares expressive eyes with a third.

A third shares mature confidence with a fourth.

A fourth shares facial individuality with a fifth.

A fifth shares softness with a sixth.

No single bridge connects everyone, yet the network remains coherent.

This may be the best model for a personal type.

A "type" is not necessarily one woman copied many times.

It can be a family of visual possibilities.

## What This Taste Seems to Reject

Sometimes a preference becomes clearer when we ask what it does not require.

This group does not require one age.

It does not require one hair color.

It does not require one face shape.

It does not require extreme thinness or one exact body form.

It does not require a permanently glamorous style.

It does not require perfect symmetry.

Most importantly, it does not require the face to be generic.

This last point may be the strongest.

The list includes women who fit conventional beauty ideals very easily and women whose attraction depends more on expression, individuality, acting presence, maturity, or unusual facial character.

So the standard is broad but not careless.

It accepts variety, but not emptiness.

## The Difference Between Beautiful and Attractive

I keep returning to this distinction.

Beauty can be admired from a distance.

Attraction feels directional.

It pulls attention toward a person.

A perfectly balanced face may be beautiful but emotionally silent.

Another face may be less perfect but more alive.

This is why attraction often survives small flaws.

In fact, the word "flaw" becomes questionable.

A feature is only a flaw compared with an external standard.

Inside the total face, the same feature may be essential.

Remove it, and the face may become more correct but less itself.

This idea matters far beyond physical appearance.

Human beings often make the mistake of treating ideals as if they were recipes.

We assume that if we maximize every desirable quality, the result must become better.

But many valuable things depend on tension rather than maximum values.

A story with no conflict is boring.

Music with no silence becomes noise.

A personality with no contradiction may feel artificial.

A face with no irregularity may sometimes lose mystery.

Beauty can exist not despite imperfection but through the relationship between perfection and imperfection.

## The Ethics of Looking

There is also an ethical question hidden inside any discussion of physical beauty.

When we analyze faces, we risk reducing people to objects.

A person is obviously more than the observer's attraction to her eyes, body, hair, or expression.

This is especially important with actresses.

Their public image gives us the illusion that we know them.

We see hundreds of photographs, interviews, scenes, and social media posts.

Familiarity begins to feel like knowledge.

But it is not knowledge of the complete person.

So a serious philosophy of attraction should include humility.

To find someone beautiful says something about the observer's perception.

It does not define the person being observed.

The women in this list do not exist as examples of my theory.

The theory exists because my mind notices a pattern in its own responses to their public appearances.

That distinction matters.

Beauty is experienced in another person, but the experience also reveals something about ourselves.

## What Does This List Say About My Idea of Beauty?

After looking at the names together, I do not think my idea of beauty can be described as a simple preference for brunettes, tall women, thin women, young women, mature women, soft faces, or strong faces.

All of these descriptions explain some names and fail with others.

A better description would be this:

I am attracted to women whose appearance has identity.

The face should not merely be correct.

It should say something.

I seem to value expressive eyes, but not one exact eye shape.

I value femininity, but femininity can be soft like Ayça Ayşin Turan, bright like Burcu Özberk, elegant like Selma Ergeç, dramatic like Hilal Altınbilek, grounded like Şükran Ovalı, intense like Pınar Deniz, mature like Gökçe Bahadır, unconventional like Funda Eryiğit, or highly expressive like Merve Dizdar.

I seem to value beauty that can carry strength without losing softness.

This explains why Pelin Akil can belong beside Aslı Enver, why Cansu Dere can belong beside Gonca Vuslateri, why Melisa Aslı Pamuk can belong beside Tülin Özen, and why Ebru Şahin can belong beside İlayda Akdoğan.

The similarities are not always anatomical.

Sometimes they are relational qualities: balance, intensity, warmth, confidence, distinctiveness, expression.

Perhaps the most important preference is for a face that feels inhabited.

Not simply a beautiful surface, but a surface through which some inner life seems visible.

Of course, that inner life may partly be my projection.

This is unavoidable.

Attraction contains imagination.

We never see only the person in front of us.

We also see what the person reminds us of, what we expect, what we fear, what we desire, and what stories our mind begins to create.

## Beauty as Recognition Rather Than Measurement

I began with the question of why very different women can be attractive to the same person.

My answer is now almost the opposite of where I started.

Perhaps attraction is not produced by identifying the correct features.

Perhaps the features are noticed after attraction has already happened.

First comes recognition.

Then comes explanation.

We see someone and feel that something works.

Only later do we say it must be the eyes, the hair, the smile, the jawline, the voice, the body, or the style.

But these explanations may be attempts to translate an immediate experience into language.

The experience itself is more complete than the explanation.

This is why beauty remains philosophically interesting.

If it were only geometry, we would have solved it long ago.

If it were completely random, there would be nothing to discuss.

Instead, beauty exists somewhere between structure and freedom.

There are patterns, but they are flexible.

There are standards, but individuals escape them.

There is biology, but also memory.

There is culture, but also private taste.

There is the visible face, but also the imagined person behind it.

And there is one final contradiction that I like.

We often search for our "type" as if somewhere there were a perfect person who contains every feature we prefer.

But a long list of real attractions suggests something else.

Perhaps what we truly love about beauty is not repetition but variation around a hidden theme.

Selma Ergeç is not Cansu Dere.

Cansu Dere is not Merve Dizdar.

Merve Dizdar is not Burcu Özberk.

Burcu Özberk is not Funda Eryiğit.

Funda Eryiğit is not Hilal Altınbilek.

Hilal Altınbilek is not Ayça Ayşin Turan.

And that is exactly the point.

If they were all versions of the same face, the idea of beauty would become smaller.

Instead, each one demonstrates a different way in which a face can become compelling.

Maybe personal taste is not a prison with narrow walls.

Maybe it is a landscape with certain paths that we repeatedly choose.

We recognize elegance on one path, intensity on another, warmth on another, maturity on another, and difference on another.

The paths do not look the same, but they keep leading us toward the same feeling:

Look again.

Perhaps that is the simplest definition of attraction I can give.

Beauty is what pleases the eye.

Attraction is what asks the eye to return.
