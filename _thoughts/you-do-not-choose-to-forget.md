Years ago, I wrote a single sentence about forgetting: **Forgetting is not something a person can do. It is something that happens to a person.** I still think there is truth in that.

We talk about forgetting as if it were an action. *Forget her. Forget him. Forget what happened. Move on.* The language makes it sound almost like closing a window on a computer: click, done. But memory does not work like that. You cannot usually decide that a person, place, sentence, or period of your life has occupied enough space in your head and issue a delete command.

In fact, trying to forget can strengthen the memory. You check whether you have forgotten it, and that check becomes remembering. *Do I still think about it?* There it is again. This is why advice about forgetting often feels useless. People mean well, but none of the usual sentences explains how forgetting actually happens.

There is no reliable method. There is time, distance, repetition, replacement, new routines, new people, and whatever private process the mind uses to decide which details it still needs.

Some memories disappear without resistance. You forget what you ate three days ago, a phone number you once used daily, or the name of someone you met once. Other memories refuse to leave: a street, a smell, a song, the expression on someone's face, a message, a sentence that should have been ordinary. Memory has its own priorities, and they are rarely ours.

Sometimes forgetting happens so slowly that you do not notice it. A person who once occupied your mind every hour becomes someone you think about once a day, then once a week. One day something reminds you of them and you realize that months have passed. You did not decide to forget. You were busy living, and the memory changed its position while you were looking elsewhere.

Then another strange thing can happen. For a long time you wanted the memory to weaken; when it finally does, you may want it back. The exact sound of a voice becomes harder to retrieve. A room blurs. A face loses detail. Forgetting is not only relief. It is also loss.

Perhaps this is why people save old messages, photographs, tickets, letters, and objects that would be worthless to anyone else. They function as external memory: evidence that something happened, that we were there, that a person existed in our life, that a version of ourselves once felt this way.

Memory itself is not an archive. It edits, compresses, combines, exaggerates, and deletes. Unlike a computer, it rarely asks before saving changes. Songs and places sometimes preserve fragments especially well. A melody can return an entire room for a few seconds. A street corner can make the body recognize something before the mind can explain it.

Maybe that is how memory survives best: not as a continuous record, but as triggers. A word. A smell. A melody. A wall. A city. A person.

This is why trying to erase the past may be the wrong goal. We cannot control which fragments return. We can only change what happens when they do. At first, a memory may ruin the day. Later it hurts for an hour. Later still, it becomes something we can look at without being pulled inside it.

The memory did not disappear. Its weight changed.

That may be what people really mean when they say they have forgotten someone. Usually they have not forgotten the name or the story. What disappeared was the urgency: the need to check, understand, rewrite the ending, or let the memory control the room.

Complete forgetting is not always possible, and perhaps not always desirable. Some memories remain because they remind us what mattered, what we misunderstood, what we lost, what we survived, and what we should not repeat.

The problem begins when we demand obedience from memory: keep this, remove that, preserve that summer, erase this person. Human memory does not accept instructions so neatly. It has its own schedule.

So I still believe the old sentence. Forgetting is not something you do. It happens—usually while you are occupied with something else, usually later than you want, and sometimes earlier than you expected.
