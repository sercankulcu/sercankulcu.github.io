title: "When the Gold Paint Comes Off"
date: 2009-01-05

There is a kind of sadness that comes from learning too much about something you once admired.

Not because the truth is always terrible.

Sometimes the truth is simply ordinary.

And ordinary can be surprisingly destructive when you have spent years looking at something from a distance.

In January 2009, I wrote about a sentence that had stayed with me:

Do not touch your idols; the gold paint will remain on your hands.

In my old note, I associated the line with *Madame Bovary* and Gustave Flaubert.

What mattered to me then was not literary attribution.

It was the image.

An idol looks solid from far away.

Permanent.

Golden.

Something worth looking up to.

Then you get close.

You touch it.

And discover that some of the gold was only paint.

Now your hand carries proof.

That image seemed to explain something I had already begun to understand about people, success, love, and even the stories we tell ourselves.

Sometimes distance protects beauty.

We usually say that knowledge is good.

More information.

More truth.

More realism.

See things as they really are.

This sounds mature.

It sounds intelligent.

It is also incomplete.

Because truth has a cost.

There are things we survive partly because we do not know everything about them.

A person we admire.

A relationship we remember.

A place from childhood.

A successful organization.

A teacher.

An artist.

A hero.

A moment when we believed something good was possible.

We build these things partly from reality and partly from distance.

Distance removes detail.

And detail is where imperfection lives.

From far away, a person can represent courage.

Get closer and you discover insecurity, vanity, selfishness, inconsistency, boredom, jealousy, laziness, or just the ordinary weaknesses shared by almost everyone.

None of these automatically erase what was admirable.

But they change the picture.

The symbol becomes a person.

That transition can hurt.

When we are young, we often need symbols more than we realize.

Someone succeeds.

Therefore success is possible.

Someone behaves honorably.

Therefore goodness exists.

Someone creates beautiful work.

Therefore beauty can survive in the world.

We collect examples.

Not because those people are perfect, but because we need evidence.

This person proves something.

That is a heavy role to assign to a stranger.

They do not know they are carrying it.

Then one day we learn something disappointing about them.

The disappointment feels larger than the fact itself.

Why?

Because a person has not merely failed.

Evidence has failed.

If this person was not what I believed, what else have I misunderstood?

That is the dangerous question.

It explains why disillusionment can spread.

One disappointment rarely stays in its assigned area.

You discover that someone you admired was deeply flawed.

Then the mind begins auditing everything.

Was the achievement real?

Was the kindness real?

Was any of it real?

This is where excessive realism can become its own kind of distortion.

We imagine that seeing flaws means seeing the whole truth.

It does not.

Cynicism is not automatically more accurate than admiration.

A person can be selfish and generous.

Brilliant and foolish.

Courageous in one situation and cowardly in another.

A terrible partner and an extraordinary teacher.

A great artist and a difficult human being.

Reality does not organize people into clean categories.

We do that.

First we create the idol.

Then, when the idol disappoints us, we are tempted to create the villain.

Both versions are easier than accepting a person.

Maybe the gold paint metaphor is not really a warning against knowledge.

Maybe it is a warning against constructing idols in the first place.

If you require someone to remain perfect in order to remain meaningful, you have made admiration fragile.

The first scratch destroys it.

But perhaps admiration can survive contact with reality.

That is a more difficult form.

You can say:

This person taught me something important.

And also:

This person had serious flaws.

Both can be true.

You can say:

That period of my life was beautiful.

And also:

I was miserable in parts of it.

Both can be true.

You can love what something represented without pretending the entire thing was gold.

This is especially difficult with memories.

Memory performs its own restoration work.

It removes background noise.

It softens unpleasant details.

It changes scale.

A childhood place becomes larger.

An old friendship becomes simpler.

A lost relationship becomes either more beautiful or more terrible than it really was.

We preserve selected frames.

Years later, returning can be dangerous.

The street is smaller.

The building is uglier.

The person has changed.

Or perhaps they have not changed at all and you finally see what was always there.

That can feel like loss.

This is why people sometimes say:

I wish I had not learned that.

A strange sentence.

We usually think ignorance is something to escape.

Yet there are truths that arrive too late to help and just early enough to damage a memory.

Would it have been better not to know?

There is no universal answer.

Sometimes yes.

Sometimes absolutely not.

There are facts we need because they protect us.

If someone is dangerous, dishonest, abusive, corrupt, or untrustworthy, knowing matters.

Idealization can become a trap.

Refusing to see reality can keep us attached to people and institutions that do not deserve loyalty.

So "do not touch the idol" cannot be a rule for life.

Sometimes you should touch it.

Sometimes you should knock on it.

Sometimes you should look behind it.

Sometimes the gold paint needs to come off.

The question is why you are looking.

Are you trying to understand?

Or are you trying to destroy your own capacity for admiration?

Those are different impulses.

There is a kind of person who feels intelligent only when disappointed.

Every hero must be exposed.

Every beautiful story must have an ugly explanation.

Every generous act must hide selfish motivation.

Every success must contain fraud.

This can look like realism.

Often it is just pessimism with research.

If you search hard enough, you can reduce almost anything to something ugly.

Love becomes hormones.

Friendship becomes mutual benefit.

Art becomes status.

Generosity becomes self-image.

Courage becomes ego.

Perhaps some of these explanations are partly true.

But reducing something to its mechanism does not necessarily explain its meaning.

A song is air pressure changing over time.

That description is accurate.

It is also useless if you are trying to explain why someone cried while listening to it.

Reality exists at more than one level.

Maybe people do too.

The younger version of me was worried that learning the inner truth of admired people or beautiful events could destroy the little evidence we had that success, goodness, and beauty existed at all.

I understand that fear.

There are periods in life when hope is built from very few examples.

You protect them.

You need them intact.

But years later, I think the examples become stronger when they no longer need to be perfect.

A flawed person can still do something admirable.

A damaged relationship can still contain real love.

A failed period can still contain meaningful days.

A hero can become human without becoming worthless.

The gold paint may come off.

What remains underneath matters more.

Sometimes there is cheap material beneath it.

Then disappointment is justified.

Sometimes there is still something solid.

Less beautiful from far away.

More complicated up close.

But real.

That may be a better object of respect.

Not an idol.

A human being.

The phrase that stayed with me in 2009 was about not touching.

Today I think touching is unavoidable.

Life eventually brings us close to the things we once viewed from a distance.

Teachers become colleagues.

Parents become people whose weaknesses we understand.

Heroes become biographies.

Institutions become workplaces.

Love becomes daily life.

Dreams become logistics.

The shine changes.

That does not always mean the beauty was false.

Sometimes it means the beauty was incomplete.

Perhaps growing older is partly learning how to keep what was valuable after the gold paint comes off.
