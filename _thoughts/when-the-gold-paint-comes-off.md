In January 2009, I wrote about an image that stayed with me: touching an idol and finding gold paint on your hands.

What seemed solid at a distance turns out to have a surface. The hand carries evidence that the shine was not the whole thing.

I understood the image as a warning about disappointment. Get close enough to an admired person, a successful institution, or a beautiful memory, and details appear that the distant view did not contain. A teacher becomes a colleague. A dream becomes daily work. A place from childhood turns out to be smaller than the one remembered.

Sometimes the new detail is serious. Sometimes it is merely ordinary. Both can disturb an image that had been carrying more meaning than we realized.

An admired person may have served as evidence that courage or goodness was possible. A remembered period may have helped us believe that life could feel that way again. When the example changes, the disappointment reaches beyond the fact itself. We begin asking what else we misunderstood.

That is where I think disillusionment can become misleading. We replace the earlier image with an equally complete negative one. The kindness must have been false. The achievement must have been empty. Nothing in that period could have been as good as we thought.

But finding a flaw does not mean we have finally seen the whole. A person can behave generously in one situation and selfishly in another. An unhappy period can contain a friendship that mattered. New knowledge asks for revision; it does not always require erasing the previous account.

Memory makes the revision complicated. A place we miss may stand for people who are no longer there. Returning cannot give us the earlier arrangement of life, however faithfully the building has survived. What feels like disappointment with the place may really be the discovery that it could not preserve the time for us.

There are facts we need because they protect us. I would not use the old image as advice to leave dishonesty or harm unexamined. Keeping an illusion intact can have a serious cost. The point is to notice what we do after the knowledge arrives.

Do we become more accurate about what happened, or more determined never to admire anything again?

The second response can feel sophisticated because suspicion often supplies a ready explanation. Generosity was self-interest. Friendship was convenience. Love was need. Sometimes there is truth in these explanations. They may still leave out much of what made the experience valuable.

I understand why the younger version of me wanted to protect the shine. Some examples are important before we have enough others to stand beside them. Losing one can feel like losing the possibility it represented.

Now I am more interested in what remains after the surface has changed. The person may still have taught me something. The place may still belong to a meaningful part of life. I can describe that value more carefully without needing the original image restored.

The paint on the hand is evidence. What it proves takes a little longer to understand.
