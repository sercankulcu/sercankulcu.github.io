title: 'Avoidance Charges Interest'

A task can become more intimidating while nothing about the task itself changes.

The document remains unopened. The email waits. Each day adds less available time and more embarrassment about not having begun. Avoidance offers a small amount of relief, then makes the next encounter harder.

That is why I think of it as borrowing comfort. The immediate discomfort falls, but the work is still waiting when the borrowed time ends.

The instruction "stop procrastinating" does little to help at that moment. The obstacle may be vague enough that even beginning feels like agreeing to finish everything at once. A report becomes an enormous, shapeless obligation rather than a set of things someone could do this afternoon.

The first useful step is often inspection. Open the document and read what is actually required. Identify the missing section. Check which information is already available. A task with edges may still be difficult, but it asks a more manageable question.

"Write the report" hides several decisions. "Find the three sources needed for the outline" gives the next session somewhere to begin. If the real obstacle is a missing answer from someone else, discovering that early changes the job: the next action is a question, not another hour staring at the page.

This is also where it helps to leave a clear stopping point. An unfinished paragraph with a note about what belongs next is easier to return to than a closed file called "work." The point is to reduce the uncertainty of the next encounter, not to complete an elaborate planning ritual.

A deliberate postponement can be sensible. Another task may matter more, or the information may not be available yet. It becomes a different kind of decision when there is a reason, a next action, and a time to return.

For an avoided task, that may be enough progress for one sitting. Open it, find the obstacle, and decide what happens next. The work has not disappeared, but it no longer needs to remain an unnamed threat.
