---
title: 'The Knowledge Illusion'
date: 2018-11-18
tags:

---

We often think we understand more than we actually do. This is not because we are dishonest. It is partly because knowledge is social.

I can use a phone without understanding radio communication, semiconductor design, operating systems, and battery chemistry. I can drive a car without knowing exactly how every part of the engine works.

Modern life depends on knowledge distributed across many people. This creates what researchers such as Steven Sloman and Philip Fernbach describe as a knowledge illusion. We confuse the knowledge available in our community with knowledge inside our own head. A simple test can reveal this. Ask someone whether they understand how a zipper, toilet, bicycle, or mechanical lock works.

Most people say yes. Then ask them to explain every step in detail. Confidence usually falls quickly. This matters because the same effect can appear in more serious topics. We may have strong opinions about economics, technology, public policy, or science while understanding only a small part of the system.

Listening to confident people can increase this illusion. If the group around us appears certain, we may borrow some of that certainty without noticing. The solution is not to become silent about everything. Nobody can become an expert in every field. A better habit is intellectual humility.

Ask what we actually know. Separate personal understanding from something we only heard. Try to explain the mechanism. Look for information that disagrees with us. I find this uncomfortable sometimes.

But saying "I do not understand this well enough yet" is not weakness. It is often the beginning of real understanding.
