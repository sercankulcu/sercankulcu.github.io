---
title: "The Statue You Keep Carving Until It Breaks"
date: 2010-06-12
description: "Trying to perfect another person can turn love into pressure and break what affection meant to protect."

In June 2010, I described love as a statue that you keep carving even after you suspect it will never take the shape you wanted.

You have spent months on it. You have adjusted it, removed pieces, tried another angle. Nobody wants to break something they have worked on for so long, sweep away the dust, and leave. So the hands keep moving while the mind repeats: it is not working.

The detail that still interests me is the material being removed. Each attempt to make the statue work also takes something away from the whole imagined at the beginning.

Relationships do require adjustment. Two people learn each other's habits, negotiate differences, apologize, and make room. Some of those changes make a shared life possible. The trouble is that a reasonable compromise and a damaging one can look similar at first.

One person stops asking for something. The other avoids a subject. An expectation is lowered because the argument seems too exhausting to repeat. Any single decision may be understandable. Only later does the accumulation become visible: there is less conflict, perhaps, but also less of the relationship either person wanted.

That is the question the statue asks me now: what are we removing in order to keep this alive?

There is a difference between changing a habit that hurts someone and gradually giving up the ability to speak naturally. A compromise can enlarge the space two people share. It can also make one person smaller so that the arrangement remains undisturbed. Continued effort does not tell us which process is taking place.

Investment makes the distinction harder to face. Time, affection, forgiveness, and shared plans give a relationship weight. We have come this far. We have tried so much. Leaving would seem to make all those attempts pointless.

But the years already spent cannot decide whether the next years will be good. They deserve to be remembered honestly, including what was valuable. They cannot promise that another attempt will produce a different result.

This does not make a relationship an accounting exercise. It makes the question of staying a question about the future as well as the past. What is changing? Which promises have become behavior? Is repair allowing us to return to living together, or has repair become almost everything we do?

A couple can become so occupied with discussing the relationship that they have little energy left to experience it. Every conversation becomes another review of what went wrong. Every calm period feels provisional. The effort may be sincere, but sincerity does not prevent exhaustion.

Still, a difficult season is not necessarily a failed relationship. Illness, grief, work, distance, and money can make two people temporarily hard to live with. Patience matters when it gives them a chance to respond to those conditions. The useful evidence is what happens over time: whether responsibility is shared, whether behavior changes, whether trust has room to recover.

The old image ended with destruction. Break the statue, sweep it away, run. There is anger in that ending, and probably relief too. Today I can imagine a quieter version: putting down the tools.

You stop demanding that the next adjustment must justify every previous one. You look at what exists and accept that it may not become what you hoped. Affection can remain through that recognition. A person can matter deeply even when a shared life is no longer possible.

An ending does not make the earlier years unreal. It changes what follows them. The conversations, mistakes, kindnesses, and ordinary days still happened. Some will remain valuable without needing to lead anywhere else.

The metaphor has another weakness worth keeping in view. It makes the relationship sound like passive material and the person working on it sound like its rightful designer. Real people are neither.

Sometimes the imagined statue is the problem. We keep trying to make a partner more expressive, more ambitious, quieter, more social, or more like the person we expected them to become. Then we call that pressure "working on the relationship." The other person may be asking to be understood while we are still trying to correct the design.

Here the question turns back toward the sculptor. Which changes would protect both people, and which would merely make someone fit a private preference? Are we responding to harmful behavior, or treating difference itself as a fault?

Acceptance needs judgment too. It does not require tolerating harm. It does require seeing the person who is actually there, rather than treating them as an unfinished version of someone else.

Communication can help reveal that difference. It cannot guarantee compatibility. Two people may finally explain themselves perfectly and discover that they want different lives. The clarity hurts because it removes a misunderstanding they had hoped was the whole problem.

Part of the grief then belongs to a future that never happened: the home, the trips, the ordinary years both people once imagined. Those possibilities had already entered the present through plans and expectations. Letting them go can take longer than admitting that the relationship has ended.

I understand why the younger version of me wanted a decisive gesture. Smashing the statue gave uncertainty an ending. But the image is most useful before that moment, while there is still time to examine what the work is doing.

I would ask fewer questions about how much effort has been spent and more about what that effort now makes possible. Can both people speak? Can both change without disappearing into the other's wishes? Is there a life together beyond the next repair?

The hands in the old entry were trying. That deserves compassion, including when they kept trying too long. What they needed was enough distance to see whether the shape was becoming more whole or only smaller.
