title: 'Plans Break Where Reality Begins'
date: 2017-02-14

A plan usually looks most convincing before anything happens.

On paper, the steps are clean. Dependencies behave. People respond on time. Information arrives when needed. The sequence makes sense because reality has not yet had a chance to interfere.

Clausewitz called this interference *friction*.

In war, friction includes uncertainty, fear, weather, mistakes, delay, incomplete information, exhaustion, and human behavior. Outside war, the scale is different but the pattern is familiar.

A project begins with a neat timeline and immediately meets an unavailable person, a hidden technical problem, a misunderstood requirement, a budget constraint, or a decision that takes three times longer than expected.

The mistake is not making a plan.

The mistake is treating deviation from the plan as evidence that planning failed.

A useful plan is not a prediction of the future. It is a structure for responding when the future refuses to cooperate.

That distinction changes how I think about preparation.

If a plan only works when every assumption is correct, it is not very strong. Good preparation should identify what matters most, what can change, what cannot change, and which signals mean the original approach needs revision.

This is also why detailed planning can create false confidence.

More detail can feel like more control even when the detail is built on guesses.

A twenty-step plan based on uncertain assumptions is not necessarily better than a five-step plan with clear checkpoints.

Clausewitz also connected military action to political purpose. That idea transfers carefully to ordinary work: tactics make sense only in relation to an objective.

A team can execute perfectly and still fail if it is solving the wrong problem.

A person can optimize a schedule that should have been abandoned.

A project can hit every milestone and still produce something nobody needs.

Execution is not a substitute for direction.

So I now think a plan has two jobs.

It should tell us what we intend to do.

And it should make it easier to notice when reality is telling us to do something else.

Plans break where reality begins.

The strongest ones are designed with that fact already inside them.
