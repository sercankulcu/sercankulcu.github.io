---
title: 'Podcasts Borrow the Empty Parts of a Day'
date: 2018-08-26
tags:
  - learning
  - media
  - attention
---

Podcasts fit into time that other media cannot easily reach.

A walk. A drive. Washing dishes. Waiting somewhere. Repetitive work that leaves the mind partly free.

That is their great advantage.

It is also what makes them interesting to me philosophically.

Audio does not usually ask for a new block of time. It enters time that already exists.

A book competes with another activity. A video usually requires the eyes. A podcast can attach itself to something else.

In that sense, it borrows the empty edges of a day.

This feels efficient. Sometimes it is.

But efficiency can hide a cost.

Not every empty moment is wasted space waiting to be filled.

Walking without input gives the mind a different kind of work. Thoughts continue after a conversation ends. A problem rearranges itself. A memory appears. Boredom has time to become curiosity.

If every quiet interval contains another voice, those processes lose territory.

That does not make podcasts bad. I still like them.

They are excellent discovery tools. A conversation introduces a writer, technical idea, historical event, or question that I may later explore more carefully.

But listening can create a convincing imitation of understanding.

A good speaker makes an idea feel obvious while the explanation is happening. A week later, I sometimes discover that I remember the conclusion but cannot reconstruct the argument.

Recognition is not recall.

Familiarity is not mastery.

That distinction matters especially in technical subjects. Code, diagrams, equations, and careful definitions often resist purely auditory understanding.

So I have stopped asking podcasts to do the work of books, papers, or practice.

Their best role is often earlier in the chain.

They create interest.

They expose vocabulary.

They give a subject a human voice.

Then, if the idea survives after the headphones come off, I can decide whether it deserves deeper attention.

There is another strange feature of audio: repeated voices become familiar.

A host's pauses, jokes, and habits can create a sense of companionship despite the fact that the relationship moves in one direction.

That feeling is not false exactly, but it is asymmetric.

The speaker becomes part of my routine without knowing I exist.

Digital media contains many relationships like this now.

So I try to use podcasts deliberately rather than continuously.

Some empty time deserves a voice.

Some empty time deserves to remain empty.
