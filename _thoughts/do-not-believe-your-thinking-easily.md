---
title: 'A Thought Is Not Yet a Conclusion'
date: 2021-03-17
tags:
  - judgment
  - emotion
  - reflection
---

A thought has one unfair advantage over evidence: it arrives already inside the mind.

That proximity makes it feel authoritative. If I think someone is angry with me, the thought can feel like information. If I imagine that a project will fail, fear can make the prediction feel tested before anything has happened. If I remember an event vividly, confidence in the memory can hide how much memory reconstructs.

Hume was suspicious of the ease with which the mind moves from impression to belief. I find the same problem in ordinary life. The mind dislikes empty space, so it supplies causes quickly. A delayed message becomes rejection. One criticism becomes a verdict on our ability. One bad day becomes evidence about the future.

The problem is not that these interpretations are always wrong. Sometimes the person really is angry. Sometimes the project really is failing. The problem is that **plausibility and proof arrive with different responsibilities**.

A plausible thought deserves attention. A conclusion deserves evidence.

This distinction matters most when emotion is strong. Anger narrows the range of explanations we are willing to consider. Fear does the same. Both emotions may be completely justified and still be poor investigators. They tell us that something matters before they tell us exactly what is true.

I try to create a small interval between the first story and the final judgment. What did I actually observe? What am I adding? What else could produce the same facts? What would I believe if the situation belonged to someone else?

These questions are not a ritual for talking myself out of every uncomfortable intuition. Excessive self-doubt can become another way of refusing judgment. Sometimes experience detects patterns before we can explain them clearly.

The point is not to distrust thought. It is to distinguish thought from verdict.

A mind should be allowed to generate possibilities freely. That is one of its strengths. But not every possibility deserves promotion to reality simply because it was generated by us.

Perhaps intellectual humility begins much closer to home than philosophy usually makes it sound.

Before asking whether another person is wrong, I can ask what status I have given the sentence currently passing through my own head.

It may be true.

It is not true merely because I thought it.
