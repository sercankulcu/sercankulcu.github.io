---
title: 'Teaching Should Leave Something Unfinished'
date: 2021-07-21
description: "Good teaching explains enough to prevent helplessness and leaves enough open to keep curiosity alive."
tags:

---

A lesson can be complete and still leave nothing behind.

Definitions were covered. Slides were finished. Examples were solved. Students copied the notes. The class ended exactly where the plan said it should.

And sometimes almost nothing survives the exam.

This does not make clear instruction less important. Students need fundamentals; a learner cannot rediscover every theorem or algorithm from scratch.

But information alone does not explain why some ideas remain alive after a course while others disappear almost immediately.

The difference often seems to be unfinished curiosity.

A student encounters a question that the lesson does not fully close. Why does this algorithm fail here? What would happen if we changed this assumption? Why was this design chosen instead of another one?

Consider a binary search lesson. Once students understand how to find a matching value in a sorted array, ask what should happen when the same value appears several times. Finding any match and finding the first match are different tasks. A familiar algorithm suddenly contains a question that the worked example did not settle.

The student has enough structure to investigate, and a reason to do so.

As a teacher, it is tempting to resolve every ambiguity because clarity feels like success. But a perfectly closed explanation can also remove the reason to continue thinking.

Good teaching therefore has a strange task.

Explain enough that confusion does not become helplessness.

Leave enough open that understanding does not become passive completion.

This is one reason projects and experiments can work so well. They reveal that the clean version presented in class is only the entrance. Real systems contain edge cases. Real data behaves badly. Real decisions create trade-offs.

The student discovers that the subject is larger than the explanation.

That discovery matters.

A teacher cannot manufacture curiosity on command. Some subjects will interest one student and leave another cold. But we can create conditions in which questions are visible and pursuing them is possible.

Maybe the best lesson is not the one after which the student says, "Now I know everything I need."

Maybe it is the one that makes a new question difficult to ignore.

Teaching should clarify.

But sometimes it should also leave something productively unfinished.
