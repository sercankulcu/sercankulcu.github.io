---
title: 'We Begin in the Middle of Things'
date: 2022-03-18
tags:

---

Nobody begins from a blank page.

By the time we are old enough to ask what kind of life we want, much of the setting has already been chosen for us. A language is already in our mouth. A family history already exists. A country, a body, a generation, an economic situation, and a set of expectations are waiting before we can agree to any of them.

Heidegger used the word *Geworfenheit*, usually translated as "thrownness," for this condition. We are not placed carefully at the beginning of a story. We wake up somewhere in the middle.

I find the idea useful because modern language often exaggerates authorship. Choose better. Work harder. Reinvent yourself. Become whatever you want. These sentences contain some truth, but they make choice sound as if it occurs in empty space.

It does not.

Choice always begins from somewhere.

Two people can make the same effort and still face very different worlds. One decision can be courageous for one person and almost costless for another. A path that looks obvious from outside may be blocked by obligations, fear learned early, money, health, law, or family history.

Thrownness is not an excuse. That would make the concept too easy.

The tension is precisely that we did not choose the starting conditions and yet we still have to respond to them. Freedom does not arrive after circumstances disappear. It appears inside circumstances that remain stubbornly present.

This changes how I think about responsibility. I can ask what someone chose without pretending that choice is the whole story. I can also examine my own past without turning every limitation into destiny.

Some things were given to me. Some were taught to me before I knew enough to question them. Some became habits. Some became fears. Some became advantages I barely noticed because they felt normal.

Growing older may involve separating these layers.

What is actually mine now?

What did I inherit and never examine?

What can I change?

What must I carry without pretending that carrying it was my choice?

Heidegger's language can be difficult, but this part of it feels surprisingly ordinary to me. Every life begins with facts already on the table.

We do not choose the hand.

But eventually we become responsible for how we play it.
