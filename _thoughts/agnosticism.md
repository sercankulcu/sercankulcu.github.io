---
title: 'Uncertainty Is Not an Empty Position'
date: 2020-03-12

Agnosticism is sometimes treated as indecision.

You either believe or you do not. You either know or you do not. Anything in between can look like hesitation.

I think that misses the most interesting part.

Agnosticism is not the refusal to think. It is the refusal to claim more than the evidence allows.

That makes it a demanding position rather than a passive one.

Certainty is psychologically attractive. It gives shape to the world. It allows us to stop searching, defend a position, join a camp, and speak without constantly reopening the question.

Uncertainty removes that comfort.

It asks us to live with a sentence that feels unfinished: I do not know.

The phrase can sound weak when it is used to avoid examination. But it can also be exact. There are questions about God, consciousness, reality, morality, and the future for which confidence often exceeds what anyone can demonstrate.

David Hume repeatedly distrusted the distance between what the mind wants to conclude and what experience actually justifies. I find that tension more useful than simply choosing a side because uncertainty feels unpleasant.

Agnosticism also differs from indifference.

I can care deeply about a question without pretending I possess its answer. In fact, uncertainty may make a question more serious because it prevents me from closing it too early.

This matters outside religion as well.

In technical work, the most expensive mistakes often begin with hidden certainty. We assume the bottleneck before measuring it. We choose an architecture before understanding the constraints. We become loyal to a tool and then redesign the problem so that the tool remains correct.

The mature response is not endless doubt. Decisions still have to be made.

But action and certainty are different things.

I can choose the best available option while admitting that the evidence is incomplete. I can hold a belief provisionally. I can revise without treating revision as humiliation.

Perhaps intellectual maturity is not accumulating enough answers to eliminate uncertainty.

Perhaps it is learning which uncertainties deserve to remain open.
