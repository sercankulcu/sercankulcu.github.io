Some things look ridiculous until the exact moment you need them. Gym gloves were one of those things for me. At first they seemed unnecessary, almost theatrical—like wearing sunglasses at night. You are in a gym, you lift weights, your hands touch metal. Surely hands are already designed for holding things.

Then my hands started developing blisters, and suddenly gym gloves became one of the most logical inventions in human history.

That was the entire idea behind an old entry I wrote in 2012. It was a very small observation, but it belongs to a much larger category of lessons: things that seem excessive before experience and obvious after it. Human beings are extremely good at judging needs we have never personally had.

We see someone using a tool and ask why they need it. We see a routine and call it unnecessary. We see someone spending money on specialized equipment and assume they are overdoing things. Then life introduces the missing variable: pain, fatigue, repetition, risk, inconvenience. Experience changes the equation.

The gloves themselves were not the interesting part. The transition was. Before the blisters, I evaluated them aesthetically: they looked unnecessary. After the blisters, I evaluated them functionally: they solved a problem. That difference is useful because people often judge tools before understanding the problem the tool was created to solve.

This happens everywhere. Why do programmers use two monitors? Why would anyone buy noise-canceling headphones, hiking poles, a proper office chair, or a power bank? From outside, specialization can look like obsession. From inside, specialization often looks like accumulated pain.

Repetition reveals needs that first use hides. A cheap chair feels acceptable for ten minutes; eight hours later it becomes a medical argument. A backpack feels fine when empty; carry it for fifteen kilometers. A prototype may work once; production asks whether it works ten thousand times. Reliability is repetition made visible.

The gym gave me a physical version of that lesson. My first impression was "unnecessary accessory." Repeated use produced a different answer: useful protection. Beginners often evaluate by first use. Experienced people evaluate by repeated use.

There is also a social side to this. When you are new to something, you often do not want to look as if you are trying too hard. Gyms make that especially visible. Gloves? Too serious. A belt? Who do I think I am? Special shoes? Come on. The fear is not really about the object. It is about identity.

Am I experienced enough to use this? Do I deserve the equipment? Will people think I am pretending? These are strange questions. Tools do not require status. If something is appropriate and useful, that should be enough. Ironically, refusing useful tools can be one of the strongest signs that we are new.

Experience gradually changes the way preparation looks. Carrying the charger, the adapter, the extra cable, checking the address, making backups, putting fuel in the car early—these things can look overly cautious when nothing has gone wrong yet. After enough mistakes, prevention begins to look less dramatic and more intelligent.

Useful precautions have a peculiar problem: when they work, the result is often that nothing happens. The backup restores the file. The helmet prevents the injury. The gloves prevent the blister. We notice interventions more easily than prevented events, so successful protection can look unnecessary precisely because it succeeded.

Of course, gym gloves are not equivalent to serious safety equipment. The stakes are different. But the cognitive pattern is similar. We tend to understand protection better after discomfort. This is why experienced people sometimes sound annoyingly repetitive: wear this, check that, bring one more, do not skip this step. The beginner hears unnecessary rules. The experienced person often hears old mistakes.

That is also why practical knowledge is difficult to transfer. You can explain the rule, but you cannot transfer the feeling that created it. Someone tells you to back up your work. You understand the sentence. Then you lose a project. Now you understand the same sentence differently.

This distinction matters in teaching. Students can hear advice without owning it: start early, test with small inputs, read the error message, save versions, do not change ten things at once while debugging. Some of these lessons become meaningful only after the first consequence.

The challenge is to make mistakes cheap. Let the student break the small system, not the production system. Lose ten minutes, not six months. Create a safe blister, metaphorically speaking: enough discomfort to produce understanding, not enough to produce permanent damage.

There is another lesson hiding in the original joke. It is easy to confuse unfamiliarity with absurdity. "That looks ridiculous" is often another way of saying, "I do not yet understand the context." Different professions develop habits outsiders find strange. Different cultures, families, and generations solve ordinary problems differently. The first reaction is often judgment. A better reaction is curiosity.

What problem is this solving?

That is one of the best questions an engineer can develop. You see an ugly system. Before redesigning it, ask why it became this way. Maybe there was a constraint, an edge case, a regulation, a historical failure. The ugly component may be unnecessary—or it may be carrying twenty years of invisible experience.

This is the engineering version of gym gloves: do not remove the glove until you understand the blister.

Legacy systems are full of these cases. A strange validation rule, a redundant-looking check, a process everyone hates. The instinct of a new engineer is often: why is this here? Delete it. Sometimes deletion is exactly right. Sometimes the answer arrives three days later in the form of an incident.

The older I get, the more respect I have for the question, "What happened that caused someone to add this?" It turns criticism into investigation.

A tiny gym entry became useful because the sequence repeats throughout life: judge the object, experience the problem, understand the object. You think people are overreacting about sleep until you stop sleeping enough. You think documentation is bureaucracy until the person who wrote the system leaves. You think checking the weather is excessive until you spend an afternoon soaked.

The ideal would be to learn everything through wisdom. In practice, a great deal of learning happens through irritation.

I do not remember the exact day my hands started blistering or which exercise finally changed my mind. The archive preserves only the conclusion: gym gloves initially looked like sunglasses at night. Then my hands blistered. Then I understood why they existed.

That is enough to remind me that opinions formed before experience should be held lightly, especially opinions about other people's solutions.

Maybe the tool really is unnecessary. Maybe the person using it is overdoing things. That happens too.

But before laughing, there is one useful question:

**What do they know that I have not experienced yet?**

Sometimes the answer is nothing.

Sometimes the answer is a blister.
