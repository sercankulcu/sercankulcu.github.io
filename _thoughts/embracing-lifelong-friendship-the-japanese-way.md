title: 'Friendship Needs a Structure'
date: 2019-10-06

I used to think strong friendships survived mainly because the people involved cared enough.

Now I think care is only part of it.

Long friendships usually have some kind of structure.

There is a Japanese term often associated with Okinawa, *moai*, for a social group whose members support one another over long periods. What interests me is not the exoticness of the term. It is the assumption behind it: connection should not be left entirely to chance.

Modern friendship is often informal to the point of fragility.

We like one another. We intend to meet. We say we should talk more often. Then work becomes busy, families change, cities change, and months disappear without any deliberate decision to let the friendship weaken.

Nothing dramatic happens.

The relationship simply loses structure.

A recurring dinner, an annual trip, a weekly call, a shared project, even a group message that remains active over years can do something important: it gives friendship a place in time before everything else takes that time away.

This may sound mechanical, but rituals are not the opposite of affection. They are one way affection survives competing demands.

Long friendships also contain a kind of memory no new relationship can reproduce. Someone who has known us for twenty years remembers versions of us that no longer exist. They know which ambitions disappeared, which mistakes we repeated, which stories we still tell incorrectly.

That continuity can be comforting. It can also be corrective.

A longtime friend sometimes sees a pattern before we do because they have more data.

Of course, not every old friendship deserves preservation. Duration alone does not create depth, and loyalty should not become an excuse for remaining inside a harmful relationship.

But when a friendship is good, neglect is a poor way to test its strength.

I like the idea that friendship can be designed for longevity rather than merely hoped to survive.

Independence is often treated as maturity.

Perhaps another form of maturity is building a life in which dependence on a few trusted people is not an accident, but part of the architecture.
