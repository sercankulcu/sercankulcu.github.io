In April 2010, I wrote about a "good morning" that did not arrive.

It was something I had heard every day, familiar enough that I had stopped treating it as an event. When it was missing, I noticed how much of the morning had been built around it.

Until then, I had thought that love began to fade when a relationship became a habit. Repetition seemed like evidence that something important was disappearing. My old entry reached almost the opposite conclusion: perhaps love was hearing that greeting from that person every day.

I would make a smaller claim now. A greeting cannot define a relationship. But it can carry affection so reliably that the affection becomes difficult to see.

At the beginning, everything attracts attention: the first message, the first private joke, the first time you realize you are waiting for a particular name to appear. There is so much you do not know that even an ordinary reply gives you information. Will we meet again? Did that sentence mean what I hoped it meant?

Later, you know when the other person usually wakes. You recognize a certain silence. You can tell which complaint needs an answer and which only needs to be heard. That familiarity changes the experience. The person has become part of how the day works.

This can feel quieter than the beginning without being less intimate. There is a freedom in no longer having to make every evening memorable. A long relationship contains Tuesdays, headaches, laundry, bills, and one person reading while the other does something else. Being comfortable together through those hours matters too.

Yet familiarity can also make us careless. A message becomes expected. A daily kindness becomes the baseline. We notice the one occasion when someone disappoints us more readily than the many occasions when they remembered. Reliability asks for little attention, so we give it little.

Then the greeting fails to arrive, and the empty space acquires a shape.

That was the reversal in my old entry. I had taken the routine as a sign that love was becoming ordinary. Its interruption made me wonder whether ordinary life was where much of the love had gone to live.

Think of the small phrases people repeat: "Are you home?" "Drive carefully." "Do you need anything?" They can be spoken absent-mindedly, or out of obligation, or with real concern. The words alone do not settle the question. Their meaning comes from the person, the history, and what happens beyond the sentence. Someone who asks whether you need help and then makes time for the answer is doing something that a phrase by itself cannot do.

That is why I would not turn this memory into advice to preserve every routine. People stay together through fear, convenience, loneliness, and inertia as well as affection. Missing a daily message does not prove that a relationship was healthy or that it should continue. Sometimes what we miss is the predictability. Sometimes it is the person. Often the two are difficult to separate.

The distinction matters because longing can make any familiar arrangement look valuable after it ends. My old conclusion was useful as a correction to what I had overlooked, not as a test that every relationship should pass.

There is a more practical question inside it: how do we keep noticing someone once their presence is no longer a surprise?

I do not think the answer is constant novelty. A relationship does not need a succession of grand gestures to escape neglect. Sometimes it needs an ordinary question asked with attention. Sometimes it needs a thank-you for something that has been done so often that nobody calls it a favor anymore. Familiarity should help us understand the other person; it should not persuade us that we already know everything worth asking.

The beginning of a relationship has curiosity built into it. Later, curiosity needs more deliberate care. The person beside us still changes, even when the greeting stays the same. Work worries them differently. An old ambition loses its importance. They begin wanting something they would not have wanted a year ago. A routine can hold a relationship together while those changes happen, provided we still look at the person inside it.

I like the modest scale of the memory. Nothing in it requires an expensive dinner or an exceptional day. Two words were enough to reveal an expectation I had stopped noticing.

Years later, I would not say that love begins when excitement ends, or that a missing greeting proves anything by itself. I would say that affection can become so closely woven into a day that we mistake it for the day itself.

I would rather notice the person saying good morning while they are still there to hear my answer.
