---
title: "Love Was the Good Morning I Took for Granted"
date: 2010-04-24
tags:

   - habits

---

There are things we notice because they happen.

And there are things we notice only when they stop happening.

In April 2010, I wrote a short entry about love.

At the time, I thought I had misunderstood it for years.

I had believed that once a relationship turned into habit, love began to fade.

That repetition was the enemy.

That monotony meant something important was disappearing.

Then one ordinary habit disappeared for a moment.

A "good morning."

Something I had heard every day.

Something so familiar that I had stopped treating it as an event.

And when it did not come, I understood its weight.

The old entry ended with a very simple conclusion:

Maybe love was hearing that good morning from that person every day.

I still like that sentence.

Not because it defines love completely.

Nothing does.

But because it describes one of the quiet ways affection hides itself.

At the beginning, everything is visible.

The first message.

The first meeting.

The first time a hand stays a little longer than necessary.

The first private joke.

The first time you realize you are waiting for a particular person's name to appear.

Beginnings announce themselves.

Routine does not.

Routine enters quietly.

A message becomes expected.

A phone call becomes normal.

A chair becomes someone's chair.

A phrase becomes theirs.

You stop asking whether these things matter because they have become part of the day.

And perhaps that is where we begin to misunderstand them.

We often imagine love as intensity.

Something that must keep producing evidence.

Excitement.

Long conversations.

Surprises.

Jealousy.

Distance closed dramatically.

Distance opened dramatically.

Songs suddenly meaningful.

Sleepless nights.

These things belong to love sometimes.

Especially early love.

But a relationship cannot remain permanently at the emotional volume of its beginning.

If it did, nobody would survive it.

Eventually two people begin living around one another.

That sounds less romantic.

It may be more important.

One person becomes part of the architecture of the day.

You know when they usually wake.

What kind of mood a certain silence means.

How they say your name when something is wrong.

Which small complaint is serious and which is merely ritual.

You know the repeated details.

And because you know them, you stop seeing them.

Familiarity has a strange talent for making valuable things look ordinary.

A view from a hotel window can be beautiful for three days.

Live there for ten years and you may stop looking.

The same street that impresses a visitor becomes the road to work.

A song you once played obsessively becomes background.

A person's daily kindness can suffer the same fate.

Not because you no longer value it.

Because the mind is built to notice change.

Consistency becomes invisible.

Then one morning, something changes.

No good morning.

Now the silence has shape.

You notice the place where the message should have been.

The absence is not empty.

It is full of expectation.

This is one of the peculiar powers of missing things.

Presence can become ordinary.

Absence restores definition.

The cup that is always on the desk is furniture.

Remove it, and suddenly you see the empty circle.

A person who always calls becomes easy to assume.

Then the phone does not ring.

The ordinary act returns to consciousness through its disappearance.

That is what the old entry was trying to say.

I had treated habit as evidence against love.

Then habit disappeared and revealed itself as one of love's forms.

This does not mean every routine is love.

That would be too easy.

People can stay together through fear, inertia, convenience, loneliness, obligation, or simple inability to change.

Routine alone proves nothing.

But loving relationships do create routines.

And many of those routines carry care without announcing it.

Did you eat?

Are you home?

Good night.

Drive carefully.

Call me when you arrive.

Take an umbrella.

Do you need anything?

Good morning.

These are not great declarations.

Nobody writes them on monuments.

That may be why they matter.

They belong to continuation.

Grand romantic gestures prove that someone can create a moment.

Small repeated acts show that someone has made room for you inside ordinary life.

That is a different kind of intimacy.

The dramatic moment says:

Look how much I feel.

The daily gesture says:

You are part of my day.

There is no audience for most of these things.

No anniversary.

No photograph.

No story to tell friends.

They happen between breakfast and work.

Between waking and leaving.

Between one ordinary day and another.

This makes them easy to underestimate.

We remember exceptional events because memory likes contrast.

But relationships are mostly built in low contrast.

Days that resemble one another.

Conversations nobody else would find interesting.

Repeated meals.

Repeated jokes.

Repeated irritation.

Repeated forgiveness.

A thousand tiny confirmations that do not deserve separate entries in a diary.

Then years later, what you miss may be exactly those things.

Not the expensive dinner.

The sound of someone in the next room.

Not the planned trip.

The message sent every morning.

This is the cruelty and beauty of habit.

It takes what once felt special and makes it normal.

That can look like loss.

Sometimes it is actually integration.

The person has moved from event to environment.

You no longer think:

This person is doing something loving.

You think:

This is how mornings are.

Until mornings are not like that.

Then you discover how much of the day was built around something you had stopped naming.

I do not know from the source why the good morning did not arrive that day.

The archive does not tell me whether there had been an argument, distance, silence, a breakup, or simply one unusual morning.

It would be wrong to invent the missing story.

The important fact is the reaction.

Something expected failed to happen.

That interruption made me reconsider what love looked like.

That is enough.

Sometimes an old entry preserves the lesson but not the incident that produced it.

Perhaps that is fitting.

We often forget the exact trigger and retain the conclusion.

What stays with me now is the reversal:

I had thought routine meant love was ending.

Then I briefly lost the routine and realized it might have been love becoming ordinary.

There is a danger in romanticizing this idea too much.

People should not remain in relationships merely because routine feels comfortable.

Nor should absence automatically be mistaken for proof of love.

Missing someone can come from attachment, dependency, habit, loneliness, or fear.

Human emotion is not clean.

But the fact that feelings have several possible causes does not make them meaningless.

It only asks us to be careful.

The younger version of me made a universal statement from one moment.

That is what younger versions of us do.

Love is this.

Life is that.

People are like this.

Everything becomes a definition.

Age often replaces definitions with descriptions.

I would no longer say:

Love is hearing good morning every day.

I would say:

Sometimes love becomes visible in the good morning you had stopped noticing.

That is smaller.

I trust it more.

Relationships often become difficult when people compare the mature form to the beginning.

At the start, uncertainty creates intensity.

Will they write?

Do they like me?

When will we meet again?

What did that sentence mean?

Uncertainty gives every signal enormous value.

Later, certainty replaces some of that intensity.

You know the person will write.

You know you will see them.

You know what they meant.

This is healthier.

But the nervous system may interpret reduced uncertainty as reduced passion.

Something feels quieter.

We ask whether something has been lost.

Perhaps something has.

Beginnings cannot be preserved indefinitely.

But something else may have been gained.

Trust.

Ease.

Familiarity.

The freedom to be boring together.

That last one is underrated.

Anyone can be interesting for an evening.

A long relationship contains Tuesdays.

Headaches.

Laundry.

Dead batteries.

Bills.

Bad moods.

Silence.

One person reading while another does something else.

Love that survives only in exceptional moments has very little territory.

Ordinary life is most of the map.

This is why I now find the old "good morning" image more convincing than I did when I first wrote it.

It belongs to ordinary life.

A greeting is almost nothing.

Two words.

But repeated every day, from one particular person, it becomes a thread.

You do not feel every strand individually.

Together they make continuity.

One day the thread breaks.

You look down.

Only then do you realize you had been holding it.

There is another lesson here about gratitude.

We are not very good at valuing stable good things.

Not because we are ungrateful in some moral sense.

Because attention is attracted to problems.

Pain shouts.

Reliability whispers.

If someone disappoints you once, you notice.

If they are dependable for two hundred days, dependability becomes the baseline.

This is unfair but natural.

Healthy relationships may require deliberately noticing what no longer demands attention.

Not through forced gratitude rituals.

Simply by occasionally asking:

What would I notice if it disappeared?

The answer can be revealing.

The message.

The joke.

The ride home.

The person who remembers how you take your tea.

The one who listens to a story they have already heard.

The "good morning."

This question should not become anxious.

Love does not need constant rehearsal of loss to feel valuable.

But absence can teach us to see presence more clearly.

The challenge is learning the lesson before permanent absence arrives.

That may be one of adulthood's recurring tasks.

Appreciate the ordinary while it is still ordinary.

Parents aging.

Friends moving away.

Children growing.

Coworkers leaving.

Neighborhoods changing.

Objects wearing out.

Relationships ending.

We often discover value at the moment of transition.

The old entry caught one of those transitions in miniature.

A missing greeting was enough.

Not every revelation requires catastrophe.

Sometimes the day is only slightly different.

And because it is slightly different, you see yesterday properly for the first time.

The line about monotony interests me too.

I wrote that I had assumed love ended when it became habit, but that perhaps real love began there.

I would soften that now.

Love does not begin only when excitement settles.

Nor does excitement become less real.

But the transition from novelty to familiarity is a test.

In novelty, affection has help.

Everything is new.

Curiosity supplies energy.

Desire supplies attention.

In familiarity, the relationship loses some external assistance.

Now two people have to live inside what they have created.

Can they remain interested when there is less mystery?

Can they remain kind without needing an occasion?

Can they tolerate repetition without treating the other person as furniture?

Can they preserve individuality without interpreting comfort as neglect?

These are harder questions than the ones asked at the beginning.

The beginning asks:

Do we want each other?

Routine eventually asks:

How do we live with each other?

The second question has less poetry.

It may require more love.

Perhaps that is what I was reaching toward in 2010.

Not that monotony itself is romantic.

But that love can survive after novelty stops carrying it.

And when it does, love may become harder to distinguish from life.

That is both its strength and its danger.

Strength, because the bond becomes integrated.

Danger, because integrated things are easy to neglect.

You stop saying thank you.

Stop noticing.

Stop asking.

Assume the other person knows.

Assume tomorrow's good morning will arrive because today's did.

This is where familiarity can become carelessness.

The answer is not to keep the relationship artificially dramatic.

Drama is not attention.

The answer is to keep seeing the person inside the routine.

The greeting is repeated.

The person saying it is not an object.

They are choosing, however casually, to remain in contact with you today.

Then again tomorrow.

And again.

We often treat repetition as absence of choice.

Sometimes repetition is accumulated choice.

That is a more generous interpretation.

Not always true.

But worth considering.

A relationship may contain thousands of small decisions to return.

To answer.

To listen.

To stay.

To greet.

No single one looks dramatic.

Together they become a history.

This makes endings especially strange.

When a relationship ends, it is not only the person who disappears.

A whole set of tiny habits loses its destination.

You still reach mentally for the message.

The phrase.

The routine.

The time of day.

The body remembers schedules after the relationship has changed.

That may be why heartbreak feels physical.

The world has been reorganized, but habit has not caught up yet.

Morning arrives carrying an expectation that no longer has somewhere to go.

The old entry may have been written from some version of that feeling.

I do not know.

The source does not allow me to say more.

But the sentence understands the mechanism.

You recognize the importance of the good morning when you do not receive it.

Years later, I would add only one thing.

The lesson should not be merely:

Notice what you miss.

It should be:

Notice what you still have before you have to miss it.

That is much harder.

Absence concentrates attention automatically.

Presence requires intention.

The most valuable parts of life often suffer from being reliable.

They arrive so consistently that we stop celebrating the fact that they arrive at all.

A familiar voice.

A parent answering the phone.

A friend replying.

Someone sleeping beside you.

Someone asking how your day went and actually waiting for the answer.

Someone saying good morning.

These things are small enough to disappear inside repetition.

Maybe love is not the repetition itself.

Maybe love is the meaning carried through repetition.

The old version of me needed one quiet interruption to notice it.

I am glad he wrote it down.

Because the entry does not preserve a grand romance.

It preserves something more fragile.

The realization that ordinary affection can become invisible without becoming unimportant.

And that sometimes the emptiest morning is the one that finally shows you what had been there every day.
