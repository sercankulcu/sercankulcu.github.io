---
title: 'Admire the Work, Not the Statue'
date: 2018-07-07
description: "Admiration becomes healthier when we learn to value the work without turning the person into a statue."
tags:
  - admiration
  - judgment
  - reflection
---

Admiration often spreads beyond the achievement that first earned it.

A writer produces work that changes how I think. A scientist makes an extraordinary discovery. A musician creates something I return to for years. Soon I begin expecting the person to be wise, generous, and reliable in areas for which the work offered no evidence.

The expectation is easy to build because public work is selected. I encounter the finished book, the paper, the performance. The ordinary irritations and failures beside it are mostly absent. A coherent image forms around a partial view.

Discovering an unpleasant fact then creates two questions. What does it reveal about the person? What should it change about my relationship with the work?

Neither answer has to be automatic. A private disappointment may not alter the quality of an argument or a piece of music. Serious wrongdoing may change whether I want to support the person, buy the work, recommend it, or treat their public example as admirable. Those decisions involve more than whether the work is technically good.

It helps to be precise about the original admiration. I value how this person writes. I learned something from that explanation. I respect the discipline behind this achievement. These are claims I can examine without appointing the person an authority over everything else.

Specific admiration also leaves room to revise. If new information changes the meaning of the work itself, I can acknowledge that. If the achievement remains valuable while the person disappoints me, I can hold both judgments without pretending either cancels the other.

The temptation is to demand a complete moral package: first an idol, then a fraud when the package breaks. That leaves little room for the difficult business of evaluating a particular action, argument, or contribution.

I would rather know what I admire and why. The narrower claim is less dramatic, but it gives both the work and the person a more human scale.
