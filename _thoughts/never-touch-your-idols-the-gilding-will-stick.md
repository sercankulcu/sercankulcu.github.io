---
title: 'Admire the Work, Not the Statue'
date: 2018-07-07
tags:
  - admiration

  - reflection
---

Admiration becomes dangerous when it asks one person to contain every quality we value.

A writer produces sentences that change the way we think. A scientist makes an extraordinary discovery. A musician creates something we return to for years. From a distance, achievement begins to spread beyond its proper boundaries.

The writer must also be wise.

The scientist must also be admirable in private life.

The musician must also be emotionally generous, politically sensible, and pleasant to meet.

We turn excellence in one area into evidence about the whole person.

Then reality arrives.

There is an old warning: "Never touch your idols; the gilding will stick to your fingers." I understand the image, but I think the deeper problem begins before we ever meet the idol.

We built the statue ourselves.

Distance helped. Public work is edited by definition. We encounter the finished book, the published paper, the performance, the speech, the interview. We do not encounter every petty argument, insecurity, contradiction, boring afternoon, or private failure that existed beside it.

The image becomes coherent because most human mess has been removed.

Then discovering an unpleasant fact feels like betrayal.

Sometimes it should. There are actions serious enough to change how we relate to someone's work.

But disappointment can also reveal something about our own expectations.

Why did I need technical brilliance to imply kindness?

Why did I assume artistic sensitivity would produce emotional maturity?

Why did I expect public courage to guarantee private decency?

These qualities can coexist.

They do not have to.

The distinction that helps me is between **admiring a capacity** and **idealizing a person**.

A capacity can be specific.

I admire how this person writes.

I admire the discipline behind this work.

I admire the courage required for that decision.

None of those sentences require turning the person into a moral package.

This also makes disappointment less catastrophic. Learning that someone I admire is flawed does not automatically erase the quality that first mattered. It simply returns the quality to human scale.

Perhaps mature admiration is narrower than worship.

It allows greatness to remain local.

A person can be extraordinary at one thing and ordinary, confused, or disappointing at many others.

The work does not need a statue around it.

Sometimes it is enough to admire the work.
