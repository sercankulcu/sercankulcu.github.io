Human beings divide the world into us and them with disturbing ease: family, clan, religion, class, race, nation, party, ideology.

The categories change. The mechanism remains.

Tribalism is not simply primitive behavior left behind by civilization. Civilization scales it. A modern nation can mobilize millions around shared identity, and digital networks can create tribes with no geography at all.

Why is tribalism so persistent?

Group belonging offers protection, meaning, status, trust, and identity. Humans depend on groups.

The problem begins when belonging requires enemies.

A strong "we" can coexist with a humane "they." Often it does not.

Political entrepreneurs understand this. Fear unifies. A threatened group tolerates hierarchy. Compromise becomes betrayal. Opponents become dangerous. War becomes morally easier.

Can this be overcome?

Not by eliminating identity. People need belonging. The more realistic goal is layered identity.

A person can be Turkish, European, Muslim or secular, scientist, parent, human. These identities need not cancel one another.

Conflict becomes dangerous when one category consumes all others. Then every issue becomes tribal.

Institutions can reduce this through shared schools, markets, law, cross-group organizations, mixed neighborhoods, professional communities, and common projects. Repeated cooperation weakens caricature.

A person becomes harder to hate after becoming colleague, neighbor, or friend.

But contact alone is not enough. It can increase conflict when competition is severe. Conditions matter: equality, shared goals, norms, political leadership.

War presents an even harder problem. States exist partly because they can use force, while international politics lacks a fully sovereign authority above states. This creates insecurity.

One state arms because another might. The other interprets arming as threat. Both become less safe.

The security dilemma mirrors the dynamics before the First World War. Rational local choices can create collective danger.

Nuclear weapons made this worse and better simultaneously. They made major war potentially catastrophic, which may deter it.

Peace becomes dependent on fear.

This is not moral transcendence. It is strategic restraint.

Could humanity eventually make war culturally obsolete?

Practices once considered normal have disappeared or been delegitimized in many places: dueling, public execution as entertainment, legal slavery. Perhaps interstate war could become similarly stigmatized.

But unlike dueling, war is connected to security, territory, resources, and state survival. The incentives are stronger.

Global institutions help through diplomacy, trade, international law, arms control, economic interdependence, and peacekeeping. None eliminates war. They raise its costs and create alternatives.

The deeper challenge is moral scale. Can a citizen care enough about foreign lives for leaders to face political costs when harming them?

National democracies are accountable primarily to their own populations. Human rights asks them to consider everyone.

This tension remains.

Climate change may force new forms of planetary identity. So may asteroid defense, pandemics, and AI risk. Problems that threaten everyone can create shared purpose.

But crisis can also intensify nationalism. There is no guarantee.

Humanity may never eliminate tribalism. The more realistic achievement may be civilizing it: keep local identities, forbid dehumanization, create institutions that channel competition without violence, teach history without mythology of purity, and make cooperation prestigious.

The goal is not one uniform humanity. Uniformity would be another kind of domination.

The goal is a species capable of difference without massacre.

That would be progress enough.
