A nation is too large for everyone to know everyone else. Yet millions of strangers can feel that they belong together.

This is one of modern history's most powerful social achievements.

Political theorist Benedict Anderson later described nations as imagined communities. The phrase does not mean fake; it means socially represented. Members imagine a shared belonging despite never meeting most fellow members.

How is this possible?

Through language, schools, maps, newspapers, flags, anthems, history textbooks, military service, public rituals, and shared holidays. The nation is reproduced through symbols.

Print capitalism played a major role in many regions. People reading the same language and news begin inhabiting a shared informational world. A farmer and a merchant may never meet, but both read about the same parliament, the same war, and the same national heroes.

Time becomes synchronized nationally. The morning newspaper says: this happened to us.

Nationalism also transforms sovereignty. Older political systems often centered on dynasty, and subjects belonged to rulers. Nationalism reverses the relationship: the state should belong to the nation.

This becomes revolutionary.

Empires contain multiple peoples. National movements demand self-rule. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries become filled with struggles over which people constitute a nation and which territory belongs to them.

This creates liberation. And conflict.

Nationalism helped colonized peoples organize against empire. It also fueled ethnic exclusion, forced assimilation, population transfer, and genocide. The same emotional mechanism can defend freedom or deny someone else's belonging.

Why is nationalism so powerful? Because it converts strangers into kin symbolically. "Motherland," "Fatherland," and "Brothers": the vocabulary of family expands to millions.

This makes sacrifice possible. A person can die for people they never met. The nation extends identity beyond individual life: ancestors died for it, and descendants will inherit it.

Again, mortality and meaning connect.

Nationalism also creates selective memory. Every nation tells history: victories are emphasized, defeats remembered, atrocities minimized, heroes simplified, and origins made ancient.

National identity depends partly on continuity. But real populations are mixed, borders move, languages change, and migration occurs. The nation often presents historical complexity as timeless unity.

This is politically useful. Historically inaccurate.

The danger appears when imagined unity becomes biological purity. Then minorities become suspicious, immigrants become threats, and mixed identities become betrayal. A symbolic community turns into an exclusionary category.

Nationalism also competes with universal moral systems. What happens when national interest conflicts with human rights? Should a state help outsiders, accept refugees, obey international law, or share resources?

The nation creates strong solidarity inward. Global ethics asks that solidarity to extend outward.

This tension remains unresolved.

The world is economically global but politically national. Climate change, pandemics, finance, and digital networks are global, but democratic legitimacy and taxation remain mostly national.

Humanity has built problems at a larger scale than its strongest identities.

This may be one of the twenty-first century's central structural challenges.

Nationalism is not simply irrational. People need belonging. Language and history matter. Political self-determination matters.

The problem is absolutism.

A nation can be loved without being treated as morally superior. Identity can be strong without requiring enemies.

The nation is imagined. So are money, law, and rights. Imagined things can be extremely real in consequence.

The question is not whether we should imagine communities. We cannot avoid it.

The question is how large the community can become before strangers stop feeling like us.
