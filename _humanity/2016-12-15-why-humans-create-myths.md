A myth is not simply a false story. That modern use of the word hides its older cultural function. Myths are narratives that organize reality.

They explain origins. Justify customs. Define identities. Connect humans to gods, ancestors, landscapes, or moral orders.

A myth can be historically inaccurate and socially powerful. It can also contain symbolic insight without functioning as literal history. This distinction matters.

Ancient people did not create myths because they were incapable of thinking. They created them because human communities need frameworks.

Why are we here? Where did our people come from? Why do we perform this ritual? Why is this mountain sacred? Why must this rule be obeyed?

Myths answer questions that facts alone do not always settle. They connect description to meaning.

A flood is a physical event. A flood myth can become a story about punishment, renewal, chaos, memory, or survival. The event and the meaning operate at different levels.

Myths are especially useful in oral societies because narrative preserves complex cultural information. A story can encode geography, kinship, morality, seasonal knowledge, or social boundaries. The memorable structure helps information survive.

This does not mean every myth began as practical instruction. Myths can also emerge from imagination, ritual, historical memory, political power, and repeated reinterpretation. Their origins are often layered.

Myths create collective identity. A group may believe it descends from a common ancestor. Whether that ancestor is biologically literal may matter less socially than the shared belief. The story creates kinship among people who are not close relatives. This allows cooperation to scale.

Later societies do the same with secular myths. Founding fathers. Revolutionary heroes. National destinies. Golden ages.

The language changes. The mechanism remains.

Modern people often assume we have escaped myth. We have not.

We still organize political life through simplified narratives. Our nation is uniquely virtuous. Our generation is uniquely corrupt. Technology will save everything. Civilization is always progressing. The past was better. Markets are naturally fair. History bends inevitably toward justice.

These statements may contain elements of truth, but when treated as unquestionable organizing stories they function mythically. Myth begins where complexity is compressed into identity.

This compression can be useful. No society can operate while every citizen continuously reexamines every foundational assumption. Shared stories reduce cognitive cost.

But they create danger when insulated from evidence. A myth that cannot be questioned becomes ideology. A group can then interpret contradictory facts as threats rather than information.

This is why myths are powerful political tools. Control the story of the past and you influence what actions seem legitimate in the present. If a territory is described as eternally ours, compromise becomes betrayal. If an enemy is portrayed as permanently evil, negotiation becomes weakness. If a leader is written into a savior narrative, criticism becomes sacrilege.

Myth transforms politics into morality.

Religious myths can do the same, though religion also contains philosophical, ethical, communal, and experiential dimensions that cannot be reduced to myth alone. Creation stories, heroic cycles, sacred genealogies, and cosmic battles give communities symbolic maps. They locate human life inside a larger order.

This reduces existential uncertainty. You are not merely an isolated organism. You belong to a story.

That promise is psychologically powerful. It may be one reason myths survive scientific change.

Science can explain mechanisms. It does not automatically provide belonging. A cosmological model tells us how the universe evolved. It does not tell a child what their community expects from them. Different kinds of narratives answer different kinds of needs.

The challenge is knowing which question we are asking.

Problems arise when symbolic narratives are treated as empirical explanations that cannot be tested. Or when scientific descriptions are expected to provide moral purpose by themselves.

Humanity needs both truth and meaning. The difficulty is keeping them connected without confusing them.

A mature culture should be capable of telling meaningful stories while allowing those stories to be questioned. This is harder than it sounds. Stories become identities. Identities resist revision.

Yet human progress often requires revising inherited myths. Slavery was once justified through stories. Empire was justified through stories. Racial hierarchy was justified through stories. Gender inequality was justified through stories. New moral narratives challenged them.

Myths are therefore not only conservative. They can be revolutionary.

A story about universal human dignity can weaken older stories about inherited superiority. A story about equal citizenship can reorganize institutions.

The question is never whether humans will live by stories. We will.

The question is which stories survive scrutiny. And whether we remain capable of rewriting them when reality demands it.
