Human inequality did not begin with civilization.

But civilization changed its scale.

Even small communities can contain differences in prestige, influence, age, skill, gender roles, and access to resources.

Perfect equality is unlikely to have existed everywhere.

Yet many mobile hunter-gatherer societies placed strong limits on accumulation and domination.

Mobility matters.

If a group moves frequently, possessions are costly.

Large stores of wealth are difficult to carry.

If an aggressive leader becomes intolerable, people may leave.

These conditions constrain hierarchy.

Sedentism changes them.

When communities remain in one place, resources can be stored.

Food surpluses accumulate.

Valuable land becomes defendable.

Buildings become permanent.

Property becomes meaningful in new ways.

Once wealth can persist, inequality can persist too.

Some people control more.

Others inherit less.

The archaeological record begins showing increasing differences in burials, housing, and possessions in some societies.

One grave contains extraordinary goods.

Another contains little.

Material inequality becomes visible.

This does not mean every settled society became hierarchical immediately.

Human social arrangements were diverse.

Some communities maintained relatively egalitarian structures for long periods.

The path toward inequality was not automatic.

But new economic possibilities made durable hierarchy easier.

Inheritance is especially important.

A temporary difference becomes a class system when advantages transfer across generations.

If one person is a successful hunter, that does not automatically make their child permanently privileged.

If one family owns productive land, livestock, stores, or political office, privilege can become hereditary.

History begins to harden.

Birth starts determining future opportunity.

This is one of civilization's most consequential transformations.

Inequality also creates ideology.

Large differences in wealth and power are difficult to maintain through force alone.

They need justification.

The ruler is chosen by the gods.

The noble bloodline is superior.

The social order is natural.

The poor deserve their condition.

Men should rule women.

One group is civilized and another barbaric.

Hierarchy produces stories that explain hierarchy.

This pattern will repeat through empires, caste systems, aristocracies, colonialism, race, and class.

Power seeks legitimacy.

Legitimacy often presents history as nature.

This is why inequality is both material and symbolic.

People possess unequal resources.

Then cultures explain why the inequality is proper.

Once accepted, the explanation becomes self-reinforcing.

Those at the top receive education, nutrition, connections, and authority.

Their later success is then used as proof of natural superiority.

Structure disguises itself as merit.

This does not mean all inequality is unjust.

People differ in effort, skill, risk, preference, and responsibility.

Some differences in reward may be useful or fair.

The philosophical problem is not difference itself.

It is domination and inherited disadvantage.

How much inequality can a society tolerate before freedom becomes formal rather than real?

A person may be legally free but lack meaningful choices because of poverty.

Another may possess enormous influence without formal political office because wealth buys access.

Equality before law does not automatically create equality of power.

These questions remain unresolved today.

Their roots are ancient.

Inequality may also affect trust.

Highly unequal societies can produce social distance.

Lives become difficult to compare.

The wealthy and poor inhabit different institutions, neighborhoods, schools, and risks.

Shared identity weakens.

This creates political tension.

Human beings evolved in relatively small groups where extreme permanent wealth gaps were often constrained.

Modern economies can produce differences beyond anything ancestral psychology encountered.

This may help explain why inequality generates moral emotion.

People do not object only to having little.

They object to unfair comparison.

Humans are sensitive to relative treatment.

Experiments in behavioral economics repeatedly show willingness to reject unequal outcomes even at personal cost.

Fairness appears deeply social.

The first durable inequalities therefore represent more than an economic change.

They mark a shift in the architecture of human relationships.

Power could become stored.

Status could become inherited.

Wealth could outlive the person who acquired it.

Social order could become layered.

The world of mobile communities was beginning to change.

The next transformation would accelerate everything.

Humans began deliberately producing food.

Agriculture would create surpluses, villages, population growth, property, states, and eventually cities.

It would also create new forms of labor, disease, dependency, and inequality.

The world before civilization was ending.
