Socrates left no writings of his own.

Yet he became one of the most influential figures in philosophy.

This is fitting.

His method lived through conversation.

Ask a question.

Receive an answer.

Test the answer.

Expose contradiction.

Ask again.

The goal was not merely to win.

It was to discover whether we actually understand the concepts we use confidently.

What is justice?

Courage?

Virtue?

Knowledge?

People often begin with certainty.

Then definition collapses under questioning.

This is the Socratic shock.

We use important words every day without understanding them precisely.

A politician speaks of freedom.

A parent speaks of respect.

A citizen speaks of justice.

Ask what each means, and disagreement appears.

Socrates turned that discomfort into philosophy.

The examined life begins with intellectual humility.

I may be wrong.

This sounds simple.

It is psychologically difficult.

Identity becomes attached to belief.

Being corrected feels like losing status.

Socrates made not-knowing a strength.

Recognizing ignorance is the beginning of inquiry.

This attitude later becomes essential to science.

A hypothesis must be vulnerable to evidence.

A theory that cannot be wrong cannot learn.

Socratic questioning also has moral significance.

People often behave badly while believing themselves justified.

Examination exposes rationalization.

Why do I want this?

Is my reason consistent?

Would I accept the same rule if applied to me?

Self-questioning becomes ethical discipline.

This is why the examined life can be uncomfortable.

It threatens convenient stories.

Socrates also challenged authority.

Not necessarily by proposing a political revolution, but by refusing to equate social status with wisdom.

Generals.

Politicians.

Poets.

Craftsmen.

All could be questioned.

No title guarantees understanding.

This is dangerous in hierarchical societies.

Authority depends partly on appearing certain.

Philosophy asks for reasons.

That demand can destabilize prestige.

Athens eventually sentenced Socrates to death.

The charges included impiety and corrupting the young.

The historical context is complex.

But the symbolic meaning became enormous.

A city that celebrated public debate killed a man famous for questioning.

The contradiction has haunted political philosophy ever since.

How tolerant should a society be of criticism?

Can democracy survive people who challenge its assumptions?

Can authority tolerate embarrassment?

Socrates' death transformed him into a model of philosophical integrity.

According to Plato's portrayal, he refused to abandon his principles simply to survive.

This raises another question.

Is a belief worth dying for?

Sometimes courage requires sacrifice.

Sometimes stubbornness disguises itself as courage.

The difference depends on whether the belief survives examination.

That is the Socratic standard.

The phrase "the unexamined life is not worth living" is famous because it expresses a radical view of humanity.

Life is not only to be lived.

It is to be understood.

But examination has limits.

Too much reflection can become paralysis.

Human beings must act with incomplete knowledge.

Socrates does not solve this.

He gives us a discipline.

Before certainty, question.

Before authority, ask for reasons.

Before moral confidence, test consistency.

Civilization creates powerful institutions.

Socrates creates a citizen who asks:

Why should I believe you?

That question is one of freedom's foundations.
