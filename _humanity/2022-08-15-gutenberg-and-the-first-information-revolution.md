Writing allowed memory to leave the brain.

Printing allowed writing to multiply.

That difference transformed civilization.

Movable-type printing had earlier histories in East Asia.

Johannes Gutenberg's fifteenth-century printing system in Europe combined movable metal type, press technology, ink, and existing paper networks in a way that made large-scale book production dramatically more efficient.

The result was not simply cheaper books.

It was a new information environment.

Before print, copying a manuscript required enormous labor.

Texts were expensive.

Errors accumulated.

Distribution was slow.

After print, hundreds or thousands of near-identical copies could circulate.

The cost of replication collapsed.

This is one of history's most important economic changes.

Information became easier to reproduce than before.

When reproduction cost falls, institutions change.

Books become more available.

Literacy becomes more useful.

Scholars can compare the same text.

Authors can reach distant readers.

Religious ideas spread faster.

Political criticism spreads faster.

Scientific findings spread faster.

So do rumors.

Propaganda.

Polemics.

Falsehood.

Printing did not make information true.

It made information abundant.

This is the same distinction that digital media later intensifies.

More communication does not automatically produce more wisdom.

The Reformation demonstrates print's political power.

Martin Luther's arguments traveled through pamphlets and printed texts at speeds earlier religious controversies could not easily match.

The printing press did not cause the Reformation alone.

But it changed the scale of religious disagreement.

A local dispute could become continental.

Authority lost control over distribution.

This is revolutionary.

Before mass print, institutions could regulate access more easily.

After print, censorship becomes an arms race.

Ban one book.

Another edition appears.

Ideas become harder to contain.

Printing also standardized language.

Editors and printers chose spellings.

Popular texts reinforced particular dialects.

National languages gained stability.

This helped create larger reading publics.

Language becomes political infrastructure.

People who never meet consume the same texts.

They begin imagining themselves as part of the same community.

Printing therefore contributes to later nationalism.

It also changes science.

A diagram can be reproduced.

A table preserved.

An observation compared.

Researchers can build on work they did not witness personally.

Scientific knowledge becomes cumulative at larger scale.

Again, information infrastructure changes intellectual possibility.

Printing also changes the self.

Private reading becomes more common.

A person can encounter ideas alone.

Interpret scripture personally.

Read fiction silently.

Develop an interior relationship with text.

This increases individual intellectual autonomy.

It can also weaken traditional communal interpretation.

The reader becomes authority.

This is part of modern individualism.

Gutenberg's revolution resembles the internet in a deeper sense.

Both reduce the cost of copying information.

Both weaken old gatekeepers.

Both empower outsiders.

Both generate anxiety about misinformation.

Both create new forms of authority.

Both produce information abundance faster than societies develop norms for managing it.

Technology changes distribution first.

Institutions catch up later.

The printing press therefore was not merely a machine.

It was a shift in who could speak, who could read, and how fast ideas could travel.

Once information becomes cheap to copy, power becomes harder to monopolize.

And harder to control.
