Humanity is African in origin.

This is not metaphor.

Our species evolved in Africa.

The deepest branches of modern human genetic diversity are found there.

Every population outside Africa descends largely from ancestors who left the continent relatively recently in the history of Homo sapiens.

But "the migration out of Africa" was not one event.

Different human populations moved beyond Africa at different times.

Some earlier dispersals left limited genetic traces in later populations.

A major expansion beginning roughly tens of thousands of years ago contributed substantially to the ancestry of most people outside Africa today.

The routes were shaped by climate and geography.

Northeastern Africa connects to the Levant.

The Red Sea region creates other possibilities.

During colder periods, sea levels changed.

Deserts expanded and contracted.

Green corridors appeared and disappeared.

Migration depended on windows of opportunity.

Why leave?

Perhaps populations followed animals.

Sought resources.

Responded to drought.

Expanded gradually as groups split.

There may have been no concept of "leaving Africa."

People moved into neighboring landscapes.

Their descendants moved again.

What looks dramatic on a map may have felt ordinary from within.

This is an important lesson about historical scale.

A process can transform the planet without any participant understanding the final outcome.

No group stood at a border and announced the beginning of global human expansion.

History accumulated from local decisions.

Migration also reveals how identity changes with time.

Modern political borders make movement seem exceptional.

Prehistoric humans lived before states.

There were territories and social boundaries, but no passports.

Human populations have always moved.

Climate changes move people.

Resources move people.

Conflict moves people.

Opportunity moves people.

Marriage moves people.

Curiosity moves people.

Migration is not an anomaly in human history.

It is one of its constants.

This matters because modern societies often imagine populations as ancient fixed blocks.

Genetics and archaeology reveal movement, mixture, and replacement.

A population living in a region today may not descend exclusively from people who lived there thousands of years ago.

Human geography is layered.

Waves arrive.

Intermix.

Disappear.

Leave descendants.

Identity feels permanent because a human lifetime is short.

History is longer.

The out-of-Africa expansion also involved encounters with other human lineages.

Neanderthals.

Denisovans.

Possibly others we have not yet identified clearly.

Modern genomes retain traces of these meetings.

Migration therefore did not occur across empty territory.

It involved interaction.

The result was not pure replacement everywhere.

It was a complicated network of competition, coexistence, and gene flow.

This should make racial essentialism scientifically difficult.

Modern racial categories are recent social constructions imposed on populations whose genetic differences are gradual and overlapping.

Most human genetic variation exists within populations rather than dividing humanity into clean biological races.

Our species is young.

We are closely related.

The visible traits used historically to classify people represent a tiny fraction of human biological variation.

The African origin of humanity therefore carries a moral irony.

Societies later invented racial hierarchies while all humans shared recent common ancestry in Africa.

The same species that migrated and mixed created myths of purity.

Migration also demonstrates the strength of cultural adaptation.

Leaving Africa meant entering new climates, foods, pathogens, and seasonal cycles.

Knowledge had to change.

Tools changed.

Clothing changed.

Social strategies changed.

Culture became increasingly local.

This produced diversity.

Languages diverged.

Traditions diverged.

Bodies also adapted genetically to local environments over time.

Skin pigmentation changed in response to different ultraviolet conditions.

Other adaptations involved altitude, diet, and disease.

Human diversity is real.

But diversity emerged inside one interconnected species.

Difference and unity are both true.

That balance is difficult for political thinking.

We often emphasize one at the expense of the other.

Either everyone is treated as identical.

Or difference is exaggerated into hierarchy.

Evolution suggests a better view.

Humans are variations on a very recent common theme.

The first migrations out of Africa were the beginning of global humanity.

They turned one regional species into a planetary one.

Everything that came later would happen inside that expanding map.
