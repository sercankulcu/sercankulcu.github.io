Modern societies tell people to become themselves.

The instruction sounds liberating.

It would have sounded strange in many earlier social worlds.

For much of history, identity was heavily inherited.

Family.

Village.

Religion.

Occupation.

Gender role.

Class.

Local custom.

The question "Who am I?" had many answers before the individual supplied one.

Modernity weakens some of these inherited structures.

Cities increase anonymity.

Markets increase mobility.

Education expands options.

Religious pluralism increases choice.

States recognize individual rights.

Careers become less hereditary.

Marriage becomes more personal.

Identity becomes a project.

This creates freedom.

A person can leave the village.

Change profession.

Change belief.

Choose relationships.

Invent a lifestyle.

The modern individual can resist inherited expectations.

This is a profound gain.

But every gain has cost.

If identity is chosen, the individual becomes responsible for choosing well.

The old question was:

What is my role?

The modern question becomes:

What should I become?

This can produce anxiety.

There are too many possibilities.

Career.

City.

Partner.

Belief.

Style.

Values.

Social identity.

The self becomes continuous decision.

Consumer culture intensifies this.

Products become identity signals.

What you wear.

Drive.

Watch.

Eat.

Listen to.

Purchase.

The market offers selves.

Choice feels personal.

It is also commercially structured.

Authenticity becomes a product category.

This is one of modernity's ironies.

People seek unique identity through mass-produced options.

Individualism also changes responsibility.

Success becomes personal achievement.

Failure becomes personal deficiency.

Structural conditions disappear from the story.

If everyone is an autonomous individual, poverty can be interpreted as bad choice rather than unequal opportunity.

Loneliness can be treated as personal failure rather than social structure.

The ideology of individualism can therefore hide dependence.

No person is self-made.

Language inherited.

Education received.

Roads built by others.

Knowledge accumulated by strangers.

Care provided in childhood.

Institutions maintained collectively.

The individual is real.

Self-sufficiency is largely myth.

Modern societies therefore need a balance.

People should have space to choose.

They also need communities that make choice meaningful.

Freedom without belonging can become isolation.

Belonging without freedom can become suffocation.

This tension appears in family life.

Work.

Politics.

Religion.

Digital culture.

Social media creates a particularly strange form of individualism.

People build personal profiles.

Personal brands.

Personal feeds.

The self becomes public object.

Individuality is displayed continuously to others.

This creates freedom of expression.

It also creates pressure to perform identity.

The person becomes both self and advertisement.

The ancient concern with reputation becomes quantified.

Followers.

Likes.

Views.

Metrics.

Modern individuality therefore can become more socially dependent than it appears.

We construct ourselves in front of crowds.

This raises a philosophical question.

Is there an authentic self beneath all roles?

Perhaps not in the simple sense.

The self may be partly constructed through relationships.

We become different with parents, friends, colleagues, children, strangers.

Identity is plural.

Modernity asks us to unify it into one coherent person.

That may be impossible.

The modern human became more individual because institutions gave people more choice.

The next challenge is learning how to use freedom without turning the self into a permanent optimization project.

Being a person should be more than managing a personal brand.
