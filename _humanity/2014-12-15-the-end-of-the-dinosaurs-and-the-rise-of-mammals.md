For more than 150 million years, dinosaurs dominated many terrestrial ecosystems.

Then, in geological terms, everything changed quickly.

About 66 million years ago, an asteroid struck Earth near what is now the Yucatán Peninsula. The impact released enormous energy, triggered fires and tsunamis, injected dust and aerosols into the atmosphere, and disrupted climate and photosynthesis.

Food webs collapsed.

Non-avian dinosaurs disappeared.

Many other groups vanished with them.

But mammals survived.

That sentence is often told as though small mammals had been waiting patiently for their chance to inherit the world.

Reality was more complicated.

Mammals had already existed for a very long time alongside dinosaurs. They were not failed creatures awaiting promotion.

They occupied real ecological niches and had diversified in significant ways before the extinction.

What changed after the catastrophe was opportunity.

Large ecological roles became vacant.

With many dominant competitors and predators gone, surviving mammals expanded into new environments and ways of life.

Over millions of years, mammalian diversity increased dramatically.

Some became large herbivores.

Some became predators.

Some returned to the sea.

Some took to the air.

Some moved into trees.

One branch would eventually produce primates.

This sequence shows why evolutionary success depends partly on timing.

A trait may be useful without producing dramatic expansion until ecological circumstances change.

Small body size, flexible diets, burrowing behavior, and reproductive strategies may have helped some mammals survive the immediate post-impact world.

After survival came radiation.

A lineage that survives catastrophe can become the starting point for futures that were impossible before.

Again, humanity appears only very late.

There was no direct evolutionary highway from the asteroid impact to Homo sapiens.

There were tens of millions of years of branching lineages, environmental changes, extinctions, and accidents.

But the extinction removed a major constraint on mammalian evolution.

Without it, our own lineage might not exist.

This is one reason the phrase "the rise of mammals" should be treated carefully.

Evolution has no throne.

Dinosaurs did not fail in a simple sense. Birds are living dinosaurs, and they remain extraordinarily successful.

The world after the impact was not a replacement of inferior creatures by superior ones.

It was a reorganization of ecological possibilities.

The story also reveals the danger of equating dominance with permanence.

Dinosaurs were successful for a period vastly longer than human civilization has existed.

From the perspective of a dinosaur living a million years before the impact, the dominance of its broader group may have looked permanent.

Human societies make the same mistake on shorter timescales.

We mistake duration for inevitability.

Empires do it.

Economic systems do it.

Technological platforms do it.

Species do it, if only metaphorically.

The history of life is filled with arrangements that lasted long enough to look eternal.

Then conditions changed.

For mammals, the asteroid was not "good."

Countless individual animals died.

Ecosystems collapsed.

The later success of some survivors does not convert catastrophe into benevolence.

This is another important philosophical distinction.

Benefiting from an event does not mean the event occurred for your benefit.

Human beings often search for moral structure in history.

We interpret survival as deserving, victory as superiority, and later success as proof that earlier suffering had a purpose.

Natural history resists this temptation.

The asteroid had no ethical intention.

Survival was filtered by biology and chance.

Meaning was added later by minds capable of telling stories.

That does not make meaning false.

It makes meaning something we create rather than something every event obviously contains.

The rise of mammals eventually produced creatures with larger brains, complex social behavior, and increasingly flexible ways of interacting with environments.

Among them were primates.

Their story would be shaped by trees, hands, eyes, social groups, and brains.

The road to humanity did not begin with an animal trying to become human.

It began with ordinary organisms solving ordinary problems.

Climbing.

Grasping.

Seeing.

Remembering.

Cooperating.

Surviving.

The extraordinary came later.
