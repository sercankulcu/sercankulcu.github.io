A human infant cannot survive alone. That fact may be one of the foundations of civilization.

Humans are born dependent. They require food, protection, teaching, and social interaction for years. This makes community biologically necessary.

But human communities became more than childcare systems. They became networks of memory, obligation, identity, and cooperation.

Early human groups were probably flexible. Membership changed. Groups split and merged. Marriage connected communities. Seasonal gatherings created larger networks. People moved between bands.

This fluidity matters because the image of a fixed prehistoric tribe can be misleading. Human social organization was dynamic.

Cooperation solved practical problems. Large game could be hunted together. Children could be watched by multiple adults. Food could be shared. Injured individuals could receive care. Knowledge could be pooled. The group reduced individual risk.

But cooperation creates its own problem.

What prevents cheating? If everyone shares food, why not take without contributing?

Human societies developed mechanisms of reputation and punishment. Gossip. Ridicule. Exclusion. Reciprocity. Moral norms. These mechanisms do not require formal law. Small communities can regulate behavior socially.

The fear of losing reputation is powerful because survival depends on relationships. This may explain why humans remain sensitive to shame. Shame is social pain. It tells the individual that membership is threatened. In a small group, that threat could be serious.

Modern institutions still use ancient mechanisms. Reviews. Ratings. Public criticism. Social media. Professional reputation.

The scale changed. The psychology did not.

Early communities also created identity. Who are "we"? Who are "they"? Shared language, kinship, ritual, and history made groups coherent.

This increased trust within groups. It could also increase hostility toward outsiders.

Cooperation and tribalism are therefore linked. The same mechanism that creates solidarity can create exclusion. This duality will shape all later political history.

Humans are unusually cooperative. We are also unusually capable of coordinated violence. Both abilities depend on group identity.

The moral challenge is not eliminating group belonging. That is probably impossible and perhaps undesirable. The challenge is expanding the circle of belonging.

Can "we" include a village? A city? A nation? Humanity?

The history of moral progress can partly be read as repeated attempts to widen that circle.

Early communities also preserved knowledge across generations. Older individuals had value beyond physical strength. They remembered droughts. Routes. Plant cycles. Conflicts. Stories.

An elder was a living archive.

This may have increased the evolutionary value of long human lifespans after reproduction. Grandparents can contribute to descendant survival through care and knowledge.

Community extends biology through memory. It also creates culture.

A technique practiced by one person becomes tradition when others learn it. A story becomes mythology when generations repeat it. A custom becomes norm when deviation carries social cost.

Culture is community remembered.

This is why isolated individuals cannot create civilization. Innovation matters. Transmission matters more. A lost invention is not cumulative culture. A preserved invention becomes foundation.

The first human communities therefore contained the basic architecture of everything that followed. Cooperation. Conflict. Reputation. Teaching. Identity. Obligation. Memory. Leadership. Resistance to leadership.

None required cities.

Civilization later scaled these dynamics beyond face-to-face relationships. States formalized punishment. Markets formalized exchange. Religions formalized ritual. Schools formalized teaching. Archives formalized memory.

But the social problems were ancient.

How do we trust? How do we share? Who leads? Who belongs? What happens to cheaters? What do we owe the vulnerable?

Every political philosophy eventually returns to questions that small human groups had already been solving for thousands of years.
