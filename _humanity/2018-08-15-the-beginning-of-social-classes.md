A difference becomes a class when it persists.

One person may be temporarily richer than another.

A class system appears when social position becomes durable, inherited, or institutionally reinforced.

Agriculture created conditions in which this became easier.

Surplus could be stored.

Land could be controlled.

Specialists could be supported.

Political authority could become permanent.

Some people worked fields.

Others organized labor.

Others performed rituals.

Others fought.

Roles differentiated.

Difference became structure.

The first hierarchies were not identical everywhere.

Some communities remained relatively egalitarian.

Others developed pronounced status differences.

Archaeology detects this through housing size, burial goods, diet, monuments, and access to rare materials.

A society leaves inequality in material form.

One grave contains ornaments and imported goods.

Another does not.

One household occupies more space.

One group eats better.

Class begins to become visible.

Why would people accept hierarchy?

Sometimes because leaders provide coordination.

Organizing irrigation.

Defense.

Trade.

Conflict resolution.

Ritual.

Large projects require decision-making.

Leadership can solve collective-action problems.

But useful coordination can become permanent privilege.

A leader controls surplus.

Surplus supports followers.

Followers protect the leader.

Authority reproduces itself.

This is how administration can become domination.

Class systems also need stories.

If inequality is obvious, people ask why.

The answers often become ideological.

The ruler descends from gods.

The priest understands sacred knowledge.

The warrior protects society.

The noble possesses superior blood.

The poor occupy their natural place.

Class becomes moralized.

This is crucial.

Power lasts longer when hierarchy feels legitimate.

Force is expensive.

Belief is efficient.

A peasant who thinks the order is sacred requires less coercion than one who thinks it is theft.

Ideology therefore becomes infrastructure.

It stabilizes inequality.

But class systems are never completely secure.

People compare.

Resentment grows.

Rebellions occur.

Elites compete among themselves.

States collapse.

New hierarchies replace old ones.

History is filled with class conflict because durable inequality produces durable tension.

Class also shapes consciousness.

A person's life options depend on position.

Education.

Marriage.

Occupation.

Nutrition.

Safety.

Political voice.

Even ideas about what is possible can become class-specific.

A child born into privilege sees opportunity as normal.

A child born into deprivation may see barriers as natural.

Structure becomes internalized.

This is one reason inequality can persist without explicit coercion.

People adapt expectations to circumstances.

Modern societies often claim to replace inherited class with merit.

This is a major moral improvement when real.

But inheritance continues through wealth, education, networks, neighborhoods, and cultural capital.

The language changes faster than the structure.

A society can call itself meritocratic while reproducing advantage.

This does not mean individual effort is meaningless.

It means effort operates within unequal starting conditions.

Agriculture did not create every human hierarchy.

Age, gender, prestige, and skill differences existed earlier.

But agricultural surplus allowed hierarchy to harden.

Power became storable.

Roles became institutional.

Class became intergenerational.

A child could be born into a position before doing anything.

This is one of civilization's deepest paradoxes.

Society becomes more organized.

Individual destiny can become less flexible.

The structures that create scale also create cages.

Human history will repeatedly struggle with this trade-off.

How much hierarchy is necessary for coordination?

How much becomes domination?

There is no final answer.

But the question begins in the first societies where surplus became power.
