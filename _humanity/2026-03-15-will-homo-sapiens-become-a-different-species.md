Homo sapiens will not remain unchanged forever. No species does.

The question is whether future change will continue mostly through natural selection or become increasingly intentional.

Humans already alter biology. Vaccination changes survival, medicine changes reproduction, Cesarean sections alter some selection pressures, and eyeglasses compensate for vision.

Technology has always modified the relationship between genes and survival.

Biotechnology makes intervention more direct through gene editing, embryo selection, synthetic biology, neural interfaces, prosthetics, and pharmaceutical enhancement. These technologies could eventually change inherited traits.

If modifications accumulate, future humans may differ substantially from us.

But becoming a new species is not simply becoming technologically enhanced. Species boundaries involve reproduction, genetics, population separation, and evolutionary divergence.

A genetically modified population could remain fully interbreeding with unmodified humans. The biological classification would depend on actual divergence.

Still, cultural separation can accelerate biological separation if populations stop mixing. Space settlement could create such conditions.

A human population living for generations on Mars or in artificial habitats would experience different gravity, radiation, diet, and reproductive constraints. Natural selection could diverge. Intentional engineering might diverge faster.

This raises identity questions. At what point does descendant become other?

Neanderthals were human relatives. We interbred. If future descendants look different, think differently, and live elsewhere, will they still see us as ancestors?

Probably.

Will we recognize them morally as us? That question is more difficult.

History gives reason for caution. Humans discriminate over tiny differences: skin color, language, religion, ethnicity.

Imagine differences in lifespan, cognition, sensory ability, or body design. Biological inequality could become politically explosive.

Enhancement may create classes that are not only economically but physically distinct. The old problem of inequality enters the genome.

This is why biotechnology cannot be treated as personal choice alone. If parents can select traits, social pressure follows.

A voluntary enhancement can become expected. If employers prefer enhanced cognition, refusal becomes costly.

Choice becomes norm.

The same pattern already appears with education and technology. Biology would make it deeper.

There is also the question of diversity. Engineering humans toward one ideal could reduce variation.

But variation is resilience. Evolution depends on it. Culture benefits from it.

A society convinced that it knows the optimal human risks designing around temporary prejudices. What traits would a nineteenth-century society have selected? Obedience? Gender conformity? Racial myths?

Every era mistakes some preferences for truths. This should make human enhancement ethically cautious.

Yet refusing all modification is also a choice. Genetic disease creates suffering. Correcting harmful mutations could be morally compelling.

The line between therapy and enhancement is unstable. Restore ordinary vision. Improve vision beyond ordinary. Prevent dementia. Increase memory beyond ordinary.

Where does treatment end?

No permanent boundary exists.

Will Homo sapiens become another species? Given enough time, almost certainly in some form. Whether that happens naturally, technologically, or through both is unknown.

The deeper question is whether moral community can survive biological divergence.

Human rights currently rests on shared humanity. Future ethics may need a broader foundation: consciousness, capacity to suffer, agency, relationship, personhood.

If our descendants become post-human, perhaps the word human will become less important than the question we first encountered with animals and machines.

Who counts morally?

The future may force us to answer without using species membership as shortcut.
