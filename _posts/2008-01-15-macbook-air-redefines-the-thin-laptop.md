Steve Jobs introduced the MacBook Air at Macworld on January 15, 2008, sliding it out of a manila envelope to illustrate its thinness. The MacBook Air was 0.16 inches at its thinnest edge and 0.76 inches at its thickest, 3 lb (1.36 kg), with a 13.3-inch 1280×800 display. It launched at $1,799 with a 1.6 GHz Intel Core 2 Duo (a custom LV — low voltage — version using a 45nm die in a smaller package than standard laptops), 2GB RAM soldered to the board, an 80GB 4200 RPM PATA hard drive, or optionally a 64GB SSD for $999 more. Ports: one USB-A, one Micro-DVI, one 3.5mm headphone jack. No Ethernet, no optical drive, no removable battery.

The battery was fixed — users could not swap it — requiring a service visit for replacement. Optical discs (CDs, DVDs) were accessed via Remote Disc, a software feature that let the MacBook Air share another Mac or PC's optical drive over Wi-Fi. USB peripherals required a hub since there was only one port. The hard drive version's 4200 RPM drive was significantly slower than the 5400–7200 RPM drives in competing laptops, making the machine feel sluggish for disk-intensive tasks; the SSD option ($999 more) solved this but cost $2,798 total. Intel had not publicly announced the custom low-voltage Core 2 Duo package before Apple's announcement, surprising the chip industry with how aggressively Apple had miniaturized the design.

Intel launched the Ultrabook category in 2011, defining a PC specification requiring less than 0.8 inches thick, less than 4 lb, and with fast boot times and long battery life, directly referencing MacBook Air's commercial success as the model. ASUS launched the ZenBook at $999 in October 2011 as the first Ultrabook; Lenovo's ThinkPad X1 Carbon, Dell XPS 13, and HP Spectre followed. The MacBook Air's 2008 design established the template for premium thin laptops that every major PC manufacturer followed across the next decade, sacrificing optical drives, Ethernet jacks, and user-replaceable batteries for portable form factors.
