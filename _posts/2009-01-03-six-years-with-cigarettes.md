For six years, there was almost always a pack of cigarettes with me.

Every day.

I did not leave home without them.

I did not seriously imagine leaving them behind either.

At the time, I described cigarettes as an old and very dear friend.

That sounds strange when I read it now.

But it accurately describes the relationship I had built.

Smoking had stopped being a single action.

It had become part of everything.

A meal.

A conversation.

A bus stop.

A bad night.

A good night.

Waiting.

Thinking.

Driving.

Drinking tea.

Being alone.

Being with people.

The cigarette was there so often that it no longer needed a reason.

Sometimes I walked half an hour through snow at night because the pack had run out.

That should have told me something.

Instead, I treated it as perfectly reasonable.

The problem was simple.

No cigarettes.

The solution was equally simple.

Go find cigarettes.

Weather was irrelevant.

Time was irrelevant.

Distance was irrelevant.

Addiction is very good at making ridiculous behavior feel practical.

There were times when I chose cigarettes over food.

Not in some dramatic literary sense.

Literally.

If money was limited, cigarettes could win.

I could eat something cheap.

Bread would do.

The pack felt less negotiable.

When people ask what addiction looks like, they sometimes expect something spectacular.

Often it looks much more ordinary.

A series of small decisions that would make very little sense if the addictive thing were removed from the equation.

Walking through snow.

Missing transportation.

Spending money you should spend elsewhere.

Organizing your day around the next opportunity.

None of these decisions feels dramatic at the time.

Together, they quietly create a system.

I also gave up friendships because people disliked the smell.

At the time, I could frame that as incompatibility.

They did not like cigarettes.

I smoked.

Simple.

But priorities reveal themselves through choices.

If the presence of a cigarette matters more than the presence of a person, that is worth noticing.

Smoking also created friendships.

Someone asks for a cigarette.

Someone asks for a lighter.

A conversation begins.

This happened often enough that cigarettes felt social.

Outside a building, smokers would find each other.

People who might never have spoken inside suddenly had something in common.

"Do you have a light?"

That was enough.

A cigarette created ten minutes.

Ten minutes created conversation.

Conversation occasionally created friendship.

This made the relationship with smoking more complicated.

It was easy to see what it gave.

A pause.

Company.

Ritual.

Something to do with the hands.

An excuse to step outside.

A reason to begin a conversation.

It was harder to see what it was taking because much of that happened slowly.

I burned my hand.

Curtains.

A sofa.

Probably other things I have forgotten.

I fought over cigarettes.

I waited for another bus or minibus because I had not finished the one in my hand.

That may be one of the best examples of how completely smoking could reorganize ordinary decisions.

Transportation had arrived.

I needed transportation.

But the cigarette was not finished.

So the transportation could wait.

It is funny until you think about who was actually making the decision.

Me?

Or the cigarette?

Smokers often give cigarettes personalities.

A friend.

A companion.

Something that listens.

Something that is always there.

I did it too.

Especially at night.

Wake up feeling bad.

Light one.

Sit.

Think.

There was no interruption.

No advice.

No argument.

No one telling you that you were overthinking things.

The cigarette simply burned while you talked to yourself.

It was easy to confuse that ritual with companionship.

At the time I even asked:

Where else can you find a friend who never interrupts you, listens constantly, wakes up with you in the middle of the night and never leaves your side?

It sounded almost romantic.

There is one problem with that comparison.

Real friends do not need you to remain dependent on them.

A cigarette does.

That distinction was much less interesting to me then.

What interested me was consistency.

Sad?

Smoke.

Happy?

Smoke.

Finished eating?

Smoke.

Started drinking tea?

Smoke.

Had an argument?

Smoke.

Solved the argument?

Smoke.

The event changed.

The response did not.

That is how rituals become automatic.

Eventually the cigarette stops answering a need.

It answers the fact that it is time for a cigarette.

This is probably why smokers can struggle to explain what they actually enjoy about smoking.

"What do you find in it?"

I heard that question many times.

I never had a useful answer.

Because by then the question was almost wrong.

I was not carefully evaluating the cigarette each time and deciding that it offered enough pleasure to justify another one.

The evaluation had happened years earlier.

Now there was only continuation.

Habits are powerful because they remove decisions.

The first cigarette may require a decision.

The thousandth rarely does.

It belongs to the architecture of the day.

Back then, I also wrote something very simple:

Yes, I am addicted.

There was no attempt to hide it.

No claim that I could stop whenever I wanted.

No complicated explanation.

I knew.

That admission did not automatically change anything.

Knowing and changing are different tasks.

People often assume that once someone understands a harmful pattern, the obvious next step is to stop.

Human beings are not that efficient.

You can understand something perfectly and continue doing it.

You can even joke about your dependence while obeying it.

Awareness is useful.

It is not magic.

The final sentence of that old entry is the part that tells me most about the person writing it.

I wished the cigarette would never end.

Not smoking in general.

That particular cigarette.

That moment.

The burning thing in my hand.

Maybe that is what addiction often tries to sell.

Not forever.

Just this one.

One more cigarette.

One more drink.

One more hour.

One more time.

The future is easier to ignore when desire is divided into small units.

Years later, I do not read that old entry as a celebration of cigarettes.

I read it as a remarkably clear description of dependence written by someone living inside it.

Almost every line says the same thing without needing to say it directly.

I walked for it.

Waited for it.

Spent for it.

Argued for it.

Chose it over other things.

Used it when sad.

Used it when happy.

Built routines around it.

Called it a friend.

Then admitted I was addicted.

The word "friend" makes sense emotionally.

But the word "addiction" explains the story better.

That is one reason old writing can be useful.

It lets you meet a version of yourself before you knew how the story would look from a distance.

That person does not know what you know now.

He is simply describing his world.

A pack in his pocket.

A lighter nearby.

A cigarette in his hand.

And the sincere wish that it would not end.
