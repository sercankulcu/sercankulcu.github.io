Some cities enter your memory through monuments.

Others through a bottle of water.

In July 2010, I wrote a short entry about Kalabak water from Eskişehir.

The first thing I remembered was practical.

The one-liter plastic bottles seemed to be filled all the way to the top.

Normally there is a little empty space below the cap.

With these, at least as I remembered them, there was almost none.

Open the bottle carelessly and some of the water ended up on your shirt, your hand, or the table.

That alone was enough to make the product memorable.

But the real memory was older.

Four years earlier, four of us had gone to Eskişehir for a four-day business trip.

People there proudly told us that Kalabak water was famous.

They made us drink it.

Then, laughing, they said something like:

"Now that you've drunk Kalabak water, you're half Eskişehirli."

We laughed too.

Then something unexpected happened.

After that trip, we started going to Eskişehir roughly once a month.

The joke became strangely accurate.

Not legally.

Not geographically.

But in the way repetition slowly gives you a relationship with a city.

The first visit was work.

Later visits became familiarity.

That difference matters.

A place changes after you stop needing directions for everything.

The first time, you look outward.

Street names.

Landmarks.

Where is the hotel?

Where are we supposed to meet?

Where do we eat?

How do we get back?

The city is a problem to solve.

After enough visits, it becomes background.

You begin recognizing roads before signs.

You know where you can park.

You remember where you ate last time.

The trip stops feeling like travel.

It becomes a route.

This is one of the strange things about repeated business travel.

You can visit a city many times without ever becoming a tourist.

You may never see the places visitors are supposed to see.

Your map is shaped by work.

Office.

Hotel.

Customer.

Restaurant.

Road.

Maybe a café if there is time.

Then back.

You know the city in fragments.

Not landmarks.

Habits.

That is still a kind of knowledge.

Perhaps a more personal one.

I do not remember from the old entry what exactly we were working on in Eskişehir.

The archive does not say.

So I should not invent the project.

What remains is enough:

four friends,

four days,

a business trip,

a bottle of local water,

a joke about becoming half Eskişehirli,

and the fact that we kept returning.

That is already a story.

Work trips have a peculiar atmosphere when you are young.

They feel more important than ordinary workdays.

You are going somewhere.

There is a road.

A hotel, perhaps.

A bag.

People from another company.

Meals in places you did not choose.

Technical problems that suddenly have geography.

Even a small trip can make a young engineer feel temporarily more professional.

You are not merely going to work.

You are going on assignment.

The distinction is partly imaginary.

But imagination matters.

Early career is full of symbols.

Your first business card.

Your first company email.

Your first access card.

Your first meeting where someone asks for your opinion.

Your first business trip.

None of these necessarily changes your competence.

They change how adulthood feels.

You begin collecting evidence that this is now your life.

The road itself becomes part of the work.

Four people in a car can create a very different memory from four people in an office.

There is more time.

More nonsense.

More conversation.

Less formal posture.

People reveal themselves differently when nobody is sitting behind a desk.

Road trips flatten hierarchy.

A colleague becomes the person who insists on stopping for tea.

Someone sleeps immediately.

Someone controls the music.

Someone complains about the route.

Someone always says they know a shortcut.

The company may be paying for the trip.

The memory belongs to the people in the car.

This is why some work memories survive long after the work itself disappears.

You forget the spreadsheet.

You remember lunch.

You forget the presentation.

You remember getting lost.

You forget the exact technical issue.

You remember somebody saying:

"Drink this. Now you're half Eskişehirli."

Memory is not fair to productivity.

The most useful things are rarely the most memorable.

A local product can become a marker for an entire period.

Kalabak water did that.

Taste has this power.

So do smells.

Tea in a certain glass.

A particular soup.

A brand of coffee from a machine in an office corridor.

Years later, the sensory detail brings back the architecture around it.

Who was there.

What age you were.

What kind of work you were doing.

What you worried about.

What you had not learned yet.

This is one reason regional foods and drinks matter beyond quality.

They become geographical passwords.

You eat or drink something and people say:

Now you've really been here.

Cities use food to make belonging playful.

You are not from here?

Try this.

Then we will accept you a little.

It is a friendly ritual.

Almost every place has one.

A bread.

A dessert.

A kebab.

A drink.

A fruit.

A strange preparation locals insist must be tried in exactly one correct place.

The claim is rarely only culinary.

It says:

There is something here that belongs to us.

If you taste it, you participate.

The "half Eskişehirli" joke worked because of that.

It turned consumption into temporary citizenship.

The funny part is that repeated visits later gave the joke more weight.

We actually did begin returning.

Once a month is enough to create rhythm.

Twelve visits a year can alter your mental map of a country.

A city no longer exists only as a name on road signs.

It becomes somewhere you have routines.

That is when belonging becomes interesting.

How many times must you visit a city before it becomes part of your life?

There is no threshold.

One dramatic day can be enough.

Or twenty visits may remain emotionally empty.

Belonging does not follow mileage.

It follows memory.

Some places attach themselves through people.

Some through work.

Some through love.

Some through loss.

Some through food.

Eskişehir, at least in this small archive entry, attached itself through repetition and water.

That is modest.

I like modest attachments.

They feel honest.

Not every city needs to change your life.

Some simply become familiar.

Familiarity itself has value.

It softens geography.

The first time you enter a city, it is "there."

After enough visits, it becomes "we're going again."

A subtle linguistic change.

Distance becomes routine.

The road acquires expectations.

You know where boredom starts.

Where someone will probably ask for a stop.

Where the city begins to feel close.

Journeys gain texture through repetition.

The same road changes because you change.

One trip you are tired.

Another excited.

One has bad weather.

Another good conversation.

The geography is constant.

The internal landscape is not.

That may be why repeated travel creates stronger memories than a single spectacular trip.

Layers accumulate.

You do not have one Eskişehir.

You have versions.

The first four-day visit.

The next monthly trip.

Another visit.

Another.

Eventually the city becomes a stack of ordinary days.

This is closer to living somewhere than tourism is.

Tourism seeks highlights.

Living collects repetition.

That is why locals may love very ordinary things about their city.

A road.

A bakery.

A view from a bus.

A certain light in the afternoon.

Visitors seek significance.

Residents inherit it from repetition.

We were not residents.

But repeated work visits gave us a little of that mechanism.

The archive also preserves my complaint about the bottle design.

This matters because memory should not become too poetic.

Yes, Kalabak water became part of my Eskişehir memory.

Also, I thought they filled the bottle too much.

Both can be true.

Nostalgia is better when it keeps irritation.

Otherwise the past becomes advertising.

Real memories contain bad packaging.

Cold food.

Traffic.

Awkward hotels.

Stupid arguments.

Wrong turns.

A city you like may still have something that annoys you every time.

Affection is not the absence of complaint.

Often the opposite.

You complain because the place has become familiar enough to deserve expectations.

The first-time visitor accepts.

The regular starts saying:

Why do they always do this?

That is practically citizenship.

So maybe the overfilled bottle was evidence that I had become slightly more local.

A tourist says:

Interesting local water.

A regular says:

Why is there no air in this bottle? I'm going to spill this again.

That is progress.

There is another thing I like about the story.

The people in Eskişehir made belonging generous.

They did not ask whether we were born there.

Whether our family came from there.

Whether we knew the history.

They gave us water and a joke.

Done.

Half Eskişehirli.

Of course it was not a serious statement about identity.

That is exactly why it was warm.

Belonging can become dangerous when it is treated as purity.

Who counts.

Who does not.

Who is real.

Who arrived later.

The joke did the opposite.

It expanded the category.

You drank the water?

Fine.

Come in.

There is wisdom in that kind of hospitality.

A city becomes more lovable when locals enjoy including outsiders in its rituals.

Not testing them.

Including them.

Try this.

Go there.

You need to see this.

Next time call me.

These are small invitations into a place.

Travel is richer because of them.

The business context makes this even more interesting.

Professional relationships often begin formally.

Company names.

Roles.

Technical requirements.

Schedules.

Then somebody says:

You have to try our water.

The abstract organization becomes local and human.

There is a person who is proud of where they live.

A bottle appears.

Someone makes a joke.

For a moment, nobody is vendor or customer or engineer.

They are hosts and guests.

Work creates the meeting.

Hospitality creates the memory.

This is one reason face-to-face work can produce connections that remote communication struggles to replicate.

A video call can solve the technical problem.

It does not naturally give you local water.

It does not put four colleagues on a road.

It does not create accidental lunch conversations.

Remote work has enormous advantages.

But physical travel creates peripheral experiences.

And peripheral experiences often become the part we remember.

The official purpose of our trip may have been important.

The archive preserved the water.

That tells you something about human memory.

We are poor databases.

Excellent storytellers.

A database stores what was designated important.

Memory stores what had texture.

The bottle spills.

People laugh.

The phrase is strange enough to repeat later.

The city becomes associated with it.

Years pass.

A one-line entry survives.

Then, much later, the one-line entry opens an entire corridor back into early working life.

Four friends.

Four days.

Eskişehir.

A joke.

Monthly returns.

I cannot recover every detail.

That is fine.

Personal history is not forensic reconstruction.

It is an honest conversation between surviving evidence and present understanding.

The evidence says we went on a four-day business trip roughly four years before the 2010 entry.

It says there were four of us.

It says locals had us drink Kalabak water and joked that this made us half Eskişehirli.

It says we began going there about once a month afterward.

Everything else should remain interpretation.

What I can say now is what the pattern means to me.

A place often enters our lives sideways.

You do not decide:

This city will matter to me.

You go for work.

You drink the water.

You return.

Then return again.

Years later, the city has a drawer in your memory.

That is how much of life happens.

Not through declarations.

Through repetition.

Friendships grow that way.

Careers grow that way.

Homes become homes that way.

Cities too.

You keep showing up.

One day, without noticing exactly when it happened, the place is no longer foreign.

Maybe you are not half Eskişehirli.

But you know enough to complain about the bottle.
