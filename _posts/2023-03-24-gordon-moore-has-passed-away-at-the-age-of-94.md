Gordon Moore died on March 24, 2023, at the age of 94. His name will remain closely connected with one of the most famous observations in computing: Moore's Law. In 1965, Moore observed that the number of components that could be placed economically on an integrated circuit was increasing rapidly. The exact formulation changed over time, and it was never a physical law. It was an observation and later an industry target.

Still, it described an extraordinary period. Transistors became smaller. Chips became more capable. Computers became cheaper, faster, and more widely available. Moore was also one of the founders of Intel, together with Robert Noyce.

Intel became central to the growth of the semiconductor and personal-computer industries. What interests me about Moore's Law is that a prediction can influence the system it describes. The semiconductor industry organized research, manufacturing, and expectations around continued improvement. For decades, the pace became almost normal. We began to expect next year's computers to be better.

That expectation shaped software too. Programmers could imagine future hardware solving problems that were expensive today. The traditional form of Moore's Law has become harder to sustain as transistor scaling approaches physical and economic limits. But the culture it created remains. We still expect computing to move forward quickly. Gordon Moore helped define that expectation.
