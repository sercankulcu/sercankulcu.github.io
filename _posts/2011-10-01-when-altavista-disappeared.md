Some technologies disappear loudly.

A company announces the end.

People write long farewell articles.

Old users share screenshots.

Someone makes a documentary.

Then there are the technologies that simply stop answering.

One day, years ago, I wrote a very short entry about AltaVista:

It had become history.

You could no longer communicate with it.

That was almost the whole thought.

A dead search engine.

A vanished address.

A small piece of the internet that used to exist and no longer did.

At the time, I wrote it casually.

Years later, it feels like a marker.

Because AltaVista belonged to an internet that felt different from the one that came after.

Not necessarily better.

Definitely not more efficient.

But smaller.

Stranger.

More exploratory.

And perhaps more obviously made of places.

Today, when people talk about the internet, they often mean a handful of platforms.

You open an app.

Search inside it.

Scroll.

Watch.

Message.

Buy.

Leave.

The boundaries are clear.

The interface is polished.

The system wants to keep you inside.

The early web felt less like a collection of platforms and more like a city built by people who had not agreed on urban planning.

Every site looked different.

Navigation could be terrible.

Fonts were unpredictable.

Backgrounds were unforgivable.

Links were blue because nobody had yet decided they should pretend not to be links.

You clicked because you were curious.

Sometimes the next page was useful.

Sometimes it was broken.

Sometimes it had not been updated for four years.

Sometimes it belonged to one person somewhere in the world who had decided to explain one extremely specific subject in extraordinary detail.

Search engines were maps for this chaos.

And there were many maps.

AltaVista.

Yahoo.

Lycos.

Excite.

Ask Jeeves.

Later Google would dominate the mental model so completely that "searching the web" and "Googling" became nearly the same action.

Before that consolidation, search engines had personalities.

You tried another one because maybe it would find something different.

That feels strange now.

The web was the same web.

But the doorway mattered.

A search engine was not just infrastructure.

It was a place you visited.

You knew its name.

Its homepage.

Its logo.

Its behavior.

Its strengths.

Its annoying habits.

AltaVista was one of those places.

Then one day it was not.

This is one of the strange things about digital memory.

Physical places usually leave ruins.

A closed cinema may still have a building.

An old school may still stand.

A bar may become another business.

A house may remain even after everyone who made it meaningful has left.

Digital places can disappear more completely.

The domain changes.

The service shuts down.

The data is removed.

A company redirects the address.

Suddenly there is nowhere to return.

You remember the interface.

You remember using it.

But the place itself is gone.

That produces a peculiar kind of nostalgia.

There is no train you can take back.

No street to walk.

No door to stand in front of.

Only memory.

This is why old screenshots can feel surprisingly emotional.

They look ridiculous.

Low resolution.

Gray buttons.

Tiny icons.

Dense text.

Primitive layouts.

And yet a screenshot can contain an entire period of life.

Not because the design was beautiful.

Because that was where the world was.

There is a difference between remembering technology and remembering life through technology.

Nobody misses a search box simply because it was a search box.

You miss the period when you used it.

The room.

The computer.

The connection speed.

The things you were trying to find.

The person you were when finding information online still felt slightly magical.

That magic is difficult to explain now.

Search is ordinary.

Of course you can find something.

Of course information appears immediately.

Of course there are millions of results.

The surprise today comes when something cannot be found.

Earlier, the surprise was often the opposite.

You typed a phrase.

Waited.

And somewhere out there, a page existed.

Someone had written about exactly the obscure thing you wanted to know.

That discovery created a different relationship with the web.

The internet felt large because you could get lost in it.

Modern systems are much better at preventing that.

They recommend.

Rank.

Filter.

Personalize.

Guide.

Predict.

This is useful.

It is also a different experience.

The old web often made you responsible for your own wandering.

You followed a link because the title sounded interesting.

Then another.

Then another.

Eventually you forgot the original question.

An hour later you knew something completely unrelated.

Not productive.

Often wonderful.

Search engines were the beginning of those journeys.

AltaVista belonged to a time when search itself still felt like a technical marvel.

There is another reason old internet services matter.

They remind us that dominance is temporary.

At any moment, a technology can feel permanent.

Too large to disappear.

Too established.

Everyone uses it.

Everyone knows it.

There is no reason to imagine the web without it.

Then time continues.

A better competitor appears.

User habits change.

A company makes a bad decision.

Another company buys it.

The technology survives in name for a while.

Then the name survives only in memory.

This happens constantly.

Engineers know this intellectually.

Users rarely feel it while the system is alive.

We build around the present as if the present were infrastructure.

It is not.

It is a version.

That is a useful lesson beyond nostalgia.

Technologies should be treated with less reverence.

Today’s unavoidable platform may become tomorrow’s historical footnote.

Today’s standard tool may be something students laugh at fifteen years from now.

The idea that "everyone uses this" is one of the weakest arguments in technology, because "everyone" changes surprisingly fast.

AltaVista also reminds me how often the internet erases its own history.

The web looks permanent because copying is easy.

But services disappear.

Links rot.

Images vanish.

Formats become unreadable.

Accounts are deleted.

Domains expire.

Companies collapse.

Even pages that technically still exist may become unreachable through normal navigation.

Digital information can be duplicated infinitely.

Digital history can still be fragile.

This contradiction matters.

We often assume that if something was online, it will always be online.

Not true.

Preservation requires effort.

Archives.

Backups.

Mirrors.

Exports.

People deciding that something is worth keeping.

Without that, the web forgets.

My old Ekşi Sözlük archive itself is proof of this.

A short line I wrote in 2011 survives because it was exported and kept.

Otherwise, I might remember that I once used AltaVista.

I probably would not remember the exact moment I noticed it had become unreachable.

A tiny personal record preserved a tiny technological transition.

That is why mundane archives become valuable with time.

At the moment of writing, the entry was almost nothing.

AltaVista is gone.

History.

You cannot reach it anymore.

Years later, that sentence contains context I did not know I was saving.

The old internet was disappearing piece by piece.

I was noticing one piece.

We rarely recognize endings when they happen.

Usually there is no final ceremonial use.

You do not say:

This is the last time I will search with this engine.

This is the last time I will use this forum.

This is the last time I will log into this chat client.

This is the last time this website will still look like this.

You simply stop.

Then one day you try to return.

And discover that the place is gone.

That may be why technology nostalgia can be sharper than expected.

There was no goodbye.

Physical relationships often have endings.

Digital relationships often have redirects.

The address still exists.

But it sends you somewhere else.

That is almost crueler.

You arrive at the old door.

Someone has replaced the building.

The internet is full of these invisible demolitions.

Old communities.

Old services.

Old personal pages.

Old search engines.

Old versions of ourselves.

AltaVista became one of them.

I do not think we should romanticize everything old.

Search today is faster.

More capable.

The web is more accessible.

Browsers are better.

Security is better in many ways.

Standards are better.

Mobile access changed everything.

Modern tools solve problems earlier systems barely understood.

Progress is real.

But progress removes environments along the way.

We gain capability and lose texture.

The old web was messy because nobody had fully standardized the experience yet.

That mess produced frustration.

It also produced individuality.

Sites looked like their owners.

Search engines felt distinct.

Software had visible edges.

You knew which program you were using because it had opinions.

Today many interfaces converge toward the same clean language.

Better usability.

Less personality.

That is not necessarily bad.

It is simply another trade.

When I wrote that AltaVista had become history, I was probably making a small joke.

The service was dead.

The sentence treated it almost like a person who could no longer be contacted.

I still like that framing.

Because we did have relationships with these technologies.

Not emotional relationships in the dramatic sense.

Habit relationships.

Daily-use relationships.

They occupied places in our routines.

Then they vanished.

And sometimes the most accurate obituary for a technology is very short:

We used to go there.

Now we cannot.

That is enough to make it history.
