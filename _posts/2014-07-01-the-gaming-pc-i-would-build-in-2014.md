If I were building a gaming PC on **July 1, 2014**, this is the machine I would actually want. I am not simply choosing the most expensive part in every category. The priority is gaming performance, then reliability, cooling and enough headroom to keep the system useful for years.

### The context

Mid-2014 is a quiet moment in GPU development. NVIDIA’s Kepler architecture has been the dominant performance GPU since the GTX 680 in 2012, and the GTX 780 Ti — launched November 2013 — represents its peak. AMD’s R9 290X (launched October 2013) is a credible competitor; it matches or beats the 780 Ti in several benchmarks and costs less, but runs significantly hotter (AMD specifies up to 95°C junction temperature) and louder under sustained load.

Intel’s Devil’s Canyon (4790K) launched in June 2014, providing a meaningful clock increase over the 4770K at the same price. NVIDIA’s Maxwell architecture is coming — the GTX 750 Ti (Maxwell) launched in February 2014 demonstrated dramatically better efficiency — but no high-end Maxwell card exists as of July 1. The Maxwell GTX 980 will not arrive until September 2014.

Popular games that are driving GPU demands this year: Crysis 3, Battlefield 4, Watch Dogs (just released May 2014), The Witcher 2 continues as a benchmark reference. 1440p gaming via Korean IPS monitors is an established enthusiast choice.

### The build

- **CPU:** Intel Core i7-4790K
- **GPU:** NVIDIA GeForce GTX 780 Ti 3 GB
- **Motherboard:** ASUS Maximus VII Hero (Z97)
- **Memory:** 16 GB DDR3-2133
- **Storage:** Samsung 840 EVO 500 GB SSD + 2 TB WD Black
- **Power supply:** Seasonic X-750 750 W
- **Case:** Fractal Design Define R4
- **CPU cooling:** Noctua NH-D15
- **Gaming target:** 1440p ultra settings

### Why this CPU?

The i7-4790K is Devil’s Canyon — a refreshed Haswell with improved TIM between the die and IHS, allowing sustained higher clocks. Its 4.0 GHz base (4.4 GHz turbo) is the fastest Intel mainstream clock available in July 2014. The Intel X99 platform with Haswell-E offers more cores but at significantly higher cost and with no gaming advantage over the 4790K.

### Why this GPU?

The GTX 780 Ti launched at $699 in November 2013. On July 1, 2014, it remains the fastest single-GPU gaming card available. The AMD R9 290X is a serious alternative — cheaper and competitive in performance — but I choose the 780 Ti for quieter sustained operation. A build centered on the Define R4 case and NH-D15 cooler aims for quiet operation; the 290X’s thermal management works against that goal.

### What I would pay

A complete build would be roughly **$1,900–2,150** in the U.S. market of the period. This is an approximate enthusiast-system total, not a claim that every retailer sold every part at MSRP on July 1.

### The weak point

Three gigabytes of VRAM is the obvious constraint. At 1440p ultra settings in demanding titles of 2014 — particularly those using high-resolution texture packs — 3 GB gets used. The GTX 780 Ti is otherwise a very capable card, but the VRAM budget will age faster than the compute performance.

### My verdict

This is a late-Kepler build: very fast, mature, and well-understood. I would build it knowing Maxwell is coming later this year. Waiting for an unannounced product in a different price bracket while sitting on older hardware is a poor trade. The 780 Ti is excellent and available today.

The date matters. I am judging only hardware that was available by July 1, not products announced or released later in the year. That makes each build a snapshot of what I could realistically have assembled at that moment.
