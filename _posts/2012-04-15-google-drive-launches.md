Google launched Drive on April 24, 2012, offering 5 GB of free storage with integration into Google Docs, Sheets, and Slides. The service entered a cloud-storage market that Dropbox had pioneered in 2008 — Dropbox had approximately 50 million users by 2012 and offered 2 GB free — but Drive's advantage was converting Google's existing Docs user base and tying storage directly to a Google account that many people already owned for Gmail.

Synchronization across multiple computers requires tracking file changes, handling concurrent edits, and resolving conflicts when offline edits are later uploaded. Drive handled ordinary files through a block-level sync algorithm while treating Google Docs, Sheets, and Slides differently: those formats existed only as metadata pointers to server-side documents, meaning the "file" on disk was a shortcut that opened a browser. This architectural split between binary files and native Google formats complicated third-party integrations and desktop clients for years.

Microsoft launched OneDrive (then SkyDrive) in February 2012 — two months before Drive — with 7 GB free, and later raised it to 15 GB before eventually cutting free storage to 5 GB in 2014. Apple's iCloud Drive arrived with iOS 8 in September 2014. By 2012, the broader pattern was clear: cloud storage was becoming bundled infrastructure offered free at small scale to lock users into a vendor's application ecosystem, with paid tiers for the storage-heavy users who depended most on the service.
