In July 2012, I wrote a tiny entry about what came to mind when I thought of Eskişehir.

Not the river.

Not the university.

Not a landmark.

Not a museum.

Not one of the things a visitor is supposed to say.

My list was much smaller.

Gürgenci Hotel.

The liquor shop across from the hotel.

The soup place next to the liquor shop.

The pastry shop next to the soup place.

And inside that pastry shop, a sweet known locally by a rather indecorous nickname: ballı.

That was my Eskişehir.

A row of places.

One beside another.

A hotel.

A shop.

A soup place.

A pastry shop.

A dessert.

A complete geography built from adjacency.

This is one of the things I love about memory.

Cities are enormous.

Memory reduces them to routes.

You may spend days, months, or years in a place and later remember only a handful of coordinates.

Not necessarily the important ones.

The repeated ones.

The useful ones.

The ones near where you slept.

Where you ate.

Where you bought something late.

Where someone said, "Let's go there."

A city map tells you distance.

Memory tells you sequence.

Hotel.

Across the street.

Next door.

Next door again.

Dessert.

That sequence contains movement.

You can almost walk it.

This is how personal geography works.

We do not remember cities evenly.

We remember corridors.

The route from home to work.

The street between campus and the bus stop.

The block around a hotel.

The road from a bar to where we ate afterward.

Certain paths become disproportionately dense with memory because we repeat them.

Everything outside the path remains vague.

A visitor may ask:

What do you remember about Eskişehir?

The answer could reasonably be:

These four neighboring places.

That is not a failure of tourism.

It is evidence that the city became lived rather than merely seen.

Tourism teaches landmarks.

Routine teaches corners.

Landmarks are designed to be remembered.

Corners earn memory accidentally.

That distinction matters.

A monumental building asks for attention.

A soup shop near your hotel does not.

You remember it because life repeatedly passed through it.

That is a more private form of significance.

The archive does not explain why Gürgenci Hotel mattered.

It does not say how often I stayed there.

It does not say who I was with.

It does not tell me what I ordered from the soup place, what I bought from the shop, or how many times I ate the dessert.

Those details are absent.

I should leave them absent.

What survives is enough to show a pattern:

When I thought of Eskişehir, I thought of a very specific cluster.

That cluster had become the city in miniature.

We do this constantly.

Ask someone about a university and they may remember one corridor.

Ask about military service and they may remember the canteen.

Ask about a first job and they may remember the coffee machine.

Ask about a foreign country and they may remember the convenience store below the hotel.

Human memory is anti-monumental.

It often prefers the place where ordinary needs were met.

Food.

Sleep.

Waiting.

Conversation.

The official hierarchy of places means very little to it.

A famous building may be visited once.

The cheap soup place may be visited ten times.

Repetition wins.

This is why returning to a city after many years can feel disorienting even when the landmarks remain.

The city may still be there.

Your city may not.

The hotel has changed name.

The shop is gone.

The soup place became something else.

The pastry shop closed.

The block still exists geographically, but the emotional route has been broken.

Urban change is experienced through losses too small for newspapers.

A major building being demolished becomes public history.

The place where you used to eat disappearing becomes private history.

Both alter the city.

Only one gets documented reliably.

This is why old entries like this are useful.

A sentence that once looked trivial becomes a record of lived urban space.

It says:

These places were next to one another.

They mattered enough to be recalled together.

They formed a route.

That is a small historical document.

Not authoritative.

Not comprehensive.

But real.

The final item in the chain was the dessert.

The source calls it ballı and notes a colloquial nickname that is much more colorful.

That detail gives the entry its local texture.

Every city has foods with unofficial names.

Names that would not appear on a polished tourist brochure.

Names people say because people have been saying them.

Sometimes rude.

Sometimes absurd.

Sometimes impossible to translate without losing the joke.

These names matter because they distinguish lived culture from presented culture.

Cities have an official vocabulary.

Then they have the vocabulary residents actually use.

You learn the second by being around people.

This creates another kind of belonging.

When you know the unofficial name, you know slightly more than the menu.

You know how people talk about the thing.

Language becomes part of the food.

It is difficult to separate taste from naming.

A dish with a strange local nickname is easier to remember than a generic pastry.

The name gives it a story before you even eat it.

Then the story travels.

Someone introduces it.

You laugh.

You remember.

Years later, the nickname may survive more strongly than the flavor.

This is how local culture reproduces itself.

Not through official instruction.

Through small acts of transmission.

Have you tried this?

Do you know what people call it?

Come, I'll show you.

The old Eskişehir entry is built entirely from that kind of local knowledge.

It does not say:

Eskişehir is known for X.

It says:

When I think of Eskişehir, I think of this exact little chain.

That difference is important.

One is encyclopedia knowledge.

The other is autobiography.

A city can have both identities at once.

There is public Eskişehir.

And there is the Eskişehir belonging to each person who passed through it.

One person remembers a university.

Another remembers a neighborhood.

Another remembers a relationship.

Another remembers work trips.

Another remembers a hotel and three neighboring businesses.

None replaces the city.

Together, they show why a city cannot be reduced to a guidebook.

Guidebooks optimize for shared significance.

Memory optimizes for personal recurrence.

The strongest example may be hotels.

A hotel is temporary by definition.

You are not from there.

You are passing through.

Yet repeated stays can make a hotel oddly domestic.

You begin to know where things are.

Which direction to walk.

What is open nearby.

Where to eat.

Which shop is convenient.

Temporary residence develops routine surprisingly fast.

This may be one reason work travel creates unusual attachments to cities.

You are neither tourist nor resident.

You know some parts very well and almost nothing else.

Your geography is functional.

Airport.

Hotel.

Workplace.

Restaurant.

Shop.

Repeat.

You can spend considerable time in a city and still carry an absurdly narrow map.

That narrow map can become very intimate.

You may know exactly where to find soup at night while being unable to name a famous square.

This is not ignorance so much as route-based knowledge.

The city was experienced through purpose.

Work trips especially create this effect.

Time is constrained.

Your movements organize around obligations.

Then the small freedoms become memorable.

Breakfast.

Dinner.

A walk.

The shop downstairs.

A dessert.

The moments outside the formal purpose gain extra weight because they belong to you.

The work may be why you traveled.

The soup place may be what you remember.

That pattern appears elsewhere in my archive too.

A business trip to Eskişehir became associated with Kalabak water and a joke about becoming half Eskişehirli.

A professional trip to Montreal left memories of cafeterias, flags, buildings, and getting lost.

The formal reason for travel is often the least durable part.

Experience leaks around the assignment.

This is one reason organizations underestimate what travel does to people.

A trip is not only meetings.

It is a temporary life.

You wake somewhere else.

You navigate unfamiliar streets.

You form habits quickly.

You discover the practical ecosystem around where you are staying.

Then you leave.

These temporary lives accumulate.

Later, cities become shelves containing old versions of you.

Eskişehir contains one.

Montreal another.

Konya another.

Ankara contains many.

You cannot return to the exact version because the person returning is different.

Even if the street is unchanged.

That is the difficulty of nostalgia.

We imagine the place stored the old experience for us.

It did not.

The experience existed between place and person.

Change either side and the result changes.

Suppose Gürgenci Hotel still exists exactly as it did.

Suppose the shop is still opposite.

Suppose the soup place and pastry shop are unchanged.

You could walk the same sequence.

Would it feel the same?

Probably not.

The route would now include the knowledge that you were repeating it.

That alone changes the experience.

The first version was life.

The second would be reenactment.

Reenactments can be beautiful.

They are not restoration.

Time is not a location you can navigate back to.

This makes archives valuable because they preserve the unselfconscious version.

The 2012 entry was not trying to create nostalgia.

It was just answering a title.

What comes to mind when you think of Eskişehir?

These places.

Simple.

That simplicity is precisely why it feels trustworthy.

Later reflection is contaminated by knowing something has become the past.

The original sentence was written while the associations were still ordinary enough to be casually listed.

There is no grand emotion.

No elegy.

No "those were the days."

Just memory in present tense.

That is a rare thing to recover.

I also like the architecture of the list.

Each place is defined through the previous one.

The shop across from the hotel.

The soup place next to the shop.

The pastry shop next to the soup place.

The dessert inside the pastry shop.

It zooms inward.

City.

Hotel.

Street relationship.

Shop.

Neighboring shop.

Interior.

Food.

The world narrows until it reaches one sweet.

Memory often works like that.

A large concept triggers a smaller one.

Eskişehir.

Hotel.

Street.

Soup.

Pastry.

Dessert.

Then perhaps a face.

A conversation.

An evening.

Associative memory is not arranged alphabetically.

It travels through contact.

One thing touches another.

This may be why physical adjacency becomes so powerful in memory.

Places beside one another become part of the same mental unit.

If you always visit them in sequence, the brain stores the route as one experience.

The same happens in homes.

The kitchen table and a particular chair.

The hallway and a mirror.

The balcony and a view.

We remember environments as linked actions.

Walk here.

Turn.

Open.

Sit.

Order.

Eat.

Cities are learned by the body before they are learned conceptually.

Your feet know routes your mind cannot explain.

You emerge from a station and automatically turn the right way.

Years later, return and the body may still attempt the old movement.

Muscle memory is urban memory too.

The entry also reminds me that "what comes to mind" is a better question than "what is important."

Importance invites performance.

You start giving the correct answer.

What comes to mind allows the brain to reveal its own hierarchy.

Ask:

What is important about Eskişehir?

You may discuss history, culture, universities, urban development.

Ask:

What comes to mind?

Hotel.

Liquor shop.

Soup.

Pastry.

Dessert.

Much better.

Personal writing improves when it follows that second question.

What do I actually remember?

Not what should I remember?

This is the difference between essay and testimony.

Both have value.

But testimony begins with specificity.

The strange object.

The ordinary shop.

The sentence someone said.

The route.

The food.

The embarrassment.

These details resist generic writing.

Anyone can write that travel broadens the mind.

Only one person will write that Eskişehir means a hotel, the shop opposite it, the soup place beside that, the pastry shop beside that, and one locally nicknamed dessert inside.

Specificity is where ownership enters the story.

This matters even when the story is tiny.

Especially then.

Big events provide their own structure.

Small memories survive only through detail.

The detail does not have to be spectacular.

It has to be true.

That is one of the lessons I keep finding in the archive.

The strongest entries are not always the most serious.

They are the ones containing one stubbornly specific thing.

An eraser on a string around a child's neck.

A minibus that disrupted a hiking trip.

A bar inside an office building.

An older man selling meatballs near a metro exit.

A line of shops around a hotel in Eskişehir.

These details can hold an era because they were not invented for symbolism.

Symbolism arrives later.

At the time, the thing was just there.

That is good material.

I do not know which of those Eskişehir places still exist today.

For this story, I do not need to know.

The entry is about 2012 memory, not a current city guide.

Trying to update it would change the subject.

The relevant city is the one preserved in the archive.

That version has its own map.

Gürgenci Hotel.

Across the street, the shop.

Next to it, soup.

Next to that, pastry.

Inside, ballı.

If someone asked for directions to my Eskişehir in 2012, apparently that would have been enough.

And perhaps that is what belonging to a city often means.

Not knowing all of it.

Knowing one little part so well that the whole city can be summoned from it.
