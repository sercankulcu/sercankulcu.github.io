Sometimes a person's relationship with work reveals itself at lunch.

Not in meetings.

Not in presentations.

Not in performance reviews.

Lunch.

Years ago, after about a month of barely speaking with an intern at work, I asked a very ordinary question:

"Shall we go eat?"

She agreed.

Then I suggested the cafeteria.

Her reaction was immediate.

The cafeteria?

The tray meals?

Like workers?

Apparently this was unacceptable.

I remember answering with the kind of irritation that does not need preparation:

What exactly happens when you eat outside every day?

Does it transform you into something else?

That was the whole argument.

Nothing dramatic.

Two people deciding where to have lunch.

But underneath it was a much larger question.

What does an engineer think he has become?

University can quietly prepare you for status.

You study for years.

You survive difficult courses.

You learn things most people around you do not know.

Eventually somebody gives you a title.

Engineer.

It sounds important.

It is important.

The work can carry real responsibility.

But a title can also begin doing strange things inside the head.

You stop seeing it as a description of your job and start seeing it as a rank.

Then ordinary things begin looking beneath you.

A cafeteria tray.

A bus.

A cheap restaurant.

A certain neighborhood.

Certain clothes.

Certain people.

You begin protecting the title from contamination.

I never understood that instinct very well.

Maybe because engineering, at least the version I respected, always felt closer to work than status.

You build.

Fix.

Test.

Measure.

Fail.

Try again.

You sit with technicians.

You talk to operators.

You ask someone who has been doing the physical job for twenty years why the design is not behaving the way the drawing says it should.

If you are lucky, you learn quickly that intelligence is not distributed according to job titles.

The person with the degree may know the theory.

The person on the floor may know exactly which bolt fails first.

Both forms of knowledge matter.

That makes contempt for "worker things" especially ridiculous in engineering.

Much of engineering exists because somebody, somewhere, will eventually have to build, operate, repair, carry, install, clean, or maintain what you designed.

The clean diagram ends in somebody's hands.

Maybe that is why the phrase came naturally to me:

An engineer who has not lost the worker inside him.

I liked that description.

Still do.

It does not mean pretending that every job is identical.

They are not.

Responsibilities differ.

Training differs.

Pay differs.

Authority differs.

But dignity should not depend on where someone eats lunch.

There is a social instinct, especially early in a career, to signal that you have moved upward.

You are no longer a student.

No longer ordinary.

No longer eating wherever everybody else eats.

Maybe the intern was simply joking.

Maybe she genuinely disliked cafeteria food.

The old entry is too short to support a grand diagnosis.

What I remember from it is my own reaction.

The phrase "like workers" bothered me.

Because what exactly was wrong with eating like workers?

We worked too.

We were not aristocrats who happened to know C++.

A company cafeteria is not a moral test.

It is lunch.

There is also something financially absurd about turning every workday into a status performance.

Eating outside every day costs more.

Usually takes longer.

Often creates another daily decision.

Where are we going?

What are we eating?

Who is coming?

How long will it take?

The cafeteria solves the problem with brutal efficiency.

Take tray.

Take food.

Sit.

Eat.

Return to work.

There is an engineer's beauty in that.

Low overhead.

Predictable process.

Good enough output.

No unnecessary optimization.

Of course, food is not only fuel.

Going outside with colleagues can be enjoyable.

Some of the best conversations at work happen away from the office.

A lunch can rescue a bad day.

A restaurant can become part of a team's culture.

Nothing is wrong with that.

The interesting part begins when the choice becomes hierarchy.

When one option is considered embarrassing because of who else uses it.

That is when lunch stops being lunch.

It becomes identity.

Workplaces produce these little hierarchies everywhere.

Who sits where.

Who parks where.

Who has an office.

Who travels business class.

Who eats with whom.

Who enters which door.

Some distinctions have practical reasons.

Others exist mostly because people enjoy knowing where everybody stands.

Status is a language.

The dangerous part is that once you learn it, you can spend enormous energy speaking it.

A better phone.

A better car.

A better restaurant.

A better title.

A better desk.

Eventually you are not enjoying any of these things.

You are using them as evidence.

Evidence that you have moved up.

That can become exhausting because there is always another level.

Someone always has a better car.

A higher floor.

A larger office.

A more expensive lunch.

If self-respect depends on winning that comparison, lunch becomes the least of your problems.

The engineers I admired most were usually not the ones who performed the title hardest.

They could move between worlds.

Talk theory in a meeting.

Then stand beside a machine.

Explain something to a manager.

Then ask a technician for help without acting as if the question diminished them.

They knew what they knew.

They also knew what they did not.

That kind of confidence is quieter.

It does not need a restaurant receipt.

Perhaps this is one reason early-career memories remain useful.

You can see values before you had words for them.

I did not write an essay that day about class signaling, professional identity, or organizational hierarchy.

I wrote something much shorter.

She did not want to eat tray food "like workers."

I asked what was so special about eating outside every day.

Then I described myself:

An engineer who had not lost the worker spirit inside him.

That was enough.

There is a piece of personal philosophy hiding in many jokes.

This was one of mine.

Work should not make you ashamed of work.

Education should expand the number of people you can understand, not reduce it.

A professional title should give you responsibility before it gives you pride.

And if your sense of status cannot survive carrying a cafeteria tray, perhaps the title is doing too much emotional labor.

I do not remember what we ate that day.

I do not even remember whether we actually went to the cafeteria together.

The food was never the memorable part.

The sentence was.

"Like workers?"

Yes.

Exactly.

We were at work.
