The strange thing about credentials is that their value depends on who is looking at them.

In August 2010, I wrote a short entry about graduate education.

At work, I wrote, a master's degree could be emphasized loudly.

Very loudly.

Repeated.

Underlined.

Made visible.

Something people wanted noticed.

Then military service entered the picture.

Suddenly the same degree seemed to receive a completely different response:

A master's degree?

What is that?

Almost as if the institution had never heard of such a thing.

That contrast was the joke.

But it also captured something I was beginning to understand about adult life.

You carry the same biography into different systems.

The systems do not read it the same way.

A university looks at one set of things.

A company looks at another.

A government office looks at another.

The military looks at another.

A bank looks at another.

A stranger at a dinner table may care about something completely different again.

You remain the same person.

Your relevance changes.

This can be surprising when you are young, because education trains you to think in cumulative terms.

You pass an exam.

You earn a grade.

You complete a degree.

You add another qualification.

The stack grows.

The natural assumption is that each addition permanently increases your value everywhere.

Bachelor's degree.

Then master's degree.

Perhaps another certificate.

Another language.

Another skill.

Upward.

Permanent.

But institutions do not score the same game.

A qualification can be central in one environment and almost invisible in another.

This is not necessarily unfair.

Different systems have different needs.

A research laboratory should care about things a military intake process may not.

A software company may care about technical experience that a bureaucracy cannot easily classify.

A university may care deeply about publications that mean very little to a customer who simply wants a broken system fixed.

Context determines the meaning of achievement.

That sounds obvious now.

It did not always feel obvious.

Early in adult life, titles and degrees can become part of identity very quickly.

You work hard for them.

Other people congratulate you.

They appear on forms.

On CVs.

In introductions.

They become shorthand for competence.

Then you enter a system that shrugs.

That can feel almost insulting.

Did all that work suddenly disappear?

No.

The work remains.

The institution simply does not reward it in the way you expected.

This distinction is important.

Recognition and value are not identical.

Something can be valuable without being recognized in a particular setting.

A skill can matter to your life even if it does not change your bureaucratic category.

A degree can shape the way you think even if a form has no box for it.

A language can matter even if your current job never uses it.

A qualification is not worthless because one system ignores it.

It is simply not universally convertible.

That may be one of the hardest lessons for people who invest heavily in education.

We are taught that education opens doors.

It does.

But not every door.

And not always the door you are standing in front of.

Sometimes the person behind the desk has a checklist.

Your accomplishment is not on it.

End of discussion.

Bureaucracies are especially good at this.

They need categories.

Categories allow large systems to operate.

Age.

Status.

Degree.

Rank.

Document.

Code.

Date.

Eligibility.

The system cannot hold the full richness of a person.

It compresses.

Compression is necessary.

It is also absurd when you encounter the edges.

You say:

But I have a master's degree.

The system effectively says:

Interesting.

Next question.

This is not personal.

That is what makes it both less offensive and more frustrating.

You cannot persuade a field in a database.

If the category does not use the information, the information disappears operationally.

Young professionals often experience similar things in companies.

One workplace treats a master's degree as a major differentiator.

Another barely cares.

One hiring manager values academic depth.

Another asks what you have shipped.

One manager cares about certifications.

Another trusts demonstrated skill.

The same CV can look impressive or ordinary depending on the reader.

There is no universal evaluator.

This is why career strategy based only on accumulating credentials can become dangerous.

You can keep adding lines to a CV without asking what those lines are for.

Another degree.

Another certificate.

Another training program.

Another title.

Accumulation feels like progress because it is measurable.

But measurable progress is not always directional progress.

The better question is:

What kind of work do I want to be able to do?

Then:

What evidence, skill, experience, or qualification actually helps me do it?

Sometimes the answer is a degree.

Sometimes practice.

Sometimes mentorship.

Sometimes building something.

Sometimes writing.

Sometimes teaching.

Sometimes simply doing the work for years.

Education is powerful.

But education is not magic.

The old entry was not an argument against graduate school.

It was about status translation.

At work, the master's degree could be shouted about.

In the military context, it could be treated as if nobody had noticed.

Same person.

Different institution.

Different scoreboard.

This happens far beyond education.

Take job titles.

A "manager" in one company may lead twenty people.

In another, nobody.

A "senior" title may represent ten years of experience somewhere and three elsewhere.

A "director" at a small startup and a director at a multinational may inhabit completely different realities.

Titles are local currencies.

So are credentials.

They exchange imperfectly across systems.

Understanding this can make a person less attached to labels.

That is useful.

Not because labels are meaningless.

They affect pay.

Access.

Authority.

Opportunity.

Labels matter.

But they are not the whole substance.

If your identity depends entirely on a label being recognized, every context that does not recognize it becomes threatening.

That is exhausting.

A more stable identity comes from capability.

What can you actually do?

What have you learned?

How do you think?

What problems can you solve?

How do you behave when you do not know the answer?

Can you learn?

Can you communicate?

Can you help others understand?

These travel better than titles.

Not perfectly.

But better.

A degree may help create those capabilities.

It does not automatically prove all of them.

This is another lesson that becomes clearer with time.

Early in education, credentials and learning feel almost identical.

You study.

You pass.

You receive proof.

Later, the two separate.

Some of the most useful things you know may have no certificate.

Some certificates may represent things you barely remember.

Formal education is a structured path.

Learning is larger.

Institutions are good at recognizing structured paths.

Life often rewards usable knowledge.

The gap between those two systems can produce strange moments.

You spend years doing something academically significant.

Then somebody asks a practical question outside your specialty and your degree cannot answer for you.

Or the reverse.

You possess useful practical knowledge that a formal system cannot recognize because you lack the expected paper.

Both situations reveal the same problem:

human capability is richer than institutional classification.

Still, we need classification.

So we live with imperfect proxies.

Degrees.

Grades.

Titles.

Years of experience.

Ranks.

Certificates.

They help strangers estimate one another quickly.

They are useful.

They are dangerous when mistaken for reality.

The younger me seems to have encountered this contrast sharply around military service.

The timing matters.

Military service in Turkey, especially for young men of that generation, could interrupt the normal narrative of career progression.

One day you are in a workplace where your professional identity matters.

The next, you are entering a system organized around entirely different distinctions.

The shift can be abrupt.

Your office habits.

Your education.

Your seniority.

Your professional confidence.

Much of it may become secondary to a new set of rules.

This creates a temporary identity reset.

People who were engineers, teachers, lawyers, managers, graduates, or specialists become categories inside another institution.

That compression can feel absurd.

It can also be socially revealing.

You discover which parts of your identity were supported by context.

Remove the office.

Remove the title.

Remove the usual clothes.

Remove the email signature.

What remains?

This is not a uniquely military question.

Unemployment can ask it.

Retirement can ask it.

Migration can ask it.

Changing careers can ask it.

Moving to another country can ask it.

A person who was highly legible in one system becomes difficult to classify in another.

Credentials may not transfer.

Status may not transfer.

Networks may not transfer.

You are forced to rebuild recognition.

That can be humbling.

Sometimes painful.

But it also separates self-worth from local validation.

At least, ideally.

The old entry captured this with humor rather than philosophy.

At work:

Master's degree!

Look!

Important!

In military processing:

Master's degree?

Never heard of it.

That exaggeration makes the point better than a lecture.

Institutions do not share one brain.

We often behave as though they do.

We assume that if something is important in one system, it should be important in all.

But importance is local.

A publication matters enormously in academia.

A deployment matters enormously in engineering.

A rank matters enormously in the military.

A performance metric matters in sales.

A customer may care about none of these.

They care whether their problem is solved.

This can be liberating.

You do not need to win every scoreboard.

You need to know which game you are playing.

And whether you want to play it.

That second question is more important.

A person can spend years optimizing for a system they do not even like.

More recognition.

More title.

More status.

Then discover that the reward does not travel outside the building.

The best defense is building a life with multiple sources of meaning.

Professional competence.

Relationships.

Curiosity.

Health.

Friendship.

Useful work.

Interests.

Teaching.

Creating.

Whatever matters to you.

Then one institution's indifference does not erase you.

This is easier to say after years of experience.

At the beginning, external recognition matters a lot.

It tells you that you are moving.

You need some of that.

Everyone does.

The problem begins when recognition becomes the only evidence of value.

A degree is evidence of having completed a demanding path.

It is not a universal ranking of the person.

A job title is evidence of an organizational role.

It is not a universal ranking either.

The military's indifference to my master's degree did not make the degree meaningless.

The workplace's enthusiasm for it did not make it everything.

Both reactions were partial.

The degree was the same.

The environments were different.

That is probably the mature version of the old joke.

You should not ask:

How valuable is this qualification?

As if there were one answer.

Ask:

Valuable for what?

To whom?

In which system?

At which moment?

Those questions prevent a lot of confusion.

They also make career decisions more realistic.

Suppose you are considering graduate school.

The useful question is not:

Is a master's degree good?

Of course it can be good.

The useful question is:

What do I want from it?

Deeper knowledge?

Academic career?

Access to a field?

A professional requirement?

Personal interest?

Credential signaling?

A future PhD?

Different goals justify different choices.

Without a goal, the degree risks becoming a symbolic upgrade.

Another line.

Another status marker.

And symbolic upgrades are especially vulnerable to contexts that do not care.

This does not mean every educational choice must have a financial return.

Learning for its own sake is legitimate.

Perhaps more legitimate than many career optimizations.

But then say that honestly.

I am doing this because I want to know.

That is a strong reason.

Stronger, in some ways, than hoping every future institution will reward the paper.

Because some will not.

The old entry also reminds me of something funny about adult institutions.

They frequently overvalue things internally and undervalue them externally.

Inside a company, a particular title feels important.

Leave the company and nobody knows what it means.

Inside a university, a distinction may seem enormous.

Outside, people have never heard of it.

Inside a military unit, rank structures daily life.

Outside, that hierarchy may disappear.

We move through overlapping worlds.

Each has its own vocabulary of importance.

Maturity may be partly learning not to confuse local importance with universal importance.

This applies to social life too.

A person can be famous in a niche community and unknown outside it.

A technical expert can be deeply respected by peers and anonymous publicly.

Someone can have thousands of followers and still be unknown at the next table.

Status is more fragmented than it feels from inside the group.

That is healthy to remember.

It keeps ego proportional.

It also protects confidence.

If nobody in the next room knows your accomplishment, the accomplishment still happened.

Recognition is not retroactive causation.

People do not create your past by noticing it.

You did the work.

That remains.

In 2010, I wrote only one sharp contrast.

Workplace:

make the master's degree visible.

Military service:

what master's degree?

The joke still works.

But what I hear underneath it now is a useful warning:

Do not build your entire sense of self from credentials that belong to someone else's classification system.

Earn them if they matter.

Use them where they help.

Be proud of the work behind them.

Then remember that you are larger than the box that happens to recognize them.

Some places will underline the degree.

Some will ignore it.

You still have to be the same person in both rooms.
