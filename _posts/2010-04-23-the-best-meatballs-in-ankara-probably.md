In April 2010, I wrote a very short recommendation for where to eat meatballs in Ankara.

Not a restaurant.

Not a famous place.

Not somewhere with a menu, reservations, reviews, or carefully designed lighting.

My recommendation was this:

At the exit of Dikimevi metro station in Cebeci, from an older man who seemed to be there roughly between ten at night and one in the morning.

Then I added the important qualification:

Either the meatballs were genuinely very good,

or I had simply always eaten them while drunk.

That was the whole entry.

It still makes me laugh because it contains one of the great problems in food criticism:

context.

We like pretending taste is objective.

This is better.

That is worse.

This place is excellent.

That place is overrated.

But food is rarely consumed in laboratory conditions.

You are tired.

Hungry.

Happy.

Cold.

Lonely.

With friends.

Coming out of somewhere.

Going somewhere else.

Maybe slightly drunk.

Maybe very drunk.

The meal enters a situation that changes it.

A sandwich at 1 p.m. is one thing.

The same sandwich at midnight after several hours out with friends is another.

Technically identical.

Emotionally different food.

This is why late-night street food develops mythology.

It is not only about ingredients.

It appears at exactly the moment people are most ready to love it.

You have been out.

The night is ending.

The body has begun issuing practical demands.

Conversation has become slower.

Someone says they are hungry.

Then you see a familiar stand.

At that moment, the food does not need a philosophy.

It needs to be hot.

Available.

Fast.

And there.

Presence becomes quality.

The older man near Dikimevi metro apparently solved this problem very well.

At least according to my 2010 self.

The source tells me almost nothing else.

I do not know his name.

I do not know how the meatballs were prepared.

I do not know the exact price.

I do not know whether he had a cart, a grill, a small stand, or some other setup.

I should not invent any of that.

What survived is enough.

Location.

Approximate hours.

An older man.

Meatballs.

And uncertainty about whether they were objectively good.

That last part is what turns a recommendation into a memory.

If I had simply written "good meatballs," there would be very little to say now.

But admitting that alcohol may have influenced the evaluation preserves the atmosphere of the nights around the food.

It tells me that the meatballs did not belong to ordinary dinner.

They belonged to a specific urban rhythm.

Cebeci.

Dikimevi.

Late night.

Metro exit.

That is a small piece of Ankara.

Cities are often remembered through food at transportation points.

Outside stations.

Near bus stops.

Beside campuses.

At intersections people pass when the official part of the evening is over.

These places are not destinations when the night begins.

They become destinations later.

That is an important difference.

Nobody necessarily says at eight:

Tonight, we are going to the man outside Dikimevi metro.

The evening produces him.

He appears at the end of a chain of decisions.

Where did we go?

Who came?

How late did we stay?

Which route are we taking home?

Are we hungry?

The food waits at the intersection of nightlife and transportation.

It belongs to the return journey.

This makes it especially memorable.

The trip home is one of the least celebrated parts of going out.

Stories focus on where you went.

The concert.

The bar.

The party.

The dinner.

But many of the best details happen after the official event.

Walking toward transport.

Waiting for a bus.

Buying something cheap to eat.

Talking more freely because the main evening is finished.

The night has no remaining objective.

That changes conversation.

People linger.

One more cigarette.

One more story.

One more piece of food.

Then everyone separates.

The late-night meal becomes a small closing ceremony.

I suspect that is why food eaten at those hours can become disproportionately important in memory.

It marks transition.

The social night is ending.

Ordinary life is returning.

Tomorrow exists again.

But not yet.

First:

meatballs.

There is also something democratic about this kind of food memory.

Expensive restaurants create memories through rarity.

Street food creates them through repetition.

You may return many times.

The same person.

Same corner.

Same approximate hour.

The food becomes part of routine.

Routine creates belonging.

This is how cities become personal.

Not through the places everyone is told to visit.

Through the places you repeatedly need.

The café where you always waited.

The market where you bought something on the way home.

The man who sold food near the metro.

The shop that stayed open later than the others.

Official city identity is made of monuments.

Personal city identity is made of dependable small places.

Those places may disappear.

Then the city changes more than maps can show.

A business closes.

A vendor stops appearing.

A building is renovated.

The road changes.

A familiar route remains geographically possible but emotionally different.

This is why tiny archive entries matter.

They preserve urban infrastructure that was never important enough for history.

A late-night meatball seller may leave almost no institutional record.

For the people who ate there, he can still represent an entire period.

This is one of the inequalities of memory.

Famous places are documented endlessly.

Ordinary places survive only if ordinary people mention them.

A photograph.

A forum post.

A sentence in an old blog.

An entry like mine.

Without those fragments, everyday cities vanish.

We know where governments were.

Where major buildings stood.

Where important events occurred.

But where did people eat after midnight in 2010?

What did students consider cheap?

Where did friends meet?

Which corner mattered?

These are harder questions.

Yet they describe lived history.

The old entry also says something about recommendation culture before everything became fully quantified.

Today, a food recommendation often comes with numbers.

4.7 stars.

1,842 reviews.

Price level.

Photographs.

Opening hours.

Ranking.

Delivery score.

In 2010, a useful recommendation could simply be:

Go to the Dikimevi metro exit between ten and one. There is an older man there. Eat the meatballs.

That was enough.

The recommendation traveled socially.

Trust me.

I ate there.

Of course, my own recommendation immediately undermined itself.

Maybe it was just the alcohol.

This actually makes me trust it more.

Certainty is suspicious in food writing.

Taste is too contextual for absolute claims.

One person's perfect meal is another person's disappointment.

The same person's perfect meal on Friday may be ordinary on Tuesday.

Mood changes perception.

Expectation changes perception.

Price changes perception.

Company changes perception.

Hunger changes perception dramatically.

Hunger may be the greatest chef in history.

A person who has eaten recently can be extremely sophisticated.

A hungry person becomes philosophical about bread.

This matters because memory often preserves peak experiences created partly by deprivation.

Food after a long hike.

Water after heat.

Soup when sick.

Breakfast after little sleep.

Meatballs late at night.

The pleasure is real.

The source of the pleasure is distributed across the whole situation.

This does not reduce the food.

It explains it.

Maybe the meatballs really were excellent.

The fact that I cannot isolate the variable does not invalidate the memory.

Human experience is not designed for isolation.

We live multivariable lives.

That is one reason revisiting old favorite places can be risky.

You think you are returning for the food.

But the original recipe included things the kitchen cannot reproduce.

Your age.

Your friends.

Your financial situation.

Your expectations.

Your route home.

The music you had just heard.

The person you were trying to impress.

The fact that tomorrow seemed far away.

You can order the same meal.

You cannot order the same context.

This is why nostalgia sometimes produces disappointing restaurant visits.

The place did not necessarily get worse.

You are asking it to cook time.

No kitchen can do that.

If I went today to the exact spot where that older man stood, even if by some miracle the same meatballs appeared, I would not be able to test my old claim properly.

The experiment is impossible.

The 2010 version of me was part of the apparatus.

Remove him and you have changed the conditions.

This is why I like the uncertainty in the old entry.

It does not try to solve the question.

Were they actually that good?

Maybe.

Was I usually drunk when I ate them?

Apparently enough for me to question the data.

Both can be true.

A good late-night food vendor benefits from customers arriving with highly favorable experimental conditions.

That is not cheating.

That is good location strategy.

Restaurants talk about ambiance.

Street food has context.

The city provides the ambiance for free.

Passing cars.

Cold air.

People leaving the metro.

Friends standing around.

The awareness that you should probably go home.

No interior designer can reproduce this exactly.

The food is integrated into the city instead of separated from it.

That gives street food a different kind of authenticity.

Not "authentic" in the marketing sense.

Functional authenticity.

It exists because people need it there.

At that hour.

In that place.

The business model and the social environment fit each other.

That fit creates character.

A luxury restaurant can be placed in many cities and still operate.

A late-night seller beside one metro exit belongs specifically to a route.

Move him elsewhere and the meaning changes.

This is one reason informal urban places matter.

They adapt closely to human flow.

They know when people appear.

What they want.

How much time they have.

What they can pay.

They form around behavior.

Urban planners design cities from above.

Street vendors often understand them from below.

They learn the real map.

Where people slow down.

Where they wait.

Where hunger appears.

Where nightlife empties into transport.

Where workers pass.

Where students gather.

This is practical urban intelligence.

My entry did not analyze any of that.

I was just recommending meatballs.

But looking back, the location is not accidental.

Dikimevi metro exit.

Late at night.

That tells a complete small story about movement.

The city carries people toward the seller.

Then carries them away.

I also like that the recommendation centered on "the older man," not a business name.

Informal places are often person-based.

You do not go to a brand.

You go to the man who sells the thing.

The woman who makes the sandwiches.

The uncle with the tea.

The person becomes the address.

This creates a kind of human-scale commerce that branded environments rarely reproduce.

It can also be fragile.

If that person stops, the place may stop.

There is no franchise continuity.

No corporate identity.

No succession plan.

Just a person and the people who know where to find them.

That fragility makes these memories especially worth preserving.

There is another angle here that younger me probably would not have cared about.

The person selling food between roughly ten at night and one in the morning was working while the rest of us were ending our evening.

Nightlife for one person is labor for another.

Bars.

Taxis.

Food stalls.

Security.

Cleaning.

Transport.

The night economy depends on people whose work is largely invisible to those consuming the night.

You buy the food and continue home.

The seller remains.

This does not need to turn a light memory into a heavy social lesson.

But it is worth noticing.

Pleasure has infrastructure.

Someone keeps the city functioning after ordinary hours.

Someone cooks.

Drives.

Cleans.

Waits.

Watches.

That work allows everyone else to experience the city as spontaneous.

Good infrastructure disappears into convenience.

The late-night meatballs seemed simply to be there.

Of course they were there because someone chose to be there.

That distinction grows more visible with age.

When younger, services feel like properties of the world.

The metro runs.

Food exists.

Shops open.

Someone fixes things.

Later, you see the labor network.

People.

Shifts.

Costs.

Tradeoffs.

The city becomes less magical and more impressive.

Maybe that is maturity:

understanding the machinery without losing affection for the result.

Still, I do not want to over-intellectualize a meatball.

That would be disrespectful to the original entry.

The original spirit is simple.

If you are in Ankara, around Cebeci, near Dikimevi metro, late enough at night, there was an older man selling meatballs.

I thought they were excellent.

I was not entirely confident in the reliability of the reviewer.

That is the story.

The uncertainty is part of the charm.

Some memories should remain unresolved.

We do not need to determine whether the food was objectively great.

There is no objective tribunal waiting.

What matters is that it tasted great then.

That is enough.

Taste exists in time.

So does happiness.

If the moment worked, it worked.

You do not need to audit it twenty years later.

The younger version of me had already done enough quality control by adding the disclaimer.

Maybe the meatballs were fantastic.

Maybe I was drunk.

Probably both.

Either way, I kept going back.

And for a late-night food recommendation, repeat business is a better review than stars.
