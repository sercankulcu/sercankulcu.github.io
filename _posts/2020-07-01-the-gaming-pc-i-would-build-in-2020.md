If I were building a gaming PC on **July 1, 2020**, this is the machine I would actually want. I am not simply choosing the most expensive part in every category. The priority is gaming performance, then reliability, cooling and enough headroom to keep the system useful for years.

### The build

- **CPU:** Intel Core i9-10900K
- **GPU:** NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti 11 GB
- **Motherboard:** ASUS ROG Maximus XII Hero (Z490)
- **Memory:** 32 GB DDR4-3600
- **Storage:** Samsung 970 EVO Plus 1 TB + Crucial MX500 2 TB
- **Power supply:** Corsair RM850x
- **Case:** Fractal Design Meshify S2
- **CPU cooling:** Arctic Liquid Freezer II 360
- **Gaming target:** 4K/60 and 1440p high refresh

### Why this CPU?

The ten-core 10900K was Intel’s Q2 2020 enthusiast gaming part, with boost clocks up to 5.3 GHz and a recommended tray price around $537. For a gaming machine, its frequency advantage mattered more to me than chasing workstation core counts.

### Why this GPU?

RTX 30-series had not yet been announced on July 1. That makes the 2080 Ti old but still the fastest conventional gaming choice I could actually buy that day.

### What I would pay

A complete build would be roughly **$2,700–3,000** in the U.S. market of the period. This is an approximate enthusiast-system total, not a claim that every retailer sold every part at MSRP on July 1.

### The weak point

This is the year most likely to produce buyer’s remorse: Ampere is only months away. I would build only if I need the PC now, and I would keep the GPU resale box.

### My verdict

The date rule matters. A July 2020 dream PC is not the machine we would recommend in September. Historical builds should respect what a buyer actually knew and could buy.

The date matters. I am judging only hardware that was available by July 1, not products announced or released later in the year. That makes each build a snapshot of what I could realistically have assembled at that moment.
