Google announced the first commercial Chromebooks at Google I/O on May 11, 2011, with two models going on sale June 15: the Samsung Series 5 Chromebook ($499 Wi-Fi / $549 3G) and the Acer Chromebook AC700 ($349). Both ran Chrome OS, a Linux-kernel operating system built around the Chrome browser as the exclusive application runtime, with no support for installing native Windows or Mac software. User accounts, bookmarks, apps, and settings synchronized to Google's servers, meaning any Chromebook could be logged into and return the same environment within seconds.

Chrome OS's verified boot used a hardware TPM chip and cryptographic signatures to check the bootloader and OS image at each startup. If any component was modified or corrupted, the device fell back to a known-good recovery partition. Automatic silent background updates applied within minutes of login (requiring a restart to activate), eliminating patch cycles that required IT involvement — the model that caused enterprise support costs on Windows. The Chrome OS Admin Console let IT administrators enroll devices, set policies, push extensions, and remotely disable stolen machines across fleets of any size.

Initial consumer sales were weak against Windows laptops with vastly more software compatibility. The education market proved decisive: by 2012, Google had introduced a $99-per-year-per-device management subscription and Chromebooks at $249–$299 undercut iPad and Windows netbook costs for schools. By 2013, Chromebooks had become the top-selling laptop segment in US K–12 education, overtaking Apple. The Google for Education program — combining Chromebooks, Google Classroom (launched August 2014), and G Suite for Education (Drive, Docs, Sheets at no cost to schools) — created a feedback loop where students accustomed to Chrome OS drove later enterprise interest. The Chromebook Pixel, launched February 2013 at $1,299, showed that Chrome OS could target premium hardware design, though it remained a niche product outside education.
