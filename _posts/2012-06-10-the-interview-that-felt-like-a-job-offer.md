There is a dangerous moment in a job interview when everything starts going too well.

The person across from you smiles.

The conversation flows.

Nobody looks bored.

Nobody checks the clock.

They explain the company.

They explain the role.

They ask when you could start.

They speak in a way that makes the future sound strangely close.

You leave the building and begin solving problems that do not exist yet.

How will I resign from my current job?

Should I have said I need two or three months before starting?

Can I finish everything properly?

How quickly can I reorganize my life?

This is what happened to me after one interview.

The first person had called to arrange the appointment.

Another person welcomed me when I arrived.

A third person conducted the interview.

By the time it ended, their behavior had convinced me of one thing:

They were going to offer me the job.

Not maybe.

Probably not even "probably."

In my head, the decision had almost been made.

I had already moved to the next problem.

What would I tell my current workplace?

That is the ridiculous part.

The company had not offered me anything.

I was already mentally resigning.

Then the next day came.

No call.

Fine.

Companies are busy.

The following day.

Nothing.

Still normal.

A week.

Nothing.

Then another.

Eventually a month passed.

Nobody contacted me.

No offer.

No rejection.

No explanation.

Just silence.

There is a particular kind of embarrassment in realizing that you have celebrated something that never happened.

Nobody else even needs to know.

You know.

That is enough.

You remember leaving the interview with confidence.

You remember interpreting every smile.

Every encouraging sentence.

Every detail about the job.

Now all of those memories have to be reexamined.

Maybe they were simply polite.

Maybe they speak to every candidate that way.

Maybe I misread everything.

Maybe somebody else appeared the next morning and was better.

Maybe the position disappeared.

Maybe the budget changed.

Maybe the manager changed their mind.

Maybe they forgot.

The problem with silence is that it provides unlimited space for theories.

A rejection email is disappointing.

Silence is productive.

It can generate explanations for weeks.

Job interviews are strange because they temporarily create a fictional future.

For thirty minutes or an hour, both sides behave as if the future is possible.

"What would you do in this situation?"

"How would you work with this team?"

"Where do you see yourself?"

"When could you start?"

The grammar itself pulls you forward.

You begin imagining yourself at that desk.

Working with those people.

Using that route to commute.

Eating lunch nearby.

Your brain is remarkably cooperative.

Give it a few details and it builds an entire life.

Then the interview ends.

For the company, you may return to being one row in a list of candidates.

For you, the fictional future may continue for days.

That difference is important.

The interview is one meeting for the employer.

For the candidate, it can represent escape.

More money.

A better title.

A new city.

A more interesting project.

A way out of a workplace they no longer enjoy.

A chance to begin again.

So when the interviewer sounds positive, hope grows very quickly.

Perhaps too quickly.

Years ago, my complaint was simple:

You should not give a person that much hope.

I still understand the feeling.

Professional friendliness can be surprisingly easy to misinterpret.

A good interviewer is supposed to make the candidate comfortable.

They want conversation.

They want honest answers.

They may explain the role enthusiastically because that is their job.

None of this is a promise.

But when you are sitting on the other side of the table, every signal feels meaningful.

Especially if you want the job.

Desire is an excellent translator.

It translates:

"We'll be in touch."

into:

"We liked you."

It translates:

"When could you start?"

into:

"We are preparing an offer."

It translates:

"Your experience seems relevant."

into:

"You are exactly who we need."

Then reality sends no message at all.

Recruitment teaches a useful lesson about signals.

Do not count warmth as an offer.

Do not count interest as a decision.

Do not count a successful interview as a job.

Until something is written clearly, the process is still a process.

This sounds cold.

It is actually protective.

Hope is useful.

Premature certainty is expensive.

It changes behavior.

You may stop applying elsewhere.

You may delay another opportunity.

You may begin emotionally leaving your current job.

You may tell people too early.

You may organize your plans around a future that exists only in conversation.

I nearly made that mistake.

I was already thinking about how to leave my current workplace cleanly.

The company had not even called.

Looking back, there is something funny about the speed of it.

One successful conversation and I had mentally created resignation procedures.

Human beings can build entire futures from very little evidence.

A smile.

A sentence.

A good feeling.

This does not happen only with jobs.

Relationships work the same way.

A promising first date.

A message.

A plan for next weekend.

Suddenly the imagination is months ahead.

Then reality may quietly stop.

No explanation.

No continuation.

The mind has to walk backward from a future it built alone.

That may be one reason ghosting feels so unpleasant in any context.

It is not only the absence of communication.

It leaves one person responsible for closing a story that two people appeared to be writing.

Companies are especially good at this because recruitment has professional language for uncertainty.

"We are evaluating candidates."

"We will get back to you."

"The process is ongoing."

Sometimes these statements are true.

Sometimes they become a polite fog through which nobody returns.

From the employer's side, there may be dozens of perfectly ordinary reasons.

Priorities change.

Budgets disappear.

Positions are frozen.

A stronger candidate accepts.

Managers delay decisions.

None of that requires cruelty.

But from the candidate's side, silence still feels personal because the interview was personal.

You were there.

You answered.

You hoped.

Then you heard nothing.

I think the most useful response is not to become cynical.

It is to separate possibility from fact.

Enjoy a good interview.

Be pleased when you think you performed well.

Learn from it.

Then continue with your life.

Continue applying.

Continue working.

Do not resign in your imagination.

Do not buy furniture for the office you do not have.

Do not solve the commute yet.

An interview can go beautifully and still lead nowhere.

That does not mean the interview was fake.

It means the result was not yours to decide.

A month after that meeting, nobody had contacted me.

The people involved had played their parts so convincingly that I had left expecting a new job.

Perhaps they were simply good at their jobs.

Perhaps I was very good at hope.

Either way, I learned to wait for the actual offer before planning the resignation.
