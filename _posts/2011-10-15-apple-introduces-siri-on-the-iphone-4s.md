Apple introduced Siri as a headline feature of the iPhone 4S at an event on October 4, 2011 — the first Apple event presided over by Tim Cook as CEO, and the day before Steve Jobs died on October 5. Apple had acquired Siri Inc. on April 28, 2010 for approximately $200 million; the original Siri app had been released on the App Store on February 4, 2010 as a standalone application that used natural language to book restaurants, find movies, and access web services through third-party API integrations with OpenTable, Fandango, and others before Apple acquired the company and removed the app from the App Store four weeks later. The Siri built into the iPhone 4S was substantially redesigned for deeper iOS integration and marketed with the label "Beta" — the first time Apple had shipped a product feature explicitly marked as unfinished, acknowledging that the system would improve over time rather than being presented as complete at launch. Siri ran on the iPhone 4S's Apple A5 SoC (the dual-core ARM Cortex-A9 processor also used in iPad 2), with audio processing performed on-device but natural language understanding and response generation performed on Apple's servers, requiring a data connection for most requests. Supported capabilities at launch included setting alarms and reminders, sending messages and emails, making calls, querying the weather, looking up restaurant information via Yelp, performing calculations, and answering factual questions through a Wolfram Alpha integration. Siri responded in synthesized speech and displayed text on screen simultaneously.

The technical architecture combined multiple subsystems that Apple had to build or integrate at scale. Speech recognition converted audio input to text using server-side processing (Apple had licensed Nuance's recognition technology for early Siri versions). Natural language understanding (NLU) parsed the resulting text to identify the user's intent and extract named entities — times, people from the user's contacts, locations, application references — mapping them to structured actions the phone could execute. A dialogue management component maintained conversational state across turns, enabling follow-up clarifications like "Change that to 3 PM" after setting a reminder. Third-party data integrations (Yelp for local business, Wolfram Alpha for factual queries, MovieTickets.com for showtimes) supplemented Apple's own services. The server-side processing requirement meant that Siri's availability was coupled to Apple's server infrastructure, which experienced significant overload in the days after launch — Apple reported over 100 million Siri queries in the first week. Error rates were higher than Apple's promotional materials suggested, particularly for proper names, accents outside standard American English, and domain-specific vocabulary. Google had offered voice search on Android since 2010 and its own voice actions since Android 2.2, but these operated as transcription tools routing to web searches rather than conversational assistants maintaining context across exchanges.

Siri's cultural impact significantly exceeded its technical capabilities at launch. The television advertising campaign featuring Siri demonstrations — including a widely-aired commercial showing John Malkovich having a conversation with Siri about rain and oysters — positioned voice interaction as aspirational rather than utilitarian, generating a level of public awareness for a smartphone feature that had no precedent. This framing accelerated competitive responses: Google announced Google Now at Google I/O in June 2012 as a proactive assistant that delivered information before users asked, based on search history and calendar data; Amazon launched the Echo speaker with Alexa in November 2014 as a dedicated far-field voice device; Microsoft introduced Cortana on Windows Phone 8.1 in April 2014 and on Windows 10 desktop in July 2015. For iOS developers, Siri initially presented a limitation rather than an opportunity: Apple did not provide a public Siri API for third-party application integration at launch, meaning developers could not have their apps respond to Siri requests. SiriKit, the API allowing third-party apps to handle specific Siri request domains (messaging, calling, payments, ride-hailing, photo search, workouts), was not introduced until iOS 10 in September 2016 — five years after Siri's original launch — reflecting Apple's cautious approach to expanding Siri's third-party surface area.
