There is a question that can change the atmosphere of a first date very quickly:

"How much do you earn?"

It is not necessarily an unreasonable question.

Money matters.

Jobs matter.

Adult life costs money.

Nobody discovers this at thirty-five with genuine surprise.

Still, there is something about hearing the question too early that makes the conversation feel less like two people meeting and more like an application form.

Name.

Age.

Occupation.

Salary.

Car?

House?

Please attach supporting documents.

Years ago, I wrote about women who asked about salary on the first date.

Then I remembered an even earlier version.

Sometimes the question arrived before the date.

"Do you have a car?"

That one seemed to be popular too.

At the time, I found it funny and slightly irritating.

Then eventually I bought a car.

Problem solved.

Or so I thought.

The question simply changed.

"Do you have a house?"

That was when I began to understand that adulthood sometimes works like a game where the finish line moves every time you get close.

When you are a student, having a job sounds impressive.

Then you get a job.

Now the salary matters.

You increase the salary.

Now transportation matters.

You get a car.

Now property matters.

You buy a house.

Perhaps the next question is about the neighborhood.

Or the size.

Or whether there is a second house.

The checklist is capable of expanding indefinitely.

This is not only about dating.

Life is full of moving targets.

As children, we imagine adulthood as a small number of clear achievements.

Graduate.

Find a job.

Earn money.

Maybe buy a car.

Maybe buy a home.

Then you arrive and discover that each completed item reveals several new ones.

The strange part is that other people's expectations can move faster than your own progress.

You may feel that you are doing reasonably well.

Then a conversation informs you that apparently you are behind.

That was how I felt.

As I got older, I had the sense that expectations from the opposite sex were increasing faster than I could satisfy them.

I was following the same road.

Just a little behind the schedule.

There is humor in that sentence because there is also truth in it.

Adult relationships do involve practical questions.

At twenty, two people can spend an evening walking around with almost no money and call it a date.

Later, practical realities become harder to ignore.

Where will we live?

What kind of work do we do?

Can we support ourselves?

Do we want children?

Do we have debt?

Do our plans point in compatible directions?

These are not shallow questions.

They can determine whether two lives fit together.

The problem is timing.

Ask them too early and the person disappears behind the answers.

You stop meeting a human being.

You begin evaluating infrastructure.

A car can tell you that someone owns a car.

It cannot tell you whether they are kind.

A salary can tell you roughly what they earn.

It cannot tell you how they treat people when nobody important is watching.

A house can tell you they own property.

It cannot tell you whether living with them would be peaceful.

We use measurable things because they are easy.

Numbers feel reliable.

Salary.

Age.

Square meters.

Model year.

Job title.

These can be written in boxes.

Character is much less cooperative.

There is no field that says:

How does this person behave when tired?

Do they apologize?

Can they laugh at themselves?

Are they generous without performing generosity?

What happens when plans fail?

Can they sit quietly with someone?

Do they become cruel when angry?

Those answers require time.

A salary can be asked before the coffee arrives.

This may be why material questions become shortcuts.

They are imperfect proxies for things people actually care about.

Stability.

Responsibility.

Ambition.

Security.

Future prospects.

Someone asking whether you have a car may not truly care about the car.

Maybe they are asking whether your life appears organized.

Someone asking about a home may be imagining marriage, stability, or independence.

The literal question and the real concern are not always identical.

I understood that less generously when I was younger.

My reaction was closer to:

First it was the car.

Now it is the house.

What exactly am I supposed to buy next?

That reaction also had some justification.

There is a difference between wanting a stable partner and treating dating like procurement.

The moment another person feels that they are being priced, attraction changes.

Nobody wants to discover that their most interesting characteristic is an asset column.

There is another uncomfortable truth.

Men do similar things.

We may judge different variables.

Appearance.

Age.

Body.

Style.

Whatever happens to matter to us.

It is easy to criticize somebody else's checklist while pretending not to have one.

Most people enter relationships with preferences, expectations, fears, and filters.

The question is not whether they exist.

It is whether they leave enough room for an actual person.

A checklist can protect you from obvious incompatibility.

It can also prevent surprise.

And surprise is one of the better parts of meeting people.

Sometimes the person who does not match the imagined specification fits your life much better than the person who does.

That requires getting far enough into the conversation to find out.

I think the older I became, the more I understood why practical questions appear.

Life becomes heavier.

Choices become more expensive.

A relationship at eighteen and a relationship at thirty do not always carry the same consequences.

People are not only choosing whom to spend Saturday with.

They may be choosing who will share rent, responsibilities, families, plans and years.

Of course they want information.

But there is a graceful way to discover a person's life.

And there is an interview.

The distinction matters.

If the first conversation feels like a credit assessment, romance has already taken a fairly bureaucratic turn.

Perhaps that old irritation came from feeling that I was always arriving one step late.

When I did not have a car, the car mattered.

Once I had one, the house mattered.

I imagined an endless sequence.

One day I would finally acquire everything anyone could ask for.

Then somebody would lean across the table and say:

"Do you own a summer house?"

At that point, the only reasonable response would be to ask for the evaluation rubric in advance.

At least then I could prepare properly.

Looking back, the moving finish line seems less threatening.

There is no finish line.

That is the trick.

You cannot build a life that satisfies every possible person's expectations.

The requirements are contradictory.

One person wants ambition.

Another wants more free time.

One values property.

Another values travel.

One wants a quiet life.

Another wants constant movement.

Trying to become universally acceptable is an impossible engineering problem.

A better question is whether the life you are building makes sense to you.

Then find someone whose idea of a good life overlaps with it.

That sounds much simpler than trying to pass every first-date inspection.

So yes, ask about work.

Ask about plans.

Eventually ask about money if a relationship becomes serious enough for money to matter.

But perhaps allow the coffee to arrive before requesting the balance sheet.

A person is more than the things they have managed to buy so far.

And if every achievement only produces a new requirement, the problem may not be that you are behind.

It may simply be that you are running toward someone else's finish line.
