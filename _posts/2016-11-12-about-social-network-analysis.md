Social network analysis studies relationships rather than only individuals. That small change in perspective can reveal a lot.

Imagine a group of people as a graph. Each person becomes a node. A friendship, communication link, collaboration, or other relationship becomes an edge.

Once the group is represented this way, questions become possible. Who is highly connected? Which people connect separate communities? Are there clusters inside the network? How quickly can information spread? Which relationships are central to the whole structure?

Measures such as degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality try to capture different meanings of importance. A person with many direct connections may be influential in one way. A person who connects two otherwise separate groups may be important in another.

This is one reason social networks are more interesting than simple popularity counts. The number of connections is only part of the story.

Social network analysis has applications in sociology, marketing, communication, epidemiology, security, recommendation systems, and many other areas. It can help researchers study the spread of information or disease. Organizations can examine collaboration patterns. Online platforms can study communities and interactions.

Computer science becomes important because large networks can contain millions of nodes and edges. Efficient graph algorithms, databases, visualization tools, and statistical methods are needed to make the data understandable. But there is also an ethical side.

A social network can reveal sensitive information even when individual data looks harmless. Connections may tell us who someone works with, trusts, visits, or communicates with.

So the ability to analyze networks also creates responsibility. I like social network analysis because it shows something we already know in everyday life but often forget in data analysis: people do not exist alone. Sometimes the relationships explain more than the individual parts.
