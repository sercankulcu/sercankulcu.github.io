Years ago, I wrote a single sentence about forgetting:

**Forgetting is not something a person can do. It is something that happens to a person.**

I still think there is truth in that.

We talk about forgetting as if it were an action.

Forget her.

Forget him.

Forget what happened.

Forget the past.

Move on.

The language makes it sound simple.

Almost like closing a window on a computer.

Click.

Done.

But memory does not work like that.

You cannot usually decide that a person, a place, a sentence or a period of your life has occupied enough space in your head.

You cannot issue a command.

You cannot type:

delete memory

and expect silence.

In fact, trying to forget something often does the opposite.

You check whether you have forgotten it.

That check itself becomes remembering.

You ask yourself:

Do I still think about it?

And there it is again.

The thing you were trying not to think about.

Maybe this is why advice about forgetting can feel so useless.

People usually mean well.

They see someone suffering and want to give them an exit.

Forget it.

Do not think about it.

There are other people.

There will be other places.

Time heals everything.

Perhaps.

But none of those sentences explain how forgetting actually happens.

Because there is no method.

There is only time, repetition, replacement, distance and whatever mysterious process the mind uses to decide which details it still needs.

Some memories disappear quickly.

You cannot remember what you ate three days ago.

You forget a phone number you used for years.

A password vanishes.

The name of someone you met once becomes impossible to retrieve.

Then there are memories that refuse to leave.

A particular street.

A smell.

A song.

The exact expression on someone's face.

A message.

A sentence that should have been ordinary.

These stay without asking permission.

Sometimes what remains is completely irrational.

You forget entire conversations but remember where the other person was sitting.

You forget the date but remember the weather.

You forget what you were wearing but remember what song was playing.

You forget the important part and keep something useless.

Memory has its own priorities.

They are rarely ours.

There is another strange part.

Sometimes forgetting happens so slowly that you do not notice it.

A person who once occupied your mind every hour becomes someone you think about once a day.

Then once a week.

Then something reminds you of them unexpectedly and you realize that three months have passed.

You did not decide to forget.

You were busy living.

New things arrived.

Old things moved deeper into storage.

Then one day you try to remember something that used to be immediate.

The exact sound of a voice.

A face.

A room.

And for the first time, the memory does not come clearly.

That can be frightening.

For a long time you wanted to forget.

Then, when forgetting finally begins, you may suddenly want the memory back.

Because forgetting is not only relief.

It is loss.

We sometimes keep painful memories because they are the last remaining proof that something mattered.

If the pain disappears completely, what happens to the person?

What happens to the relationship?

What happens to that version of ourselves?

Maybe this is why people save things.

Old messages.

Photographs.

Letters.

Tickets.

Objects that would be worthless to anyone else.

They are external memory.

A backup.

Evidence.

This happened.

I was there.

I knew this person.

I felt this way.

We distrust our own memory because we know what time does to it.

It edits.

Compresses.

Combines.

Deletes.

Sometimes improves.

Sometimes makes worse.

And unlike a computer, it rarely asks before saving changes.

I have noticed that songs are particularly resistant to forgetting.

You may forget the room where you first heard a song.

Then the song begins and suddenly the room returns.

Not perfectly.

Enough.

Music seems to keep doors open that ordinary memory eventually closes.

Places do the same thing.

You walk through a street after many years and your body recognizes it before your mind does.

A corner feels familiar.

A building triggers something.

The past appears not as a complete story, but as fragments.

Maybe that is how memory survives best.

Not as a continuous archive.

As triggers.

A word.

A smell.

A melody.

A wall.

A computer.

A city.

A person.

This is also why trying to erase the past is probably the wrong goal.

You cannot control which fragments will return.

You can only change what happens when they do.

At first, a memory arrives and ruins the day.

Later, it arrives and hurts for an hour.

Later still, it becomes something you can look at.

Then one day you may even smile.

The memory did not disappear.

Its weight changed.

That may be what people really mean when they say they have forgotten someone.

They usually have not forgotten.

They can still tell you the name.

They remember what happened.

They remember enough.

What has disappeared is the urgency.

The need to check.

The need to understand.

The need to rewrite the ending.

The memory is still there.

It simply no longer controls the room.

That is different from forgetting.

Maybe it is better.

Complete forgetting is not always possible, and perhaps not always desirable.

Some memories should remain.

Even painful ones.

They remind us what we wanted.

What we misunderstood.

What we lost.

What we survived.

What we should not repeat.

What was once important enough to hurt.

The problem begins when we demand that memory obey us.

Forget this.

Keep that.

Remove this person.

Preserve that summer.

Human memory does not accept instructions so neatly.

It has its own schedule.

So I still believe the sentence I wrote years ago.

Forgetting is not something you do.

It happens.

Usually while you are occupied with something else.

Usually later than you want.

And sometimes, when it finally happens, earlier than you expected.
