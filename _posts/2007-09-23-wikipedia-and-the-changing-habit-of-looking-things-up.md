When I was younger, looking something up usually meant finding the right book. An encyclopedia had weight, order, and a limited number of pages. If the information was not there, the search had to continue somewhere else.

Wikipedia has changed that habit for me. It is often the first place I visit when I meet an unfamiliar person, scientific term, historical event, or technology. The speed is difficult to ignore. A question appears, and within seconds I have a page containing definitions, dates, links, and references.

The most unusual part is that the encyclopedia is written and edited by volunteers. At first, this sounds like a reason not to trust it. Anyone can make a mistake, and controversial pages can become places of argument. The quality is not equal everywhere.

But traditional sources are not perfect either. The difference is that Wikipedia makes the editing process more visible. A page has a history. Discussions can be read. Sources can be checked. When used carefully, it becomes not only an answer but a starting point for deeper reading.

I think the important skill is knowing when "good enough for orientation" is enough. If I want to understand who a historical person was or what a technical term means, Wikipedia may be useful. If I am writing an academic paper, I should follow the references and find stronger original sources.

This is a larger lesson about the web. Information is becoming easier to reach, but easier access does not remove the need for judgment. In some ways it increases it. When ten answers are one click away, choosing which answer deserves trust becomes part of learning.

I sometimes miss the slower feeling of opening a physical encyclopedia and accidentally reading the article beside the one I wanted. The web is faster but more directed. Still, Wikipedia has made curiosity cheaper. A small question no longer needs to wait until I find the right shelf.
