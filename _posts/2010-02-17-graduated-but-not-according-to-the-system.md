Graduating from university sounds like a clear event.

You finish the courses.

You pass the exams.

The department accepts that you are done.

There should be a moment when the system looks at you and says:

Congratulations. You graduated.

That was not exactly how it felt.

In February 2010, I needed a temporary graduation document from Hacettepe University.

It cost fifty lira.

Calling it a piece of paper would have been unfair.

It had official information on the back.

There were stamps.

Signatures.

Different offices.

Different people.

Each part seemed to require its own small pilgrimage.

I had submitted the petition five working days earlier.

Still, the document was not ready.

That would already have been annoying.

The real problem was that I needed it for something that did not care about university bureaucracy.

Military status.

On paper, I had graduated.

In practice, the system still had other ideas.

The document itself eventually stated that I had graduated on February 3, 2010.

Yet on February 16, I was apparently still not officially shown as a graduate.

That creates a wonderful bureaucratic question.

If I was not considered graduated yesterday, why does the document you are giving me today say that I graduated almost two weeks ago?

There is no satisfying answer to that kind of question.

Only counters.

Forms.

Waiting.

And increasingly loud conversations.

I eventually got the document after explaining — not especially quietly — that the delay was pushing me into a military-status problem.

Nothing teaches assertiveness quite like discovering that a harmless administrative delay has consequences for you and none for the person behind the desk.

University prepares you for many things.

Algorithms.

Exams.

Projects.

Presentations.

Deadlines.

Then graduation introduces a different curriculum.

Forms.

Signatures.

Official dates.

Databases that do not agree with each other.

Documents proving events that everybody already knows happened.

This is one of the first lessons of adult bureaucracy:

Reality is not enough.

Reality needs paperwork.

You may have finished the degree.

Your professors may know.

Your department may know.

You may know.

None of that necessarily matters to the next institution.

They want the document.

Preferably stamped.

Possibly signed.

Sometimes photocopied.

Maybe submitted in person.

And if one database has not been updated, you enter a strange state.

Graduated in real life.

Not graduated in the system.

Schrödinger's engineer.

The transition out of university is already psychologically strange.

For years, your identity is simple.

Student.

There is always another semester.

Another registration.

Another course.

Another exam.

Then suddenly the structure ends.

You are finished.

At least academically.

But institutions do not transition as quickly as people do.

You can finish on one date and become officially finished later.

Between those moments, you exist in an administrative borderland.

No longer quite a student.

Not yet fully recognized as a graduate.

And naturally, this is exactly when other systems begin asking you to prove what you are.

Employment.

Military service.

Applications.

Official procedures.

Everybody wants documentation immediately.

The university wants time.

This is where adulthood begins to reveal one of its recurring jokes.

Organizations communicate with each other through documents you personally carry between them.

One institution says:

Bring proof from the other institution.

You go there.

They say:

Submit a request.

You submit it.

Come back later.

You come back later.

The document is not ready.

You explain why you need it.

Eventually someone signs something.

Now you carry the evidence back to the first institution.

In theory, all of these organizations are part of the same modern society.

In practice, you are the network protocol.

I find that funny now.

At the time, much less so.

Fifty lira also felt meaningful.

Not because it was an enormous amount of money in every context.

Because it was being charged for a document proving something I had already spent years and far more money accomplishing.

Four years of education, and then:

Please pay for the paper confirming it.

There is an almost ceremonial quality to that.

The final exam is not the final exam.

The final exam is paperwork.

Perhaps this is why people remember administrative frustrations from university so clearly.

A difficult course can at least justify itself.

It is supposed to be difficult.

An exam is designed to test you.

A project is supposed to require effort.

But waiting several days for a document feels like friction without purpose.

No knowledge is gained.

No skill is demonstrated.

Nothing interesting is produced.

Time simply disappears between offices.

And because the procedure is official, the absurdity becomes untouchable.

"This is how the process works."

That sentence has ended more arguments than logic ever could.

Still, bureaucracy occasionally teaches useful habits.

Keep copies.

Check dates.

Do not assume systems update automatically.

Ask what document is actually required.

Submit things earlier than seems necessary.

Follow up.

And when someone says "it should be ready," understand that "should" is doing important work in that sentence.

These are not glamorous lessons.

Nobody puts them on a university brochure.

But they become surprisingly valuable.

Years later, what remains funny is the contradiction printed directly into the situation.

The paper said:

Graduated on February 3.

The system effectively said:

Not graduated on February 16.

Both statements somehow coexisted.

Then on February 17, after enough effort, the paper finally entered my hand.

Perhaps that is when graduation became real.

Not at the final exam.

Not when the department approved it.

Not on the date printed on the document.

When the bureaucracy finally produced evidence that it had happened.

There is a small tragedy in reducing years of university life to a stamped sheet.

There is also something satisfying about it.

The sheet may be ridiculous.

But it closes a door.

You hold it.

You leave the office.

And for once, nobody can tell you to register for another semester.

At least until the next institution asks for another document.
