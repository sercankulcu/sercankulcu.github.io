I once decided that you realize you are getting older when you notice that you cannot sleep more than six hours.

At the time, I joked that I was getting dangerously close to my grandfather.

It was not a scientific observation.

It was something better for an old internet entry:

a small complaint that felt completely true at two in the morning.

Age is supposed to arrive through birthdays.

Thirty.

Forty.

Fifty.

Round numbers.

Candles.

People making jokes about your back.

But I do not think that is how most of us actually notice it.

You notice age through strange little betrayals.

You wake up earlier than you used to.

You start caring about the quality of a chair.

Loud places become less interesting.

A night out develops consequences.

You look at young people and wonder why they are shouting.

You hear yourself say things that older people used to say.

Then one morning you open your eyes after six hours of sleep and discover that your body has apparently completed the transaction.

No negotiation.

No snooze button capable of restoring youth.

Just:

Good morning.

We are awake now.

This can be deeply annoying if you remember a younger version of yourself who could sleep through half the day.

Student sleep had ambition.

You could go to bed late and wake up when the morning had already given up.

No guilt.

No schedule.

No mysterious internal alarm clock.

Sleep was not something you managed.

It was something that happened until it stopped.

Then adulthood begins assigning appointments to your body.

Wake up.

Go to work.

Do this.

Do that.

Even on a free day, the body sometimes continues following a timetable nobody asked for.

That may be one of the first signs that routine has moved from your calendar into your nervous system.

The unfair part is that young people often imagine adulthood as greater freedom.

No parents telling you when to sleep.

No school in the morning.

No one controlling your schedule.

Technically correct.

Then you become an adult and discover that the schedule has become internal.

You are finally free to sleep until noon.

Your body wakes you at seven.

Excellent.

This is how age often works.

You gain permission after losing the ability.

You can buy all the chocolate you want.

Then you start reading labels.

You can stay out all night.

Then you prefer going home.

You can spend money however you like.

Then bills arrive.

Freedom remains.

Enthusiasm becomes more selective.

I do not think this is necessarily bad.

There is a tendency to describe aging as a list of things disappearing.

Energy.

Time.

Hair.

Patience.

Sleep.

But something else happens too.

Preferences become clearer.

You stop doing certain things simply because everybody else is doing them.

You begin to understand which inconveniences are worth tolerating and which are not.

At twenty, spending three hours somewhere terrible can become a funny story.

Later, you may simply leave.

That is not always becoming boring.

Sometimes it is becoming efficient.

Still, sleep is a cruel place to notice the change because sleep is supposed to be the one activity that requires no effort.

You lie down.

That is the assignment.

Then somehow even this becomes complicated.

Too warm.

Too cold.

Wrong pillow.

Noise outside.

Wake up early.

Wake up for no reason.

Look at the clock.

Calculate how many hours remain.

Nothing makes sleep disappear faster than calculating it.

The younger version of you would be embarrassed.

He did not need mathematics to sleep.

There is also something funny about comparing yourself with your grandfather.

When you are young, grandparents seem to belong to another biological category.

They wake up early.

They sit quietly.

They have routines.

They know what time everything happens.

You cannot imagine becoming anything like them.

Then life begins moving small pieces into place.

You start waking earlier.

You enjoy silence.

You prefer familiar places.

You catch yourself repeating a story.

You become suspicious of unnecessary noise.

One habit at a time, the distance becomes smaller.

Not because you have become old overnight.

Because you finally understand that older people were not born old.

They arrived there through thousands of ordinary days.

This should be obvious.

It does not feel obvious until it starts happening to you.

The strange thing about aging is that the person inside does not update at the same speed as the body.

Some part of you remains surprised.

You can be thirty, forty or sixty and still encounter evidence of your age like unexpected news.

What do you mean I cannot do that anymore?

What do you mean that happened twenty years ago?

What do you mean that student is younger than the shoes I still own?

Time passes continuously.

Awareness arrives in episodes.

A photograph.

A reunion.

A pain where no pain used to be.

Someone calling you "sir."

A favorite album having an anniversary edition.

Or simply waking up after six hours and realizing that sleep has apparently completed its work.

I like these small moments because they are more honest than birthdays.

Birthdays are scheduled.

You know they are coming.

You prepare.

The small moments surprise you.

They reveal the distance traveled without ceremony.

You do not need to become sentimental about it.

Sometimes the correct response is simply to complain.

That is what I did.

Six hours?

Seriously?

At this rate, I am going to catch up with my grandfather.

The joke worked because aging felt impossibly far away and strangely close at the same time.

Maybe it always does.

We imagine age as a destination.

In reality, it arrives as habits.

One morning at a time.
