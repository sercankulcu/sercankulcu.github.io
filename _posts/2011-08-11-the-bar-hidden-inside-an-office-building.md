Some places feel strange before you even enter them.

Not because they are dangerous.

Not because they are beautiful.

Because they are in the wrong building.

In August 2011, I wrote a short entry about SSK İşhanı in Ankara.

Years earlier, it had contained the second bar I had ever gone to.

The bar was called Baraka.

What I remembered most was not the music.

Not the drinks.

Not even the people.

It was the fact that the bar was inside an office building.

At the time, that idea felt bizarre.

A bar belonged on a street full of other bars.

Or behind a visible entrance.

Or somewhere that announced itself properly.

An office building belonged to daytime.

Desks.

Paperwork.

People carrying folders.

Elevators.

Fluorescent lights.

A bar belonged to night.

Music.

Noise.

Smoke.

Crowds.

Two different worlds.

Then somebody told me there was a bar inside an iş hanı.

I first found the idea strange.

Then I saw it.

Still strange.

Then we went in.

And I had a very good time.

That is the entire archive entry.

A few sentences.

But it preserves a useful moment from being young in a city.

The moment when your mental map of what belongs where begins to break.

Cities teach you categories.

Residential.

Commercial.

Office.

Nightlife.

Industrial.

Campus.

Shopping.

Then real cities ignore their own categories.

A bar appears upstairs in a business building.

A restaurant operates in a basement.

A workshop hides behind an apartment entrance.

A bookstore exists on the third floor of a place you would never enter unless someone told you.

The city has a visible layer and a hidden one.

When you are young, discovering the hidden layer feels like gaining access.

Not necessarily to anything exclusive.

Just to knowledge.

You now know there is something there.

That changes a building permanently.

Before:

office building.

After:

the building with that bar.

Cities become personal through this kind of annotation.

Official maps contain streets.

Personal maps contain stories.

This is where we waited for someone.

This is where the car broke down.

This is where we ate after midnight.

This is where I got rejected.

This is where I laughed until my stomach hurt.

This is where a bar was somehow operating inside an office building.

The official function of the structure becomes irrelevant.

Memory assigns its own label.

That is why cities lived in are different from cities visited.

A visitor sees architecture.

A resident accumulates invisible metadata.

A corner has emotional meaning.

A stairwell has history.

A building may look unremarkable to everyone else and still contain one of your strongest memories.

SSK İşhanı became that kind of place for me.

The detail that it was only the second bar I had ever gone to matters too.

Second experiences are underrated.

First experiences receive ceremonial importance.

First flight.

First job.

First car.

First concert.

First bar.

But the first time you do something, you are often too busy understanding the rules.

The second time can be when the activity begins to feel like part of your life.

The first bar may teach you what a bar is.

The second teaches you that bars are not all the same.

One may look conventional.

Another may be hidden inside a building that seems designed for accountants.

That second example expands the category.

This is how experience works generally.

The first instance creates a model.

The second breaks it.

You meet one professor and think professors are like that.

Then another is completely different.

One workplace teaches you what offices are.

The next makes you realize the first was only one office.

One relationship teaches you a pattern.

The next reveals the pattern was local, not universal.

The first example gives confidence.

The second restores humility.

Baraka, at least in this tiny memory, performed that function.

Apparently nightlife could live somewhere I would never have guessed.

Ankara has always rewarded that kind of discovery.

The city is not visually generous in the way some cities are.

It does not always reveal its best places from the street.

A person may pass a building many times without knowing what exists above, below, or behind it.

This encourages recommendation culture.

Someone has to tell you.

Go into that building.

Take the stairs.

There is a place upstairs.

Trust me.

This kind of instruction creates social geography.

The city is navigated partly through friends.

Not signs.

A recommendation is a temporary key.

Without the person who knows, the place might remain invisible.

That creates a different relationship with urban space.

You do not simply discover by walking.

You inherit routes.

Someone takes you.

Later you take someone else.

This is how hidden places survive in collective memory.

Not through visibility.

Through transmission.

A bar inside an office building needs stories more than signage.

That may also be why such places feel more intimate.

The act of finding them filters the crowd slightly.

Not necessarily by status.

By information.

You need to know.

Or be with someone who knows.

This can create the feeling of a secret even when the place is publicly accessible.

Young people love this feeling.

We like believing we have found the real version of the city.

Not the obvious place.

The hidden one.

The upstairs one.

The basement one.

The one tourists would miss.

Sometimes this belief is ridiculous.

The place may be extremely well known.

Only we are new.

But newness makes ordinary knowledge feel like discovery.

That is part of youth.

The world contains many things other people already know.

You encounter them for the first time and experience revelation.

There is no shame in that.

The personal first time is still real even when the world is unimpressed.

I suspect this is why the memory survived.

Not because the location was objectively extraordinary.

Because my expectations were still narrow enough to be surprised.

A bar in an office building?

Can that happen?

Yes.

Apparently.

And better:

it can be fun.

The younger mind likes categories because categories make the world manageable.

Bars look like this.

Adults behave like that.

Workplaces function this way.

Relationships progress in this order.

Success looks like that.

Then life keeps placing things in the wrong building.

People refuse their categories.

Careers take strange routes.

Friendships form between unlikely people.

Important moments happen in unimpressive places.

You begin to understand that reality is much less architecturally disciplined than your expectations.

That is a good thing.

One of the quiet freedoms of getting older is becoming less surprised by mixed-use life.

You stop asking whether something belongs there.

You ask whether it works.

That is a more useful question.

The bar apparently worked.

We had fun.

Case closed.

There is another layer to the memory.

Office buildings transform after business hours.

During the day, they have one identity.

Reception desks.

Workers.

Deliveries.

Phones.

At night, many empty out.

The same hallways become strange.

A building without its normal population feels almost theatrical.

Places have social operating systems.

Remove the expected people and the architecture changes emotionally.

A school at night feels different.

An office on Sunday feels different.

A shopping center before opening feels different.

A bar inside an office building creates overlapping schedules.

One system powers down.

Another starts.

People who spent the day processing paperwork leave.

People going out arrive.

The building changes shift.

This kind of layered use is common in dense cities.

One structure serves several worlds.

The same elevator may carry employees in the morning and people going to music at night.

From an urban perspective, that is efficient.

From a young person's perspective, it is amusing.

Buildings, it turns out, have secret lives too.

The old entry says only that I found it strange and that we had a lot of fun.

It does not say who I was with.

It does not describe the music.

It does not tell me the floor.

It does not preserve the interior.

Those details are gone, at least from this source.

That absence is important.

It would be easy to manufacture atmosphere.

Dim stairs.

A certain smell.

A narrow corridor.

A particular song.

But none of that is in the archive.

Memory writing becomes less honest when missing details are silently replaced with plausible ones.

Plausible is not remembered.

So the story has to work with what survived.

Second bar.

Office building.

Initial disbelief.

Then seeing it.

Then a good night.

Sometimes four facts are enough.

The lack of detail also says something about how memory works.

We think we remember experiences.

Often we remember summaries.

That night was fun.

That place was strange.

I liked that person.

I hated that job.

These conclusions remain after the raw sensory data disappears.

The brain keeps the compressed file.

Years later, we try to decompress it.

Some information is simply gone.

Personal archives help because they preserve little pieces before compression finishes.

A single entry can rescue the existence of a place from total forgetting.

Without it, maybe I would still remember Baraka.

Maybe not.

Maybe I would remember the bar but forget it was only the second one I had gone to.

Maybe I would remember the building but not my original surprise.

The archive preserves not just what happened.

It preserves the version of me reacting to it.

That is more valuable.

Facts can sometimes be reconstructed.

Reactions are time-sensitive.

A person today would not be surprised by the same thing in the same way.

Once your mental model expands, it cannot become innocent again.

You can imitate surprise.

You cannot restore the old expectation exactly.

That is why younger writing matters.

It preserves obsolete assumptions.

At the time, I genuinely thought:

A bar inside an office building?

What kind of place is this?

Now the idea barely seems unusual.

Cities contain stranger combinations.

My reaction has changed because experience has expanded the allowed configuration space.

That sentence sounds too technical for nightlife.

But it is accurate.

The range of arrangements I consider normal is larger now.

That is one form of growing up.

Not becoming harder to impress.

Becoming harder to confuse.

The downside is that novelty decreases.

The upside is tolerance.

Things do not have to fit the expected category before you accept them.

People benefit from the same lesson.

A serious person can be funny.

A quiet person can have strong opinions.

An engineer can love poetry.

A bar can live in an office building.

We make errors when we confuse packaging with capability.

The location suggested one thing.

The experience delivered another.

This is why actually entering matters.

From outside, we classify.

Inside, we learn.

Many judgments fail at the doorway.

That can be literal.

Or not.

There is also something charming about remembering a time when going to a bar itself still carried novelty.

After enough repetition, nightlife becomes ordinary.

You develop preferences.

You know what you like.

You recognize patterns.

The discovery phase disappears.

Early experiences are messier and more open.

You may go somewhere simply because someone suggests it.

You do not yet have a carefully optimized social routine.

That openness creates unusual memories.

Later, people often become efficient about leisure.

We know the neighborhood.

The type of place.

The music.

The price range.

The crowd.

Efficiency reduces bad nights.

It can also reduce strange ones.

A hidden bar in an office building belongs to the era before everything was filtered into a preference system.

You heard about it.

You went.

It was weird.

You had fun.

There is value in occasionally keeping that process.

Not every outing needs extensive research.

Not every place needs to match an established taste profile.

Serendipity requires some informational imperfection.

If you know everything before arriving, arrival has less work to do.

In the early 2000s and around that period, places circulated differently too.

Recommendations traveled through people more heavily.

A friend.

A colleague.

Someone who had been there.

Online information existed, of course, but not with today's constant map reviews, photographs, menus, crowd reports, and algorithmic suggestions.

You could arrive with less visual preloading.

That made physical discovery stronger.

You heard:

There is a bar in that building.

Then you actually had to reconcile the statement with the building in front of you.

Today, before visiting, you might see thirty photographs.

Know the entrance.

Know the floor.

Read the reviews.

See the interior.

Check the busiest hours.

By the time you arrive, surprise has been partially consumed.

Convenience increased.

Mystery decreased.

This is not nostalgia for inconvenience.

Maps are useful.

Reviews are useful.

Finding places is easier.

But there was a specific pleasure in going somewhere based on incomplete description.

You had to trust the person.

"There's a bar in there."

You look at the building.

Really?

"Yes."

Fine.

Let's go.

The moment contains doubt.

That is part of the story.

Cities are less magical when every hidden door has already been photographed.

Still, hidden urban experiences have not disappeared.

They have only changed.

The real secret is no longer necessarily location.

It may be timing.

Who you are with.

What happens unexpectedly.

A place can be thoroughly documented online and still become personally unique.

Because experience is not metadata.

The internet can tell you where the bar is.

It cannot tell you what the night will mean later.

That remains private.

The old entry gives me one final thought.

I remembered having fun more clearly than I remembered what made the place fun.

This may be one of the purest measures of a good night.

The details fade.

The emotional verdict remains.

We had a very good time.

No elaborate justification.

No need to explain why.

Sometimes memory should be allowed to remain that simple.

A building that looked wrong for a bar.

A young man going to only the second bar of his life.

A moment of disbelief.

Then the door opens.

The category breaks.

And the night works anyway.
