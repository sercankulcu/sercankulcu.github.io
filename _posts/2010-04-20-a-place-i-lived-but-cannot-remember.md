There are places from childhood that stay with you forever.

A staircase.

A garden wall.

A kitchen window.

The smell of a hallway.

A tree you climbed.

A shop on the corner.

A street that seemed enormous when you were five and strangely small when you returned as an adult.

Then there are places that belong to your life but not to your memory.

Tütünçiftlik is one of those places for me.

Years after childhood, I learned that I had lived there until I was about two years old.

There were photographs.

One or two.

That was almost all.

No street I could recognize.

No room I could reconstruct.

No sound.

No smell.

No neighbor.

No little scene that returned when somebody mentioned the name.

According to the facts of my life, I had been there.

According to my memory, I had not.

I found that strange.

We usually think our past belongs to us because we lived it.

But living through something does not guarantee ownership of it later.

Memory decides.

Or fails to decide.

The first years of life are full of experiences that shape us without becoming stories we can tell.

People held us.

Rooms surrounded us.

Journeys happened.

Someone carried us through doors.

We slept in beds whose locations we may never know.

We learned sounds.

Faces.

Safety.

Fear.

Habits.

And then almost all of it vanished from conscious memory.

That means every person has a hidden biography.

A period of life known mostly by other people.

Parents remember.

Relatives remember.

Photographs remember in their limited way.

Documents remember addresses and dates.

But the person at the center remembers almost nothing.

There is something unsettling about that.

Imagine being told:

You lived here.

And looking around as if someone were describing another person's childhood.

That was how Tütünçiftlik felt to me.

The name belonged to my biography.

The place did not belong to my inner map.

Most places in our personal history are connected to scenes.

Ankara is not simply Ankara.

It is roads, schools, apartments, bars, buses, friends, winter, university, work, certain songs, certain nights.

A place becomes yours through accumulation.

Memory attaches itself to corners.

Without those attachments, a location remains geographic.

Tütünçiftlik was geographic.

I could point to it.

I could be told facts about it.

But I could not enter it from inside myself.

The photographs made the situation even stranger.

Photographs are supposed to help memory.

Sometimes they do.

You see an old image and suddenly the rest returns.

The room outside the frame.

The person who took the picture.

What happened afterward.

But very early photographs can do the opposite.

You look at yourself in the picture and recognize the child only because everybody has told you who it is.

That child is you.

Obviously.

Same biography.

Same body continued forward through time.

Yet psychologically, the connection can feel weak.

You do not remember being him.

You cannot remember what he was looking at.

You do not know what he wanted five seconds after the shutter clicked.

You are observing yourself as a stranger.

That is one of photography's quiet paradoxes.

A photograph can prove that something happened without helping you remember it.

There I am.

Therefore I was there.

Evidence without recollection.

We rely on this kind of evidence more than we admit.

Family stories become part of our identity even when we cannot verify them from memory.

"You used to do this."

"You were afraid of that."

"You loved this food."

"You always wanted to go there."

"You lived in this house."

These statements slowly become our own history.

We repeat them later.

"I used to..."

Even though the original memory may belong entirely to someone else.

Our autobiographies are collaborative documents.

Parents write the opening chapters.

Eventually we take over.

Nobody announces the handoff.

At some point, memories become truly ours.

Before that, other people are carrying them for us.

This makes losing older relatives especially significant.

When someone who remembers your earliest years dies, part of your archive disappears with them.

Not necessarily the major facts.

Those may exist in documents.

The small details vanish.

How you reacted to a certain person.

Which word you pronounced strangely.

What made you laugh.

Where you sat.

What ordinary afternoon happened once and never became important enough to photograph.

We do not know which questions to ask until much later.

By then, sometimes the person with the answer is gone.

That is why a simple sentence such as "you lived there until you were two" can feel unexpectedly large.

It reveals how much of your own life is inaccessible.

Not secret.

Simply unrecoverable.

Modern life encourages us to think this problem has been solved.

Children today may have thousands of photographs.

Videos.

Messages.

Cloud backups.

Locations.

School records.

Digital traces beginning almost at birth.

In theory, much more of childhood can be preserved.

But preservation is not memory.

A thousand photographs can still become a collection of scenes you do not internally remember.

They may tell you more.

They cannot return consciousness to the child in the image.

You can know what happened.

You still cannot become the person who experienced it.

That boundary is permanent.

Maybe that is why places are so powerful.

Returning to a childhood location sometimes triggers memory in ways photographs cannot.

The shape of a room.

A smell.

The angle of light.

The distance between two buildings.

Something bodily recognizes the environment before language does.

People say:

I had completely forgotten this.

Then suddenly they remember.

But what happens if you return and nothing comes?

That possibility interests me more.

You stand somewhere objectively connected to your life.

Nothing inside responds.

No flash.

No familiarity.

No emotional recognition.

Just knowledge:

I was here.

It is almost archaeological.

You are investigating your own existence through evidence.

The place becomes an excavation site.

This building existed.

This photograph was taken nearby.

My family lived here.

Therefore the child in the story was me.

Personal history normally feels continuous because memory hides the gaps.

We remember selected moments and assume a smooth road between them.

But there are enormous missing sections.

Years blur.

Months disappear.

Entire routines vanish.

We remember one afternoon from a period and allow it to represent hundreds of other days.

The continuity is partly constructed.

This is not a flaw.

It is how memory works.

If we remembered every breakfast, every walk, every ordinary conversation, we would drown in detail.

Forgetting is necessary.

The strange part is realizing how much was forgotten.

Tütünçiftlik gave me that realization in miniature.

I had lived somewhere for perhaps two years.

Two years is not a small amount of time.

For an adult, two years can contain jobs, relationships, houses, trips, losses, achievements.

For a very young child, two years can disappear almost entirely.

Same duration.

Different access.

Time exists objectively.

Memory does not respect equal units.

One minute can survive for forty years.

A year can vanish.

This is why our remembered life is not the same length as our lived life.

Some periods become dense.

Others become almost empty.

There are weeks from university I can reconstruct through classrooms, people, and routines.

There are later months that compress into a single sentence.

"I was working there."

That is all.

Memory edits without asking permission.

Sometimes it edits too aggressively.

Places can resist that editing.

A street name appears.

A photograph is found.

Someone mentions a neighborhood.

Suddenly the blank area in your biography becomes visible.

Not filled.

Visible.

There is a difference.

I do not know whether I ever returned to Tütünçiftlik expecting some miraculous recognition.

Perhaps that would be asking too much.

The place in 2010 would not have been the place from the beginning of my life anyway.

Buildings change.

Roads change.

People leave.

Even if the geography remained, the world around it would be different.

Returning does not truly return you.

It creates a meeting between two times.

The present person visits the location of the past person.

Sometimes they recognize each other.

Sometimes they do not.

That can be disappointing if you believe memory should behave like a film archive.

But perhaps there is another way to think about it.

Not remembering does not mean the period had no effect.

The two-year-old child does not need to leave a story for the adult to have been shaped by what happened there.

The environment mattered.

The family situation mattered.

People mattered.

Experiences entered the developing person even if they never became accessible memories.

We are built partly from things we cannot narrate.

That idea is both frustrating and comforting.

Frustrating because self-knowledge will always be incomplete.

Comforting because not everything meaningful needs to be remembered consciously to have mattered.

There are people who cared for us before we could remember their care.

Places that protected us before we could recognize them as home.

Routines that gave us stability before we knew what stability meant.

The absence of memory does not erase their contribution.

It only erases our ability to tell the story directly.

So other evidence has to speak.

Photographs.

Family stories.

Addresses.

Names.

A line in an old archive.

In 2010, I wrote that Tütünçiftlik was somewhere I had learned, many years later, that I had lived until the age of two.

I had nothing from it except one or two photographs.

No memory.

No connection.

That was the entire entry.

At the time, the emptiness was the point.

Now I think the emptiness itself is a kind of memory.

Not a memory of the place.

A memory of discovering that my own past contained rooms I could never enter.

That realization has stayed with me much longer than any image of Tütünçiftlik ever did.

Perhaps identity is not only made from what we remember.

It is also made from what we know must have happened before memory began.

A life starts earlier than its narrator does.
