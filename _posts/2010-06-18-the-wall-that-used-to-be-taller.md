There was a wall from my childhood that I remembered as enormous.

Not metaphorically enormous.

Actually enormous.

At least that was how it existed in my memory.

There was a mulberry tree on the other side, and getting to the fruit was a small engineering project.

Three or four children would gather stones.

One stone on top of another.

Then another.

We would test the pile.

Someone would climb.

Someone would hold the stones.

Someone would complain that the others had taken the best mulberries.

The wall was an obstacle.

A proper one.

It had height.

It required planning.

It required cooperation.

And because we were children, successfully reaching the top felt like an achievement.

Years later, I went back.

About fifteen years had passed.

I wanted to see the wall again.

Part of me was simply curious.

How tall had it really been?

I had carried its size in my head for years.

I expected something impressive.

Then I found it.

The wall barely reached my shoulder.

That was all.

For a moment, I felt cheated.

This?

This was the wall?

The wall that had required stones?

The wall we had looked at from below as if it protected another country?

The wall had not changed very much.

I had.

That is one of the simplest and strangest disappointments of growing up.

You return to a place that was large in childhood and discover that the dimensions were never what you remembered.

A school corridor becomes short.

A playground becomes small.

A hill becomes a gentle slope.

A room that once seemed enormous can be crossed in a few steps.

And the wall that once required a team of children and a pile of stones does not even reach your head.

Memory does not use centimeters.

That is the problem.

It measures places according to the person you were when you experienced them.

A wall seen by a child is not the same wall seen by an adult.

The concrete may be identical.

The observer is not.

We often talk about memory as if it were a recording.

Something stored in the brain that we can replay later.

But memory behaves more like a reconstruction.

It keeps proportions badly.

It exaggerates.

It removes boring details.

It preserves strange ones.

The wall grows.

The summer becomes longer.

The street becomes wider.

The old house becomes warmer.

The mulberries become sweeter.

Maybe they really were sweeter.

There is no way to test that now.

Childhood has its own scale.

Distance is different.

Time is different.

A summer holiday seems endless.

A year feels enormous.

Walking three streets away can feel like an expedition.

Adults live in a compressed world.

We know how long things take.

We know what is behind the corner.

We can drive across a city that once seemed impossibly large.

We can reach shelves that used to require a chair.

We can look over walls that once stopped us completely.

Growing up is partly the process of becoming physically capable of things that once required imagination.

That is useful.

But something is lost too.

The wall becomes smaller.

And when the wall becomes smaller, the adventure becomes smaller with it.

As children, we did not need much to create a story.

A tree behind a wall was enough.

A ball landing in somebody else's garden was enough.

A dark basement was enough.

An abandoned building was enough.

A bicycle could create an entire afternoon.

There was no need for an itinerary.

No reservation.

No ticket.

No review score.

Nobody asked whether the experience was worth the time.

You simply went outside.

Things happened.

Maybe this is why returning to childhood places can feel disappointing.

We expect the place to return the feeling.

But places cannot do that.

The wall can still be there.

The tree may still be there.

The street name may be the same.

But the child required to experience them in the old way is gone.

That is not tragedy.

It is simply time doing what time does.

Still, there is a moment of confusion.

You stand in front of the wall and compare two realities.

The physical wall in front of you.

The enormous wall inside your head.

Both are real in different ways.

The smaller one is made of concrete.

The larger one is made of memory.

If someone measured the wall, they would prove that my childhood version was wrong.

But I do not think it was wrong.

For the child trying to reach those mulberries, it really was that high.

Difficulty is part of size.

When something is hard to overcome, it becomes larger.

When we learn how to overcome it, it shrinks.

That happens with more than walls.

Exams.

Problems.

People.

Places.

Fears.

Things that once seemed impossible can look surprisingly ordinary from the other side of time.

Sometimes we wonder why we were ever afraid.

But that is unfair to the person we were.

They were shorter.

They knew less.

They had fewer tools.

They had not yet learned where to put the stones.

Maybe growing up is not discovering that the walls were never tall.

Maybe it is discovering that we eventually became tall enough to see over them.

I went back expecting to find a piece of childhood.

Instead, I found a wall that barely reached my shoulder.

For a few seconds, that felt like a disappointment.

Then I understood.

The wall had done its job.

It had stayed where it was.

I was the one who had moved.
