The modern internet makes publishing incredibly easy. We can create an account, write something, upload a photograph, and reach people within minutes. The platform handles servers, design, discovery, and many technical details.

Convenience is powerful.

It also means the platform owns many of the rules.

A service can change its algorithm, interface, advertising policy, or account limits. A post that was easy to find last year can become invisible. A platform can disappear completely.

This is why I still like personal websites.

A personal site is slower and sometimes more annoying. Links break. Designs become old. Markdown files need maintenance. Nobody automatically delivers every post to thousands of people.

But the files are mine.

The URLs are mine.

The organization can reflect my interests instead of the structure required by a social platform.

I can place a technical article beside a personal memory, a teaching page, a game, and a tiny utility. The mixture does not need to make sense to an engagement algorithm. It only needs to make sense to me.

AI-generated content is making this feeling even stronger. The web is filling with more text than anyone can read. In that environment, I value places where I know there is a person making choices about what belongs there.

A personal website does not guarantee good writing or originality.

It does provide continuity.

After years of changing platforms, owning one small piece of the web still feels surprisingly modern.
