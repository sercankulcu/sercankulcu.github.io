The modern internet has made publishing effortless. Creating an account, writing something, and reaching readers within minutes is genuinely easier than at any point in history. The platform handles servers, design, discovery, and the distribution mechanics that would otherwise require technical expertise most people do not have or want.

Convenience is powerful. It also has costs that become visible slowly, over years, rather than at once.

## The pattern that keeps repeating

Platforms change. This is not speculation — it is what has happened, repeatedly, over the past fifteen years.

Twitter's API policy changed several times over the years, making it harder for third-party clients that many users preferred. In 2022, under new ownership, the platform restructured verification, cut significant portions of the developer platform, and changed content moderation policies substantially — all within months. Users who had built audiences there over a decade found the product they had invested in was substantively different from the one they had used.

Tumblr had a dramatic content policy change in December 2018 that removed a large portion of its community. People who had posted there for years lost access to material they had created, and the communities that had organized there dispersed.

Google Reader was shut down in 2013. RSS was a distributed web standard — no single platform owned it — but Google Reader had become so dominant for reading feeds that shutting it down effectively diminished the ecosystem around RSS for years afterward.

The pattern is not that platforms are malicious. It is that platforms are businesses with their own needs, and those needs are not identical to the user's needs. When they diverge, the platform's needs win. That is what owning the infrastructure means.

## What owning a site actually changes

A personal website does not solve all of this. Hosting providers can close accounts. Domain registrars can suspend domains. Markdown files on a local disk do not reach anyone automatically.

But the failure modes are different, and importantly, the recovery is in your hands.

If my hosting provider goes away, I move my files to another hosting provider. The content still exists. The URLs — if I manage my own domain — can be preserved by pointing to the new host. The writing is not locked in a format that only one company can read.

If a platform goes away, the content may simply be gone, or available only as a bulk export in a format that no other service imports cleanly. Accounts on platforms that close do not transfer.

The IndieWeb movement has been building protocols around this for years — POSSE (Publish on your Own Site, Syndicate Elsewhere) as a workflow, webmentions as a way of connecting sites without a central platform. ActivityPub, now used by Mastodon and other federated platforms, takes the opposite approach from centralized social networks: no single company owns the servers, and moving from one server to another is a designed-in capability rather than an impossible request.

## What I value specifically

The organizational freedom of a personal site is part of it. I can place a technical article beside a teaching page beside a personal memory. The mixture does not need to satisfy an engagement algorithm. There is no recommendation engine deciding which of my posts to show to which readers based on retention metrics.

In 2026, with AI-generated content filling large portions of the web, the signal that a page was maintained by a person making choices about what belongs there matters more than it used to. A personal site does not guarantee quality or originality. It does indicate that a human being decided this content was worth keeping, organized it according to their own sense of what fits together, and continues to maintain it.

That continuity is harder to fake than a convincing paragraph.

The web was designed as a distributed system. No central authority was supposed to own it. Personal websites are the most literal implementation of that original design. After years of consolidation onto a small number of large platforms, owning one small piece of the web still feels like the right instinct.
