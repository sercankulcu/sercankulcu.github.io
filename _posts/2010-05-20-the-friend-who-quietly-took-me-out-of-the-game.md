Some friendships do not end with a fight.

They simply reveal a detail you did not know.

And after that, the friendship may technically continue, but the old version of it is already gone.

Years ago, I wrote about a friend who had begun disappearing even before he officially had a girlfriend.

That was the funny part.

Usually people complain about friends who vanish after entering a relationship.

This one had started early.

He was still in the application phase.

The woman involved was someone I liked.

He knew that.

That detail matters.

One evening, I asked him whether he wanted to come somewhere with me.

He said he was going home.

Reasonable.

People go home.

The evening should have ended there.

Instead, he apparently called her.

She told him that I should come too.

His answer was that I was already going somewhere else.

Technically true.

Conveniently incomplete.

And just like that, I had been removed from a social situation without knowing there had been a social situation to be removed from.

Efficient work.

I did not learn about this immediately.

Of course not.

These things are rarely delivered as official notifications.

Nobody sends a message saying:

"Hello. For your information, your friend is currently attempting to eliminate you from a romantic competition you did not realize had begun."

You discover it accidentally.

That is usually worse.

The woman later asked why I never spent time with them.

I remember the mental reaction better than the actual conversation.

Something suddenly connected.

Why had certain invitations not happened?

Why had some plans seemed strange?

Why was I apparently absent from situations where somebody expected me to be present?

The answer arrived all at once.

There is a special kind of intelligence that appears five minutes too late.

At the moment something happens, you understand nothing.

Later, every previous detail reorganizes itself.

Of course.

That is why.

That is what he was doing.

The past suddenly becomes much easier to read.

Unfortunately, the past is not editable.

My first reaction was anger.

That is natural.

The friend knew I liked her.

He also liked her.

Instead of saying so, he had apparently decided to compete quietly.

There is something insulting about discovering that someone close to you has been making strategic decisions while you thought everybody was simply spending time together.

You feel stupid.

Not only hurt.

Stupid.

And feeling stupid often produces more anger than the actual loss.

The romantic part was almost secondary.

What bothered me was the secrecy.

If two friends like the same person, the situation is uncomfortable but not impossible.

Neither person owns her.

She is not a prize.

She decides whom she likes.

That part should be obvious.

But friendship creates another expectation:

Do not manipulate the board without telling me there is a game.

At least acknowledge what is happening.

Say:

"I like her too."

That one sentence changes everything.

Now the problem is visible.

Awkward, yes.

But honest.

Instead, the secret version turns ordinary events into tactics.

Who gets invited?

Who gets left out?

Who calls first?

Who sits next to whom?

Who tells whom what?

Friendship starts behaving like game theory.

Everyone has incomplete information.

Everyone has incentives.

Nobody wants to reveal strategy.

The outcome may be rational.

The experience is terrible.

At the time, I described the realization with the emotional sophistication you would expect from someone angry on the internet.

Something like lightning in the head.

Or perhaps a poisoned crocodile thrashing around.

I was not exactly aiming for restraint.

Then, while writing, I had another thought.

Maybe he was doing the correct thing.

At least he was doing it behind my back.

What would have been better?

Doing it directly in front of me?

There was the joke.

And underneath it, an uncomfortable truth.

Sometimes we complain about the method because the result itself is painful.

Would honesty have made me happy?

Probably not.

If he had said:

"I know you like her, but I am going to try too."

I would have respected the honesty.

I would still not have enjoyed the situation.

There was no version in which everybody felt wonderful.

That is worth remembering.

Not every conflict has a clean solution.

Sometimes all honesty can do is prevent an additional wound.

It cannot remove the original one.

The original problem was simple:

Two friends were interested in the same person.

That happens.

Nobody planned it.

Nobody needed permission for the feeling.

But what happens next reveals character.

Competition changes people.

It can make otherwise decent people strangely tactical.

Friendship is cooperative until incentives stop aligning.

Then small behaviors suddenly become meaningful.

A missing invitation.

A selective truth.

A phone call made after saying you were going home.

Nothing dramatic by itself.

Together, they tell a story.

The most interesting part, looking back, is how quickly friendship and romance can contaminate each other.

You may lose neither person.

You may lose both.

Or nothing visible may happen at all, while trust quietly becomes smaller.

Trust is difficult because it does not usually disappear completely.

It loses resolution.

Before, you assume good intent automatically.

Afterward, you begin checking.

Why did he say that?

Why was I not invited?

Did he tell me everything?

Once checking becomes necessary, the relationship has already changed.

I do not think this means friends should never be attracted to the same person.

That would require an impossible level of coordination.

Feelings are not assigned by committee.

But secrecy creates unnecessary damage.

If the friendship matters, uncomfortable conversations are cheaper than hidden strategy.

This is easier to say years later.

At the time, I was not interested in lessons.

I was interested in the fact that my friend had apparently attempted to remove me from the field while smiling normally.

There was also something almost admirable in the efficiency.

I invited him somewhere.

He declined.

Then he called her.

She suggested including me.

He explained that I had other plans.

Clean.

Minimal.

No wasted movement.

If this had been an algorithm, I might have appreciated it.

Unfortunately, I was one of the inputs.

Maybe this is why engineers sometimes understand emotional situations too late.

We assume the system is behaving according to the visible specification.

Then we discover an undocumented process running in the background.

Debugging human beings is much harder than debugging code.

They do not provide logs.

And when they do, the logs are biased.

Eventually, I wrote a joke at the end of that old entry.

My only friends were my drink and my cigarette.

At least they would not leave me unless I ran out of money.

It was melodramatic.

It was also very appropriate for the era.

A wounded young man.

A friend behaving badly.

A woman somewhere in the middle of it.

A cigarette.

A drink.

An internet entry written as if civilization itself had collapsed.

Nothing had collapsed.

That is another thing age teaches.

Events that once seemed enormous become stories.

The betrayal does not disappear.

Its scale changes.

You begin to see everybody as younger, more insecure, more competitive, more confused.

Including yourself.

Maybe especially yourself.

I no longer think the useful question is:

Who won?

The better question is:

What kind of friend was I when friendship became inconvenient?

What kind of friend was he?

Romantic opportunities come and go.

Trust between close friends is harder to replace.

And if you decide to compete, perhaps the least you owe each other is the truth.

Otherwise one person may discover the game only after being taken off the field.
