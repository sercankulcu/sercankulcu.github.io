Two days ago, Apple began selling the iPhone in the United States. I have been reading everything written about it and trying to understand whether this is a genuinely different kind of device or only a well-designed version of things that already existed.

I think it might be genuinely different, and I want to explain why.

## What phones looked like before this

Nokia held roughly 40% of the global mobile phone market in 2007 and its Symbian platform was the dominant smartphone operating system. Symbian ran on millions of devices and supported third-party applications, but the experience of browsing the web on them was deeply limited — WAP pages were stripped-down versions of websites that looked nothing like the real thing. Windows Mobile 6.0, which shipped on devices from HTC, Samsung, and Motorola, resembled a shrunken Windows desktop with stylus-driven menus designed for a different screen size. BlackBerry was excellent at corporate email and had real keyboards, but web browsing was slow and painful.

The input problem shaped everything. Small physical keyboards required multi-tap or predictive text that most people tolerated rather than enjoyed. Touchscreens existed — Palm had used them since the 1990s — but they required a stylus and produced interfaces designed around tapping rather than touching.

## What Apple announced in January and shipped in June

On January 9, 2007, Steve Jobs introduced the iPhone at Macworld Expo. He described it as "three products in one": a widescreen iPod, a mobile phone, and an internet communicator. The third part is the one I find most significant.

The device that went on sale June 29 runs Apple's modified version of OS X on a Samsung-manufactured ARM11 processor at 412 MHz with 128 MB of RAM. The screen is a 3.5-inch multi-touch display at 320 × 480 pixels. Two price points: $499 for 4 GB and $599 for 8 GB, both requiring a two-year AT&T contract in the United States. The camera is 2 megapixels, fixed focus. There is no 3G network support — it uses AT&T's EDGE network, which means web browsing over cellular is slow, often 100–200 Kbps.

The iPhone ships with a full version of Safari. Not a mobile-adapted browser — the actual browser, able to render regular web pages. The screen is large enough and the pinch-to-zoom is smooth enough that this actually works. Multi-touch allows two-finger gestures that no stylus-based interface can replicate.

## Why this might matter beyond the device itself

The practical consequence is that carrying an iPhone means carrying something that can access the real internet, not a mobile-adapted version of it. That changes what "having internet access" means.

Until now, checking information online away from a desk required either a laptop or accepting a degraded mobile version of the web. If the iPhone works as described, the full web starts to become something that fits in a pocket — even if the current EDGE connection is slow.

Apple has stated that third-party applications will not be allowed to run natively; developers are expected to build web applications. I think this is temporary — the pressure to allow native apps will grow as the platform becomes popular — but it limits what the phone can do in its current form.

## What I am uncertain about

The competitors are not standing still. Nokia is working on a new platform. Google has been assembling what will become Android. Microsoft will not ignore a successful new smartphone category. The interesting question is not whether Apple wins this specific product announcement, but whether the category of "phone that is also a real computer" becomes the dominant form — and how quickly.

I think it will. A phone is already the device that is always with us. If it can also do what a computer does, the question of when to carry a separate computer starts to look different. The phone is becoming a computer — and two days ago, one version of that future went on sale.
