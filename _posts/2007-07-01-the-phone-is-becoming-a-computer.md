Two days ago, Apple began selling the iPhone in the United States. I have been reading everything written about it and trying to understand whether this is a genuinely different kind of device or only a well-designed version of things that already existed.

I think it might be genuinely different, and I want to explain why.

## What phones looked like before this

Mobile phones have been trying to become computers for years. Nokia's Symbian platform runs on millions of devices and supports third-party applications. Windows Mobile 6.0 looks like a shrunken version of a Windows desktop. BlackBerry devices run email reliably and have real keyboards. Sony Ericsson and Motorola have their own approaches.

All of these devices do more than make calls. But using the web on most of them is a genuinely bad experience. WAP pages — the mobile-adapted versions of websites — look nothing like the real thing. Where a full browser exists, pages designed for a 1024-pixel-wide screen become impossible to navigate on a small display with a stylus or a five-way pad.

The input problem is real too. Physical keyboards on small devices require learning a system of multi-tap or predictive text that most people tolerate rather than enjoy. Touchscreens existed before the iPhone, but they required a stylus and were frustrating to use.

## What Apple's approach changes

Steve Jobs described the iPhone in January as "three products in one": a widescreen iPod, a mobile phone, and an internet communicator. The third part is the one I find most significant.

The iPhone ships with a full version of Safari. Not a mobile-adapted browser — the actual browser, able to render regular web pages. The screen is large enough and the zooming is smooth enough that this actually works. A person can visit a website that was designed for a desktop computer and read it on a phone.

The touch interface removes the stylus. The keyboard appears on screen when needed and disappears when it is not. This is a real trade-off — a physical keyboard gives tactile feedback that a glass surface cannot — but it means the entire front of the device can be a screen when you are not typing.

## Why this matters beyond the device itself

The practical consequence is that carrying an iPhone means carrying something that can access the real internet, not a mobile-adapted version of it. That changes what "having internet access" means.

Until now, checking information online required finding a computer. Mobile devices offered a limited version of this — checking email, maybe a stripped-down news page — but not the full experience. If the iPhone works as described, the full experience starts to become something that fits in a pocket.

I am curious about what happens to software when this becomes normal. Applications designed for a phone have always had to work around screen size, input limitations, and slow connections. A device with a large touch screen and a real browser changes which constraints matter most. The interesting software questions will shift.

## What I am uncertain about

The iPhone is expensive. It requires a contract with AT&T in the United States. It does not yet support third-party applications — Apple says a web-based approach is the right answer for now, which I think is temporary. The camera is two megapixels. Mobile internet in most of the world is slow enough that a full web browser sometimes makes things worse, not better.

And the competitors are not standing still. Nokia, Microsoft, and others will respond. The interesting question is not whether Apple wins this specific product announcement, but whether the category of "phone that is also a real computer" becomes normal.

I think it will. A phone is already the device that is always with us. If it can also do what a computer does, the question of when to carry a separate computer starts to look different. The phone is becoming a computer — and two days ago, one version of that future went on sale.
