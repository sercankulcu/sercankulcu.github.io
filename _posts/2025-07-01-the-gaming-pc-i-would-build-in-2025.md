If I were building a gaming PC on **July 1, 2025**, this is the machine I would actually want. I am not simply choosing the most expensive part in every category. The priority is gaming performance, then reliability, cooling and enough headroom to keep the system useful for years.

### The build

- **CPU:** AMD Ryzen 7 9800X3D
- **GPU:** NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 32 GB
- **Motherboard:** MSI MEG X870E ACE
- **Memory:** 64 GB DDR5-6000 CL30
- **Storage:** Samsung 9100 Pro 4 TB PCIe 5.0 NVMe
- **Power supply:** Corsair HX1200i
- **Case:** Fractal Design Torrent
- **CPU cooling:** Arctic Liquid Freezer III 360
- **Gaming target:** 4K ultra/high-refresh with heavy ray tracing

### Why this CPU?

AMD launched the 9800X3D at $479 and claimed about an 8% average gaming uplift over the 7800X3D. Its eight Zen 5 cores and 104 MB total cache make more sense to me for gaming than paying for a 16-core CPU.

### Why this GPU?

The RTX 5090 became available January 30 starting at $1,999, with 32 GB of GDDR7. It is the obvious place to spend an extreme gaming budget, especially for ray tracing, high-resolution textures and AI-assisted rendering.

### What I would pay

A complete build would be roughly **$3,800–4,500 at normal retail pricing** in the U.S. market of the period. This is an approximate enthusiast-system total, not a claim that every retailer sold every part at MSRP on July 1.

### The weak point

Power and cost are again uncomfortable, and real retail pricing can be far above MSRP. The 64 GB of system RAM is intentionally generous rather than necessary for most games.

### My verdict

The 2025 philosophy is simple: buy the gaming-specialized X3D CPU and put the serious money into the 5090. I would not dilute the budget with a workstation processor.

The date matters. I am judging only hardware that was available by July 1, not products announced or released later in the year. That makes each build a snapshot of what I could realistically have assembled at that moment.
