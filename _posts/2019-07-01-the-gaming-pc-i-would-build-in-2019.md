If I were building a gaming PC on **July 1, 2019**, this is the machine I would actually want. I am not simply choosing the most expensive part in every category. The priority is gaming performance, then reliability, cooling and enough headroom to keep the system useful for years.

### The build

- **CPU:** Intel Core i9-9900K
- **GPU:** NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti 11 GB
- **Motherboard:** Gigabyte Z390 AORUS Master
- **Memory:** 32 GB DDR4-3200
- **Storage:** Samsung 970 EVO Plus 1 TB NVMe + 2 TB SATA SSD
- **Power supply:** Corsair RM850x
- **Case:** Lian Li PC-O11 Dynamic
- **CPU cooling:** Corsair H150i Pro
- **Gaming target:** 4K/60 with ray tracing where practical; very high-refresh 1440p

### Why this CPU?

Ryzen 3000 would launch on July 7, not July 1. I am freezing the build to the requested date, so the 9900K remains the safe high-end gaming choice: eight fast cores and sixteen threads.

### Why this GPU?

The RTX 2080 Ti was the $999-class Turing flagship, with the Founders Edition at $1,199. It was brutally expensive, but it was also the only GeForce that made early real-time ray tracing and uncompromised 4K feel remotely plausible.

### What I would pay

A complete build would be roughly **$2,700–3,100** in the U.S. market of the period. This is an approximate enthusiast-system total, not a claim that every retailer sold every part at MSRP on July 1.

### The weak point

Price is the weakness. The performance gain over cheaper cards does not scale with the cost, and early ray tracing often needs DLSS or reduced settings.

### My verdict

This is the first build where I knowingly pay a large early-adopter tax. I do it because 4K and ray tracing are exactly the technologies I would want to experiment with.

The date matters. I am judging only hardware that was available by July 1, not products announced or released later in the year. That makes each build a snapshot of what I could realistically have assembled at that moment.
