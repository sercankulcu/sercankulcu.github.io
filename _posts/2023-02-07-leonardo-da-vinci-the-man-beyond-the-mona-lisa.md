When people hear Leonardo da Vinci, the Mona Lisa usually appears first.

That is understandable.

But the painting is only one part of a much larger life.

Leonardo was born in 1452 in Vinci, in present-day Italy. He trained in the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio in Florence and developed as a painter, draftsman, engineer, observer, and experimenter.

His notebooks are the part I find most fascinating.

They contain studies of anatomy, water, mechanics, flight, geometry, architecture, and machines.

Leonardo drew muscles, bones, gears, wings, bridges, and weapons with the same curiosity.

Many of these ideas were never built.

Some were not practical with the materials and technology of his time.

Still, the drawings reveal a mind that did not respect modern boundaries between art and engineering.

For Leonardo, seeing carefully was a form of thinking.

Anatomical drawings improved because he wanted to understand the body.

Painting improved because he studied light, perspective, and movement.

Mechanical ideas grew from observing forces in nature.

This connection between disciplines is what makes him feel modern.

Today, education often divides knowledge into departments.

Leonardo reminds me that a difficult problem may not care which department owns it.

Curiosity can move across borders more easily than institutions do.

The Mona Lisa is famous.

The habit of looking closely may be Leonardo's more useful legacy.
