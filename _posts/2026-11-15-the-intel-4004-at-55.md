A computer that fits on one small chip sounds ordinary today. In 1971 it was a bold claim, and some engineers did not believe it. Stanley Mazor, one of the chip's architects, later recalled that many people thought the advertisement described only an arithmetic unit, not a real computer. Fifty-five years ago, on 15 November 1971, Intel introduced the 4004 anyway. It was a complete 4-bit central processing unit on a single piece of silicon, and it began the story of the microprocessor as a product anyone could buy.

## A calculator started it

The 4004 did not begin as a grand plan for personal computing. It began as a customer order. In 1969 the Japanese calculator company Busicom (called Nippon Calculating Machine Corporation in Intel's 2021 account) asked Intel to design a set of custom chips for a new printing calculator, the Busicom 141-PF. The original request was for twelve chips.

Intel was a young company then, only founded in 1968, and it earned its living from memory chips. It did not have the design capacity to build twelve different custom circuits. Ted Hoff, who had joined Intel from Stanford, proposed a different approach: instead of hard-wiring the calculator's logic into many special chips, build a small general-purpose computer on a few chips and let a program define what the calculator does. The result was a family of four chips, the MCS-4 set, with the 4004 processor at its centre.

This is the idea worth remembering. Moving behaviour out of silicon and into software is what makes one design reusable. The same chip could be a calculator today and something else tomorrow, only by changing the program.

## The four people behind the chip

Four names appear in almost every account. Ted Hoff proposed the general-purpose architecture. Stanley Mazor worked with Hoff on the specification. Masatoshi Shima, an engineer from Busicom, checked the logic against the calculator's needs and was assigned in 1970 to help with the chip design. Federico Faggin, who joined Intel in April 1970, led the physical design and project, and even put his initials on the CPU layout.

The detail that deserves attention is that this was a team effort across two companies and two countries. The idea, the logic design, and the silicon craft were different skills, and the chip needed all of them. Faggin adapted Intel's silicon-gate MOS memory technology so that a whole processor could fit on a die, which was the hard engineering step that turned a good architecture into a working part. The first fully operational 4004 was delivered to Busicom in March 1971, and Intel advertised the chip set publicly in November of that year.

## What the specifications really were

The numbers are small by modern standards, and that is exactly why they are instructive.

- **Word size:** 4 bits. The instruction set was oriented to binary-coded decimal, which suits a calculator.
- **Transistors:** about 2,300.
- **Process:** p-channel silicon-gate MOS with a 10-micrometre minimum line width, on 2-inch wafers, with a die of about 12 square millimetres.
- **Clock:** roughly 740 kHz. Intel's own anniversary materials give 740 kHz in one document and 750 kHz in another, so treat it as "about three-quarters of a megahertz."
- **Instructions:** 45, according to the data sheet that Mazor read aloud in a Computer History Museum oral history. Other sources count 46, so the exact figure depends on how the data sheet counts.
- **Package:** a 16-pin dual in-line package.

For comparison, Intel's 2021 anniversary comparison lists a 12th-generation Core processor with billions of transistors, a 64-bit instruction set and clock speeds up to 5.2 GHz. Yet the 4004 already contained the parts every processor still has: a program counter, an arithmetic logic unit, registers, and control logic that decodes instructions.

The chip also depended on its companions. The MCS-4 family put the program ROM, data RAM, and input and output into separate support chips, so the processor was never alone. That is a useful lesson for students: a CPU is a component in a system, not the system itself.

## From the 4004 to the 8086

The 4004 did not make Intel rich overnight. The Computer History Museum notes that sales of the chip set were modest at first, but the project launched Intel into a profitable new business. Intel later regained the rights to sell the chips for non-calculator uses, and Busicom itself went bankrupt in 1974.

The next steps came quickly:

1. **8008 (1972).** An 8-bit processor that Intel had contracted to build for Computer Terminal Corporation, designed by Hal Feeney under Faggin's supervision and announced in 1972.
2. **8080 (1974).** A second-generation 8-bit design that helped establish that microprocessors were here to stay.
3. **8086 (June 1978).** Intel's first 16-bit microprocessor, with 29,000 transistors by Intel's count and a 5 MHz initial clock. It was also the first x86 processor.

The 8086's cousin, the 8088, powered the first IBM PC. That connects a Japanese calculator order in 1969 to the machine on many desks in the 1980s, and, through the x86 line, to the computers still being built now. The Computer History Museum describes the x86 family as the most successful in history, although it is careful to say that the origin of the 8008 itself is disputed.

## Why it still matters in the classroom

The 8086 is a practical starting point for learning how a processor works, because its registers, memory addressing, and instruction cycle can be understood by one person. Our [Microprocessors course](/teaching/microprocessors/?lang=en) teaches exactly that, and the 4004 is the earlier chapter of the same story. When students step through an 8086 program and watch the registers change, they are using ideas that the 4004 team put on silicon in 1971: fetch an instruction, decode it, execute it, and repeat.

There is also a quieter lesson about how technology changes. Nobody set out to start the personal computer era with this chip. A calculator customer, a clever reduction in chip count, and a team willing to treat a general-purpose design as better than a custom one led there by accident. Good engineering often opens doors that its own designers did not plan to open.

Fifty-five years later, the 4004 is a reminder that even a modest design, built with care and flexible enough to be reprogrammed, can outlast the product it was made for.
