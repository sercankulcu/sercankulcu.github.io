## Teoman ile Söyleşi

**2006**  
**Interview by Coşkun Çokyiğit**

This interview finds Teoman in a more established stage of his career.

The central question is no longer whether he can become successful, or whether popularity will change him.

By 2006, those questions have largely been answered.

Instead, the conversation moves toward something more difficult:

**What exactly is Teoman trying to do as an artist?**

The answer involves originality, commercial art, language, cultural identity, Istanbul and the existence of an audience.

### Songs as Personal Property

The interview begins with accusations that Teoman had copied songs.

His response is unusually forceful.

He insists that his compositions are his own and compares them to children whose parentage can be documented.

The metaphor is revealing.

A song is not merely a product.

It carries authorship.

For Teoman, authorship appears almost biological: the song comes from somewhere specific, and that origin matters.

This strong concern with ownership fits naturally with the artistic control he had already emphasized in his earliest interviews.

### Songs Arrive Quickly

Teoman describes a creative process that is very different from the painstaking method often associated with serious songwriting.

He says that many songs arrive quickly.

Words and melody may appear within minutes.

If a song requires too much effort, he may abandon it rather than force it into existence.

This represents an interesting development from the *Onyedi* period.

Earlier, Teoman talked about collecting phrases and thinking carefully about the structure of an album.

Here he places much greater emphasis on immediacy.

The preparation may exist for years.

But the actual song should feel as if it has arrived naturally.

### Creation and Effort

This suggests a distinction between **preparation** and **construction**.

Teoman reads.

Observes.

Stores language.

Lives.

Listens.

All of this becomes material.

But when the song finally arrives, he does not necessarily want the process to feel mechanical.

The accumulated material should suddenly organize itself.

If too much visible engineering is required, something may already be wrong.

### Everything Begins with the Self

When asked whether his songs summarize his own experiences, Teoman gives a broader answer.

Whatever a person creates, he suggests, ultimately begins with the self.

This does not mean every song is literal autobiography.

The *Gönülçelen* interview had already shown how Teoman transforms personal material into fictional characters such as Ruhi.

The deeper point is that fiction still requires a starting position.

The artist cannot completely leave himself behind.

### Is Popular Music Art?

One of the strongest sections of the interview concerns the word **art**.

Teoman questions whether popular music can easily fit classical definitions of art.

The problem is commerce.

Modern music operates through record companies, concerts, sales, promotion and an industry surrounding creative work.

This complicates romantic ideas of the artist creating with complete indifference to audience or market.

Teoman does not try to escape this contradiction.

He accepts that what he does belongs, at least partly, to **commercial art**.

### The Audience Matters

The most revealing statement is simple:

> “Kimse dinlemeyecek olsa şarkı yapmazdım.”

This is one of the most important Teoman statements in the archive.

He does not claim that he would create songs in complete isolation simply because an inner artistic necessity demanded it.

An audience matters.

The possibility of being heard is part of the motivation.

This is unusually honest because artists are often expected to say the opposite.

The romantic ideal suggests that the “real artist” creates even if nobody listens.

Teoman refuses that performance of purity.

### Art Requires Another Person

This statement changes the relationship between artist and audience.

The listener is not merely the final consumer of a completed product.

The imagined listener is part of the reason the work exists at all.

For Teoman, art appears to be partly communicative.

A song without the possibility of reaching another person loses something fundamental.

This position makes his earlier acceptance of popularity easier to understand.

A larger audience is not necessarily contamination.

Communication was always part of the purpose.

### Commercial Without Shame

Teoman's use of the term commercial art is therefore not self-condemnation.

It is an attempt at accuracy.

He is not Van Gogh working without recognition and selling nothing.

He belongs to an industrial system.

He sells records.

People buy concert tickets.

Media visibility matters.

Yet none of this automatically makes the work meaningless.

The contradiction is simply part of modern popular art.

### Why Lyrics Matter

Teoman gives one of his clearest explanations of why lyrics are so important to him.

He recognizes that many musicians are technically superior to him.

Some can communicate enormous emotional complexity through music without any words.

Teoman does not see himself primarily in that tradition.

His particular contribution depends heavily on language.

Without songwriting, he says, he might not make music at all.

This is crucial.

Music is not simply the container into which Teoman places lyrics.

The combination of language and music is the artistic territory he considers his own.

### Finding His Own Place

Asked what he is trying to achieve, Teoman gives a concise answer:

> “Kendim oluşturduğum bir yere varmaya çalışıyorum.”

This may be one of the best summaries of his artistic philosophy.

He does not claim to invent music from nothing.

His musical methods come from existing traditions.

His influences are visible.

What matters is whether these borrowed tools eventually produce a space recognizable as his own.

Originality, in this sense, does not mean having no influences.

It means arranging influences until they become identity.

### Istanbul as the Source

Teoman explicitly describes Istanbul as the source of his compositions.

He knows the life of an urban middle-class family because that is the world from which he comes.

He does not pretend to possess intimate knowledge of village life or every region of Anatolia.

This restraint is significant.

Authenticity requires knowing the limits of one's experience.

He does not need to represent all of Turkey.

He can represent the Turkey he actually knows.

### The Middle-Class Urban World

This helps explain the geography of Teoman's songs.

Apartments.

Bars.

Beyoğlu.

Bedrooms.

Streets.

Relationships.

Alcohol.

Urban loneliness.

These are not randomly chosen images.

They emerge from the social environment Teoman understands from inside.

The realism of his music depends partly on this limited geography.

### Western Methods, Turkish Identity

Perhaps the most interesting cultural statement in the interview is Teoman's distinction between **method** and **identity**.

He says that he grew up heavily influenced by Western culture.

The methods he uses come substantially from there.

But he feels completely connected to Turkish culture.

This avoids a simplistic East-versus-West opposition.

Technique can travel.

Cultural belonging can remain local.

A guitar, a rock arrangement or a Western narrative structure does not automatically determine cultural identity.

### Turkish

When asked why he loves Istanbul and Turkey, Teoman eventually reduces the answer to something remarkably basic:

**Turkish.**

Language is the strongest form of belonging.

This connects directly with his emphasis on lyrics.

Teoman's relationship with Turkey is not simply geographical or political.

It is linguistic.

The language in which he thinks, jokes, writes and communicates forms one of the deepest foundations of his identity.

### Istanbul as Home

Teoman says he enjoys cities such as New York and London.

But Istanbul remains the place where he feels most himself.

He was born there.

He grew up there.

He built his career there.

And he assumes that it will probably also be the city where he dies.

This is stronger than ordinary affection for a hometown.

Istanbul functions as part of his self-definition.

### A National Audience He Never Planned

By 2006, Teoman has listeners across Turkey.

He describes traveling through different regions and encountering people who buy his albums despite living thousands of kilometers away from his own social world.

He enjoys this greatly.

But he insists that he never began his career by calculating how to appeal to all of them.

This repeats the distinction first developed around 2000:

**large audiences are welcome as an outcome, but dangerous as a creative instruction.**

### No Universal Artist

Teoman also rejects the idea that a single musician should represent every cultural possibility.

Orhan Gencebay does one thing.

Müslüm Gürses does another.

Teoman does something else.

The total culture emerges from all of them together.

This is a surprisingly pluralistic view.

An artist does not have to contain the whole society.

Perhaps no artist can.

### Limits as Identity

This idea gives artistic limitation a positive meaning.

Knowing what you cannot represent helps define what you can.

Teoman does not need to manufacture Anatolian authenticity because it would make his work broader.

His limitation to urban Istanbul life is not necessarily a weakness.

It is part of what makes the work identifiable.

### Why This Interview Matters

This interview is especially important because Teoman gives unusually direct answers to several fundamental questions.

Why write songs?

Because someone will hear them.

Why are lyrics important?

Because language is central to what he contributes.

Where does the material come from?

From himself and from the Istanbul life he knows.

Is popular music pure art?

Probably not in the classical romantic sense.

Is that a reason to stop making it?

No.

What is he trying to create?

A place that belongs specifically to him.

Several themes from the previous decade now connect:

- artistic control,
- authenticity,
- audience,
- popularity,
- language,
- Istanbul,
- cultural belonging,
- and the difference between influence and identity.

The young Teoman once worried that popularity might weaken artistic intensity.

The older Teoman has reached a more complicated position.

He needs listeners.

He accepts commerce.

He accepts influence.

He accepts limitation.

But within all of those constraints, he still wants one thing:

**to arrive somewhere that could only have been made by Teoman.**
