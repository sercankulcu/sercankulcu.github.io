## Empati — Teoman

**NTV — January 2026**  
**Interview by Ahmet Mümtaz Taylan**

This is one of the most biographically revealing conversations in the Teoman archive.

Many of its subjects are familiar.

His father's early death.

Growing up among women.

Melancholy.

Difficult relationships.

Fatherhood.

Aging.

But *Empati* produces something different.

Ahmet Mümtaz Taylan repeatedly brings Teoman back from the constructed rock-star character to the psychological structures underneath it.

The result is less about **what Teoman did** than about **why Teoman became Teoman**.

### The Father Who Was Not There

Teoman again begins from the central absence of his childhood.

His father died when Teoman was approximately two years old.

That meant he did not grow up knowing an adult male figure closely.

He was raised primarily by:

his mother,

his aunt,

and his grandmother.

This fact has appeared in earlier interviews.

But here Teoman describes its psychological consequence more directly.

### No Male Model

He says he did not really know what a male role model was supposed to be.

Instead, he found substitutes in fiction and popular culture.

One of them was **Zagor**.

Another was **Elvis Presley**.

The combination is almost perfect for understanding the child who later became Teoman.

Zagor:

strength,

adventure,

male confidence.

Elvis:

music,

sexual charisma,

fame,

stage presence.

The absent father is partly replaced by imaginary men.

### Masculinity Learned from Fiction

This gives a new dimension to Teoman's later public masculinity.

The rock-star character may not simply have developed naturally from adult confidence.

Some of it may have been consciously assembled.

The shy child watches powerful men in stories and performance.

Then eventually constructs a public figure capable of embodying some of those qualities.

### Growing Up With Three Women

Teoman also emphasizes that being raised by three women affected him positively.

He believes he learned a certain sensitivity and understanding of women from that environment.

This connects strongly with his 2007 Ayşe Arman interview, where he described his mother, aunt and grandmother as highly formative figures.

The important point is that his masculinity did not develop in a conventionally male domestic environment.

It emerged largely through women.

### Fear

But Teoman also associates the missing father with insecurity.

A father, in the child's imagination, represents protection.

Without that figure, he remembers himself as more fearful and vulnerable.

He describes the result as a kind of underlying restlessness.

This makes the famous later Teoman sentence —

**“There is a restlessness inside me that will never disappear.”**

— look less like an abstract personality trait.

He now gives it a childhood origin.

### His Mother

The conversation contains especially important material about Teoman's relationship with his mother.

He says she did not approve of his becoming a singer.

For years, she was unhappy about the profession he chose.

In Teoman's account, she wanted something more conventionally respectable for him.

### “My Mother Was Ashamed”

Teoman even says his mother was ashamed that he had become a singer.

The statement attracted considerable media attention because it reverses the public perspective.

To millions of listeners, becoming Teoman looks like extraordinary success.

Inside the family, the same career could initially appear as disappointment.

Success is therefore dependent on the observer's value system.

### Social Status and Music

This also tells us something about the social environment in which Teoman grew up.

Music was not necessarily imagined as a prestigious professional destination.

Academic or conventional professional achievement carried another kind of status.

This makes Teoman's early career choice more rebellious than it may look retrospectively.

Today the outcome seems inevitable.

At the beginning, it was not.

### Becoming Elvis

Teoman says that around twelve or thirteen he had decided he wanted to become a rock star like Elvis Presley.

This is valuable biographical evidence because it moves the origin of the ambition much earlier than his university bands.

The dream existed before the professional opportunity.

### The Childhood Fantasy Actually Happened

That creates one of the strangest structures in Teoman's biography.

A fatherless child creates imaginary male figures.

One of those figures is the charismatic rock star.

Then the child grows up and actually becomes one.

The fantasy succeeds.

But, as the rest of the archive demonstrates, becoming the fantasy does not repair everything that produced it.

### Success Cannot Rewrite Childhood

This may explain why professional success repeatedly failed to produce complete satisfaction.

Fame can confirm the adult.

It cannot retroactively give security to the child.

The public can love Teoman.

That does not create the father Teoman never knew.

The career solves a different problem.

### What Came From His Mother

Teoman says that some characteristics he disliked in his mother eventually appeared in him.

Most importantly:

depressive tendencies,

melancholy,

irritability.

This is one of his clearest statements about what he sees as inherited or learned emotional patterns.

### Self-Observation

Teoman describes himself as someone who continuously examines his own emotions.

This is perhaps the defining psychological method of his adult life.

He does not simply feel anger.

He watches himself becoming angry.

He does not simply become depressed.

He analyzes the depression.

He observes Teoman almost as another person.

### The Internal Supervisor

This creates an interesting psychological structure.

There is:

the person feeling,

and another internal person watching.

The watcher tries to control the first one.

This helps explain why Teoman can appear highly self-aware while still describing impulsive behavior throughout his biography.

Self-awareness is not automatic self-control.

It creates the possibility of control.

### Waking Up Angry

Teoman gives a concrete example.

Sometimes he wakes up angry without an obvious reason.

He knows that if he immediately enters the outside world in that state, he may create conflict.

So he stops himself.

He waits.

He tries to become calmer before interacting with people.

This is an older Teoman actively managing the temperament that younger Teoman often allowed to operate more freely.

### “Arızalık Yapacağım”

He effectively warns himself:

if you go outside like this, you are going to cause trouble.

The remarkable thing is not that anger still exists.

It is that Teoman has stopped treating uncontrolled behavior as authenticity.

### Authenticity Versus Self-Control

This represents a significant philosophical change.

Youthful rock mythology can treat impulse as truth.

Do what you feel.

Reject restraint.

Do not become domesticated.

The older Teoman has a different conclusion.

A feeling can be authentic and still be harmful.

Self-control does not necessarily make the person false.

Sometimes it makes him tolerable.

### Shame After Anger

Teoman says he feels ashamed after behaving badly.

That shame matters because it creates motivation for control.

The later project is therefore not:

**become naturally calm.**

It is:

**recognize the pattern early enough to prevent the damage.**

This is much more realistic.

### He Is Trying to Become Calm

Teoman says he is actively trying to turn himself into a calmer person.

This phrasing is important.

Calmness is not described as his natural personality.

It is a project.

Like songwriting.

Like exercise.

Like writing.

The later Teoman increasingly treats personality itself as something that can be edited.

### Fear of Becoming an Angry Old Man

Age gives this project urgency.

Teoman worries about becoming a bitter, difficult old man.

That fear is particularly revealing because he has spent decades discussing artistic caricature.

Now there is another caricature to avoid:

the permanently irritated aging man.

### Aging as Character Risk

Earlier interviews focused on whether an aging rock star would look ridiculous.

The 2026 Teoman is thinking about something more fundamental.

What kind of human being will he become?

The danger is no longer merely aesthetic.

It is moral and interpersonal.

### Sleep

The program also contains important discussion of Teoman's long-term sleep problems.

He says he has had difficulties with sleep since childhood.

For years, the problem interacted badly with his lifestyle.

Nightlife and heavy alcohol use could intensify rather than solve it.

### Alcohol

Teoman now describes his earlier alcohol use with much greater distance.

The young rock-star persona made drinking appear almost culturally natural.

The older Teoman sees it partly as a psychological and physical problem.

He concludes that continuing in the same way would damage his mental health.

### Healthy Living

This leads to one of the clearest transformations in the entire archive.

Teoman says he is pushing himself toward a healthier lifestyle.

He jokes about the contrast:

the man known for years as a reckless figure is now trying to live properly.

The statement is funny because the audience understands both Teomans.

### The End of the “Serseri”

But the change is not simply image management.

Teoman links it directly to psychological survival.

If he does not look after his body, sleep and habits, he believes his mental state will worsen.

Health becomes part of emotional management.

### Discipline Again

This returns us to a contradiction visible throughout late Teoman.

The public image says:

careless,

lazy,

undisciplined,

rock star.

The actual later routine increasingly includes:

exercise,

health management,

self-monitoring,

controlled work,

and deliberate reduction of harmful habits.

The character and the operating system have separated.

### Fatherhood

One of the most emotionally significant sections concerns his daughter.

Teoman admits that he has not become exactly the father he once imagined he would become.

That is especially important because fatherhood had been present in his imagination since the 1999 interviews.

### The 1999 Dream

At thirty-two, Teoman had imagined having a child as one of the most meaningful things that could happen later in life.

He pictured loving someone moving upward while he himself moved toward old age.

That was the theory.

By 2026, he can compare the theory with reality.

### “I Couldn't Become the Father I Imagined”

Teoman is self-critical.

The imagined future father was easier to construct than the real one.

Real fatherhood requires:

time,

presence,

patience,

consistency,

responsibility,

and emotional availability.

These are harder than philosophical ideas about parenthood.

### Apology

In an official *Empati* video excerpt, Teoman says he apologizes to his daughter for not being the father he once imagined he would be. The statement is one of the strongest corrections to the romantic interpretation of fatherhood in the earlier archive.

Fatherhood provided meaning.

It did not magically make Teoman perfect.

### The Missing Father Becomes the Father

This creates perhaps the deepest biographical circle in the interview.

Teoman begins life without a father.

He spends adulthood thinking about what a father might mean.

Eventually he becomes one.

Then he discovers that knowing the importance of fatherhood does not automatically teach someone how to perform it.

The absent father remains present even inside his own experience of being a father.

### Marriage

The conversation also touches on Teoman's failed marriage.

The subject is handled in the broader context of his difficulty with conventional domestic structures.

For decades, Teoman's strongest personal value was autonomy.

Marriage requires negotiation with another person's structure.

Those two systems do not always coexist easily.

### Freedom Has Costs

This has become increasingly clear across the archive.

Independence gave Teoman:

artistic control,

time,

privacy,

and unconventional relationships.

But independence can also create:

distance,

loneliness,

difficulty sharing a life,

and difficulty accepting ordinary obligations.

The same trait produces both freedom and cost.

### Courage

Teoman says something particularly interesting about age:

he is no longer as courageous as he once was.

This may sound negative.

But the archive suggests that some youthful “courage” was also disregard for consequences.

Older Teoman sees more possible outcomes.

Knowledge can reduce recklessness.

### Heart Versus Mind

He says that at this age he increasingly continues through reason.

Asking the heart can create confusion.

The younger person experiences desire more directly.

The older person has accumulated:

memories,

consequences,

failures,

contradictions.

Feeling is no longer simple.

### Romance Is Far Away

Teoman also says romance now feels distant.

Again, this does not need to mean total absence of sexuality or relationships.

The statement concerns the emotional mythology around them.

Love no longer occupies the central heroic position it once held.

### The Songs Remain More Romantic Than the Man

This creates an interesting artistic paradox.

The catalogue permanently contains the emotional intensity of younger Teoman.

The performer can change.

The songs cannot age psychologically unless reinterpreted.

An older, less romantic Teoman therefore continues singing texts written by a person for whom romantic intensity was much more central.

### “I Was Actually Working Like a Donkey”

Teoman pushes back against another part of his public mythology.

People imagined him spending his life drinking and partying.

He says the truth was very different.

Behind that image, he worked extremely hard from childhood onward.

This confirms something visible when his actual career is examined chronologically.

### The Work Behind Carelessness

Albums do not create themselves.

Songs do not write themselves.

Films, books and tours require organization.

The effortless Teoman image depends on substantial invisible effort.

The better the persona worked, the easier it was to hide the labor required to maintain the career.

### The Documentary

Teoman also mentions that a documentary about him is planned.

He connects his remaining *Varoluşçuluk 101* performances with the documentary, saying the shows will help establish its frame.

This is exceptionally relevant to an archive project.

Teoman is now actively participating in the construction of his own historical record.

### From Archive to Documentary

The sequence continues:

*Koyu Antoloji* organizes songs.

*Fasa Fiso* organizes memory.

*Sayın Bay Rock Yıldızı* transforms persona into fiction.

*Varoluşçuluk 101* turns biography into performance.

A documentary will transform the same material again.

Teoman's later career is increasingly about **forms of remembering Teoman**.

### How Does He Want to Be Remembered?

Ahmet Mümtaz Taylan eventually reaches a direct legacy question.

Teoman's answer is surprising.

He does not name:

David Bowie,

Leonard Cohen,

Bob Dylan,

or another major rock figure.

He names **Özkan Uğur**.

### Özkan Uğur

Teoman says he would like to be remembered in something like the way people remember Özkan Uğur.

Not merely respected.

Loved warmly.

Associated with good feelings.

This represents an enormous shift in the meaning of legacy.

### From Greatness to Warmth

Young Teoman wanted to make important songs.

Middle-aged Teoman wanted songs that would become classics.

Later Teoman organized his catalogue for survival.

But 2026 Teoman introduces another criterion:

**What emotional feeling will people have when they remember the person?**

Artistic greatness is no longer sufficient.

### The Human Legacy

Teoman knows he probably cannot reproduce Özkan Uğur's universally warm public image.

His own personality is sharper and more difficult.

That self-awareness makes the aspiration more meaningful.

He is not claiming to be that person.

He is saying he wishes he could leave something of that emotional quality.

### From “Great Artist” to “Good Memory”

This may represent the final evolution of Teoman's legacy anxiety.

1996:

make good music.

2000:

become a serious songwriter.

2007:

write songs that survive.

2018:

organize the catalogue.

2025:

manage the archive.

2026:

perhaps what matters is also whether people remember you with affection.

### Why This Interview Matters

This is one of the richest biographical interviews in the entire archive because it connects Teoman's childhood directly with his late-life attempts at self-management.

It establishes or strongly reinforces that:

- Teoman's father died when he was about two,
- he grew up mainly with his mother, aunt and grandmother,
- fictional figures such as Zagor and Elvis partly supplied missing male models,
- he decided very young that he wanted to become a rock star,
- his mother initially disliked and even felt embarrassed by his profession,
- he associates some of his depressive and irritable tendencies with his mother and childhood environment,
- he consciously monitors and controls anger because he regrets the damage afterward,
- he fears becoming an angry and difficult old man,
- long-term sleep problems and alcohol contributed to psychological difficulties,
- healthy living is now part of his attempt to protect his mental state,
- he does not believe he became the father he once imagined himself becoming,
- the supposed “careless rock star” actually worked extremely hard,
- he is thinking actively about a documentary and the framing of his own legacy,
- and he would like to be remembered less as an untouchable rock monument than with something resembling the warmth attached to Özkan Uğur.

The deepest structure, however, concerns fatherhood.

The child Teoman grows up without a father.

He builds male identity partly from fictional heroes.

He becomes the rock star he imagined.

He has a daughter.

Then, near sixty, he admits that becoming a father did not automatically teach him how to be the father he imagined.

That is much more interesting than a story of simple psychological healing.

Life does not close the circle perfectly.

It returns us to the same themes from another position.

And by 2026, Teoman seems increasingly willing to accept imperfect circles.
