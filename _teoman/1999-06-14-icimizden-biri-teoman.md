## İçimizden Biri: Teoman

**14 June 1999**

This interview captures a particularly reflective Teoman.

The discussion moves far beyond music. He talks about belonging, Turkish society, popular culture, loneliness, relationships, childhood, politics and his graduate studies in Women's Studies at Boğaziçi University.

What emerges is a musician who is becoming famous while still trying to understand where he stands in relation to the society around him.

### “One of Us”

The interview begins with an idea often attached to Teoman at the time: that he feels like “one of us.”

Teoman himself is not entirely convinced.

He recognizes that audiences may identify with his songs, but he does not describe himself as someone who naturally feels part of a large collective.

This distinction is important.

His songs may produce intimacy with thousands of people, but that does not mean he personally experiences the same intimacy.

The contradiction between **public familiarity and private distance** becomes one of the central themes of the interview.

### Communication Without Depth

Teoman says that he communicates with many people, but these connections often remain short-lived.

The problem is not the absence of communication.

It is the absence of depth.

He describes social life as a network of connections whose range is limited. This produces a strange condition: a person can constantly meet people and still remain emotionally isolated.

That observation fits remarkably well with the urban loneliness that later becomes strongly associated with Teoman's songs.

### Singer, Not “Artist”

Teoman makes an interesting distinction when describing his profession.

He prefers the simple word **singer**.

He does not seem interested in debates about who deserves to be called an artist. Singing, for him, is concrete. He spends hours performing songs, while the act of writing them occupies much less time.

He even says that he had wanted to be a singer since childhood.

This rejection of grand artistic labels is characteristic.

At the same time, he recognizes something he calls an “artist character”: a way of looking at life that can exist even without producing a novel, poem or song.

### Sadness as a Creative Environment

Teoman also describes the conditions under which he writes.

Summer is not especially productive.

Autumn is better.

Night is better than daytime.

Rain helps.

Most importantly, loneliness helps.

For him, sadness is not simply suffering. It can become a productive state.

He suggests that people may need a certain amount of depression in order to create something.

Once a song emerges from that state, sadness can paradoxically turn into satisfaction.

This gives us an early explanation for one of the most recognizable qualities of his later songwriting: melancholy is not merely a subject.

It is also a working environment.

### A Fast Life for a Slow Person

By 1999, Teoman's career is moving rapidly.

Concerts are frequent. Cities blur together. He sometimes struggles to remember where he performed only a few days earlier.

He describes himself as someone accustomed to slowness.

Fame has therefore created a conflict between his preferred internal rhythm and the speed of his external life.

This is one of the earliest signs of a tension that would appear again much later in his career.

Teoman wants time to think.

The career of Teoman demands constant movement.

### Politics at a Distance

The interview takes place during an intense period in Turkish politics.

Teoman follows events but increasingly questions how much emotional energy he should give them.

His approach is not political indifference in the strict sense. He clearly has opinions and can become deeply involved in political discussions.

But he is trying to construct distance.

He wants to look at politics from outside rather than allowing every political crisis to determine the emotional structure of his life.

His preferred response is sometimes irony.

Behind that irony, however, there is clear concern about violence, nationalism and the political atmosphere of Turkey in the late 1990s.

### Belonging to Turkey

One of the strongest passages concerns belonging.

As a teenager, Teoman imagined that his real life might exist somewhere abroad.

He later discovered the opposite.

After traveling, he realized that even his dreams belonged to Turkey.

This produces a complicated form of attachment.

He feels that he belongs to the country while simultaneously disliking many aspects of it.

For Teoman, belonging does not require unconditional admiration.

One can belong somewhere and still criticize it deeply.

This is one of the more mature ideas in the interview and contrasts with the simple fantasy of escape he associates with adolescence.

### Individual Happiness

After years of observing political and social problems, Teoman says that he has increasingly turned toward individual happiness.

He does not believe he has witnessed Turkey moving decisively toward either a completely better or completely worse condition.

The result is a retreat toward a smaller scale of meaning.

Rather than imagining that personal fulfillment will come from society becoming ideal, he looks toward individual experiences.

This does not mean that the outside world disappears.

It means that he no longer expects it to provide the foundation of happiness.

### The Child He Used to Be

One of the most revealing parts of the interview concerns Teoman's relationship with his younger self.

He speaks almost as if the child he once was still exists as another person.

That child had dreams, suffered and questioned life.

Teoman suggests that part of his adult life is an attempt to make that earlier version of himself happy.

Concerts and songs become, in this sense, gifts to the younger Teoman.

He even says that the version of himself he likes most is not necessarily the adult standing in the interview.

It is the earlier one.

This idea will become particularly interesting when compared with his much later interviews about aging.

### Songs About People, Not Just Love

Teoman rejects the idea that he simply writes love songs.

His songs, he argues, are closer to people themselves.

Human experience is never composed of one emotion at a time.

A person can wake up hungover, feel cold, experience heartbreak, remember another woman and worry about ordinary daily problems simultaneously.

A song can contain all of these things.

This explains part of the realism of Teoman's songwriting.

Love is present, but it exists alongside cigarettes, rooms, streets, alcohol, boredom, memory and ordinary urban life.

### Love and Difficulty

Teoman is skeptical about repeated declarations of love.

If someone falls in love ten times, he wonders whether all ten experiences can really represent the same extraordinary condition.

He seems attracted to a more difficult and almost literary conception of love.

Modern life, however, makes everything easier.

Travel is easier.

Communication is easier.

Meeting someone is easier.

Teoman wonders whether this convenience also reduces the emotional value of experiences.

The idea is romantic and somewhat nostalgic, but it reveals what he expects from love: transformation rather than simple companionship.

### Conflict in Relationships

Teoman is unusually critical of himself when discussing relationships.

He admits that he is not naturally inclined toward compromise.

Relationships can turn into competitions, and when they do, he wants to win.

He recognizes that this tendency can damage the other person.

Even more strikingly, he says that understanding these behaviors intellectually does not necessarily prevent him from repeating them.

This creates an important distinction:

**self-awareness is not the same as self-correction.**

Teoman can observe his own mistakes with considerable clarity while continuing to make them.

### Women's Studies

One of the most unusual aspects of Teoman's biography at this point is his graduate study in Women's Studies at Boğaziçi University.

His interest began after taking a course on feminist perspectives.

He describes the field as intellectually eye-opening rather than as something to which he planned to dedicate his entire academic life.

The effect, in his words, was to widen his perspective.

He became more capable of recognizing sexist assumptions in media and society and of noticing that people who considered themselves progressive could still reproduce sexist or homophobic attitudes.

His thesis combined this field with another personal interest: comics.

He studied representations of women in Turkish comics, particularly material from the 1950s through the 1970s, and also examined *Abdülcanbaz* as an alternative example.

### Knowledge Does Not Automatically Change Behavior

Perhaps the most interesting part of this discussion is Teoman's admission that studying gender did not magically improve his romantic relationships.

He can analyze behavior more accurately.

He can identify problems.

But he remains the same person emotionally.

This is another version of the distinction between knowledge and transformation.

Learning can widen one's perspective without immediately rebuilding one's character.

### Why This Interview Matters

This interview is one of the richest documents from Teoman's early career because it shows several dimensions of him at once.

The musician is only one of them.

We also see:

- the sociology graduate,
- the Women's Studies student,
- the skeptical observer of popular culture,
- the man uncomfortable with collective identities,
- the Turkish citizen with a complicated sense of belonging,
- the songwriter who needs solitude,
- the adult still emotionally connected to his childhood,
- and the self-aware but often self-forgiving partner.

Compared with the interview from only a few days earlier, the public image becomes much less important here.

The focus moves inward.

The 1996 interview showed **the musician before fame**.

The 9 June 1999 interview showed **the musician encountering celebrity**.

This interview, only five days later, shows something different:

**the person trying to understand himself beneath that celebrity.**
