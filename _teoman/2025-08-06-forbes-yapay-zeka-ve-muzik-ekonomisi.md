## Yakın Gelecekte Sanatçılar ve İş Dünyası Ekonomik Yıkımla Karşılaşacak

**Forbes Türkiye — 6 August 2025**  
**Interview / profile by Nilgün Balcı Çavdar**

This is one of the most unusual Teoman interviews in the archive.

There is almost no romantic mythology here.

No bar stool.

No discussion of heartbreak.

No attempt to explain the melancholy singer.

Instead, Forbes meets a version of Teoman that earlier interviews only occasionally revealed:

**Teoman the strategist.**

At fifty-eight, he describes himself not only as an artist but also as a businessman.

And the problem occupying him is no longer simply how an artist should survive fame.

It is how artists will survive artificial intelligence.

### “Artist-Businessman”

Teoman describes himself with a deliberately provocative formulation:

> “Hardcore sanatçı - hardcore iş adamı.”

The phrase initially sounds incompatible.

The romantic mythology of art often assumes that commerce contaminates creativity.

Teoman rejects that separation.

He can care deeply about literature and music while also caring about markets, investment and economic structures.

For him, these interests do not cancel one another.

### Shakespeare and Markets

Teoman makes the contrast even sharper by placing Shakespeare's sonnets beside stock-market information as things capable of interesting him.

The statement helps explain a side of Teoman that has appeared intermittently throughout the archive.

He has never been interested only in artistic emotion.

His sociology education, curiosity about systems and long career inside the music industry have made him attentive to the economic structures surrounding art.

### The Artist Inside an Economy

This represents a major development from the 2006 conversation in which Teoman openly accepted that he worked within commercial art.

Back then, the question was whether commercial success made art less authentic.

By 2025, that debate almost seems old-fashioned.

The more urgent issue is whether the economic system supporting professional artists will continue to exist at all.

### Artificial Intelligence as Economic Disruption

Teoman is extremely pessimistic about generative AI.

He rejects comforting comparisons with previous technological revolutions.

In his view, this is not simply another tool that will create a few new professions while eliminating a few old ones.

The scale may be much larger.

He expects serious economic destruction across creative industries and beyond them.

### The Problem Is Abundance

Teoman's argument is fundamentally economic.

Artificial intelligence can produce enormous quantities of content.

Songs.

Images.

Video.

Text.

Advertising material.

The problem is not whether every generated work will be excellent.

The problem is supply.

When production becomes extremely cheap and almost unlimited, the market can become flooded.

Demand cannot grow at the same speed.

### When Creation Becomes Cheap

This leads to a frightening conclusion.

If creative output becomes abundant enough, the economic value of an individual creative contribution can collapse.

Music has historically possessed value partly because producing professional-quality music required:

time,

skill,

musicians,

studios,

equipment,

distribution,

and money.

AI dramatically reduces several of those costs.

Scarcity disappears.

### Art and Economic Value Are Different

Teoman's concern is therefore not simply that AI will produce “better art.”

The more important question is whether human creativity will continue to command economic value.

A beautiful human-written song can still be meaningful.

But meaning and market price are not the same thing.

This distinction is central to the Forbes conversation.

### A New Threat to Musicians

In 2010, Teoman worried about MP3s and falling record sales.

That technological shift changed distribution.

AI threatens something deeper.

MP3s still required musicians to produce songs.

Streaming still required musicians to produce songs.

Generative AI can potentially reduce demand for the musician himself.

The economic function of the creator becomes uncertain.

### From Distribution Crisis to Production Crisis

This creates a clear technological chronology in the archive.

**2010:** How will musicians earn money when physical sales collapse?

**2025:** What happens when music itself can be produced at almost zero marginal cost?

The first crisis changed the business model.

The second may change who participates in the business.

### “Only the Biggest Will Survive”

Teoman believes established stars may retain some protection.

They already possess:

recognition,

catalogues,

audiences,

brands,

concert demand,

and cultural memory.

New artists have none of these advantages.

For them, competing against unlimited synthetic production may become extraordinarily difficult.

### The Catalogue as Economic Defense

This gives a new meaning to something Teoman has spent years discussing:

his catalogue.

Earlier, the catalogue represented artistic legacy.

Then it provided the freedom to work less.

Now it also represents economic protection.

Old songs carry accumulated human attention that newly generated material cannot instantly reproduce.

The archive becomes an asset.

### The Young Artist's Problem

Teoman's pessimism is strongest when he talks about people entering the profession now.

A young musician must compete not only against other musicians.

They may eventually compete against an essentially infinite supply of generated material optimized by algorithms.

Teoman therefore questions whether advising young people to pursue music professionally is still responsible.

### The Artist Becomes an Entrepreneur

His solution is not to retreat from technology.

It is almost the opposite.

Teoman believes the modern artist increasingly has to think like an entrepreneur.

Understand technology.

Understand distribution.

Understand audiences.

Understand data.

Understand costs.

Understand intellectual property.

Artistic ability alone may no longer be enough.

### Teoman as a Company

This leads to one of the interview's most memorable ideas.

Teoman says he increasingly manages himself almost like a company.

This does not mean he has abandoned artistic identity.

It means “Teoman” now includes multiple activities.

Music.

Books.

Advertising.

Characters.

Social media.

Technology projects.

Performances.

Intellectual property.

The person has become a portfolio.

### “Which Teoman Do You Need?”

Teoman jokes that when someone approaches him with a proposal, his response is effectively:

**Which Teoman do you need?**

This is fascinating when placed beside the rest of the archive.

For decades, Teoman complained that fame had created multiple versions of him.

The media Teoman.

The rock-star Teoman.

The private Teoman.

The songwriter.

The womanizer.

By 2025, multiplicity has become strategy.

### From Identity Problem to Business Model

This may be one of the largest transformations in the archive.

Young Teoman:

**People keep constructing versions of me that I cannot control.**

Older Teoman:

**I have several versions of myself. Which one is useful for this project?**

The problem has been converted into an asset.

### Strategic Advertising

Forbes notes that Teoman had appeared in several advertising campaigns during the previous months.

He says this did not happen accidentally.

It was part of a deliberate strategy.

Again, the attitude toward commercial work is strikingly different from the romantic artist mythology.

Advertising does not automatically contaminate the musician.

It becomes one component in a diversified professional system.

### Music Is No Longer Enough

Teoman says music no longer satisfies him by itself, either artistically or economically.

This statement deserves emphasis.

In earlier interviews, music stopped being enough primarily because of boredom.

Now the argument has an additional dimension.

Economic diversification is becoming rational.

Depending entirely on music may be creatively limiting and financially dangerous.

### Timur Becomes a Media Property

One of the most interesting examples is Timur, the protagonist of *Sayın Bay Rock Yıldızı*.

In 2024, Timur was primarily a fictional character used to investigate the aging rock star.

By 2025, Teoman and an AI-focused digital agency have expanded Timur outside the book.

The character gains a social-media presence.

He becomes something close to a virtual influencer.

### Fiction Becomes Intellectual Property

This represents another major development.

Ruhi was a fictional character inside songs.

Timur begins inside a novel but can move between media.

Book.

Instagram.

AI-generated visual material.

Potential music.

Advertising-like content.

The fictional character becomes an independent cultural property.

### The Character Can Outlive the Author

This has interesting implications for Teoman's obsession with legacy.

Songs can survive Teoman.

Books can survive Teoman.

A fictional character potentially can as well.

Timur may become another structure capable of existing independently of the person who created him.

### Artificial Intelligence as Tool and Threat

Teoman's relationship with AI is therefore intentionally contradictory.

He believes it poses an enormous economic threat.

Yet he uses it.

This is not hypocrisy.

It follows directly from his economic reasoning.

If the technology is going to transform the system, refusing to understand it does not prevent the transformation.

### Adaptation Rather Than Denial

Teoman's strategy is essentially:

**expect disruption and adapt early.**

That attitude is very different from nostalgic resistance.

He does not argue that the old music industry should simply be restored.

He assumes that it will not be.

The artist must reorganize around the new conditions.

### AI and Video Production

Forbes gives a concrete example from Teoman's new music.

AI-supported production dramatically lowers the cost of creating a music video.

A conventional production can require:

large crews,

locations,

equipment,

transport,

editing,

casting,

styling,

and significant capital.

AI can reduce those costs dramatically.

### Lower Barriers, Greater Competition

This creates another paradox.

The technology helps creators.

A young person can produce visual material without access to a large production company.

That is democratizing.

But the same reduction in barriers means everyone can produce more.

The market becomes even more crowded.

The technology simultaneously increases opportunity and destroys scarcity.

### Kırılganlar Kralı

The Forbes article also places the interview in the context of Teoman's forthcoming *Kırılganlar Kralı* project.

AI-supported visual production is already being used around the new material.

This means Teoman is not theorizing from outside.

He is experimenting with the technologies he fears.

That gives the conversation unusual practical weight.

### The Sociologist Becomes a Strategist

Teoman explicitly credits his ability to make socio-economic analyses.

This is another late return of his sociology education.

Earlier it helped him think about:

Turkey,

gender,

political identity,

class,

and polarization.

Now he applies the same perspective to technological change.

The object has changed.

The method remains structural.

### Art Is Not Outside Capitalism

The conversation ultimately rejects another romantic idea.

Artists do not exist outside economics.

They pay rent.

Employ people.

Receive royalties.

Negotiate contracts.

Invest.

Lose income.

Respond to technological change.

The symbolic value of art does not eliminate material reality.

### “Money Is Not Important, Lack of Money Is”

Teoman ends with a characteristic distinction.

Money itself does not deserve worship.

But the absence of money has real consequences.

This is a much more practical philosophy than the romantic rejection of commerce.

Economic security creates freedom.

Economic collapse removes choices.

### The Young Teoman Wanted Artistic Control

This brings the entire chronology into focus.

In 1996, Teoman wanted control over his album.

In the 2000s, he wanted to prevent commercial success from controlling his artistic decisions.

In the 2010s, he wanted control over his time.

By 2025, control means something larger:

**building a system capable of surviving technological disruption.**

### Why This Interview Matters

This Forbes profile adds an entirely new dimension to the Teoman archive.

For the first time, Teoman's artistic philosophy, sociology background and commercial strategy are examined together.

The central themes are:

- AI as an economic rather than merely artistic problem,
- unlimited creative supply and collapsing scarcity,
- the vulnerability of young musicians,
- established catalogues as protection,
- the artist becoming an entrepreneur,
- Teoman deliberately managing himself as a diversified company,
- multiple Teoman personas becoming strategic assets,
- Timur evolving from fictional character into digital intellectual property,
- AI simultaneously lowering production costs and threatening creative livelihoods,
- and economic autonomy becoming a condition of artistic autonomy.

For decades Teoman tried to separate the person from the public product called Teoman.

By 2025, he seems to have reached a surprisingly different conclusion.

The product exists.

The personas exist.

The market exists.

Technology exists.

Pretending otherwise offers no protection.

So he chooses to manage them.

This may sound less romantic than the story of the lonely songwriter with a guitar.

But it is also the logical end point of one of the oldest themes in his career:

**control.**

The young Teoman wanted control over the song.

The older Teoman wants control over the system around the song.
