## Müziğin Yerine Hiçbir Şey Koyamadım

**Milliyet Cadde — 26 May 2012**  
**Interview by Tolga Akyıldız**

This is one of the most important interviews from Teoman's retirement period.

Only about ten months had passed since he announced that he was leaving music.

The earlier GQ interview documented the immediate aftermath.

Here, Teoman goes much deeper.

He tries to explain why he stopped, what disappointed him about his career, why success had begun to feel like failure and why abandoning music created a problem he had not anticipated:

**nothing else could replace it.**

### He Still Cannot Fully Explain Why He Quit

Tolga Akyıldız begins with the obvious question.

After ten months, does Teoman finally understand why he left?

Not completely.

Teoman says time has helped him understand the decision, but he still cannot reduce it to one reason.

There were many variables.

The music industry.

Concerts.

His own creative exhaustion.

His personality.

His expectations.

The decision was the result of accumulation rather than a single event.

### The Industry Had Changed

One source of frustration was the changing structure of the music business.

Teoman complains that if a song did not have a video clip, it could almost disappear from public awareness.

An album could contain many songs, but only a small number would reach listeners.

This created a fundamental problem for someone who believed in albums as complete artistic objects.

The industry increasingly rewarded individual visibility rather than the album as a whole.

### Why Write Songs Nobody Hears?

This frustration becomes existential.

Teoman could continue writing new songs.

But if audiences at concerts mainly wanted the familiar hits, why make new ones?

The question is not economic.

It is motivational.

The feedback loop between creation and audience had weakened.

New work no longer produced the emotional reward that earlier albums had produced.

### Creative Exhaustion

Teoman says bluntly that while making *Aşk ve Gurur*, he felt his creative side running out.

The irony is that the album was well received.

External approval therefore could not solve the problem.

People might say the work was good.

Teoman no longer experienced the process as meaningful enough.

This distinction between external success and internal satisfaction had existed since 2000.

In 2012 it becomes decisive.

### Concerts Were Another Failure

Teoman is equally severe about his live career.

From the outside, he was one of the few Turkish musicians capable of major sponsored tours.

From the inside, he felt dissatisfied.

He could achieve the production quality he wanted only under specific conditions.

Ordinary ticketed concerts often failed to meet his standards.

That gap increasingly made him angry.

### “I Consider Myself Unsuccessful”

This produces one of the most revealing contradictions in the interview.

Publicly, Teoman is clearly successful.

Privately, he describes himself as unsuccessful.

The problem is the measurement.

The audience measures:

fame,

ticket sales,

albums,

songs,

career longevity.

Teoman measures:

whether the work matched the version he imagined.

Under the second system, success becomes much harder.

### Perfectionism

Tolga Akyıldız directly asks whether Teoman's judgment may come from perfectionism and excessive harshness toward himself.

Teoman accepts that possibility.

Looking backward, he believes he could have concentrated more deeply on music and created something better.

This is painful because success does not protect against counterfactual regret.

A person can achieve a great deal while remaining preoccupied with the version that might have been greater.

### The Band He Never Built

One regret is especially concrete.

Teoman says he never managed to create a stable long-term band.

Musicians changed constantly.

He never developed the kind of group chemistry in which people know one another almost instinctively.

He compares this unfavorably with Şebnem Ferah's band.

For Teoman, this failure contributed to loneliness.

### Artistic Control Becomes Isolation

This is particularly interesting when compared with 1996.

The young Teoman wanted complete artistic control.

He preferred being a solo artist partly because the songs were his and he wanted final authority.

Sixteen years later, the cost of that structure becomes visible.

Control protected authorship.

But it may also have made stable collective identity more difficult.

The solo artist remained alone.

### Anger

Teoman admits that frustration affected the people around him.

He became harsher.

He insulted people.

He broke relationships.

This is not presented proudly.

The artist dissatisfied with his own work began transferring that dissatisfaction outward.

Again, self-analysis does not necessarily protect other people from the consequences.

### Becoming His Own Caricature

One of the most important phrases in the entire interview is:

**“Kendi kendimin karikatürü olmuşum gibi hissettim.”**

This fear had appeared earlier.

In 2007, Teoman said celebrities risk becoming caricatures because they are expected to repeatedly perform the same public identity.

By 2012, he believes the process may actually be happening to him.

### “Am I Playing a Role?”

The anxiety becomes so strong that Teoman begins questioning even ordinary actions.

Is he doing something because he genuinely wants to?

Or because it belongs to the role called Teoman?

This is an identity crisis created by repetition.

Once a persona becomes familiar, the person can no longer easily distinguish spontaneous behavior from performance.

### The Wrestler

Teoman uses Darren Aronofsky's *The Wrestler* as a nightmare image.

Mickey Rourke's aging performer continues repeating a physically and psychologically destructive identity long after its heroic period has ended.

Teoman fears becoming a musical version of that character.

An aging performer trapped in the gestures that once made him famous.

### Not Becoming Bon Jovi

The interview contains another famous comparison.

Teoman says he had imagined growing toward artists such as:

Leonard Cohen,

Tom Waits,

Bülent Ortaçgil.

Instead, he feared becoming something closer to Bon Jovi.

This is not primarily an insult to Bon Jovi.

Teoman explicitly recognizes their success.

The distinction concerns aging.

### Songs That Age With the Singer

Leonard Cohen can sing an old song at seventy-eight and the song still belongs in his mouth.

That is what Teoman wants.

He worries that some forms of rock remain permanently connected with teenage emotional structures.

If the singer ages while the songs remain adolescent, a gap appears.

Teoman wants songs capable of aging with him.

### Timelessness

This concern reveals the real scale on which Teoman judges his work.

Immediate success is not enough.

A song should survive time.

He says directly that he wrote his songs hoping people would still listen later.

If they disappear culturally, that will represent a much larger disappointment than ordinary commercial failure.

### Legacy Before Legacy Exists

This is striking because Teoman is only forty-four.

Yet he is already evaluating his catalogue from the perspective of posterity.

The question is not:

**Was this album successful in 2005?**

It is:

**Will anyone care in twenty or thirty years?**

This concern explains much of his later archival work.

### Why Depressive Songs?

Tolga Akyıldız asks whether Teoman's songwriting depends on depression.

Teoman gives a nuanced answer.

A good songwriter should theoretically be capable of entering an emotional state without currently living inside it.

One can be happy and still write sadness.

That, he suggests, may represent a higher artistic skill.

### He Still Cannot Write Happy Songs

Yet when Teoman deliberately attempts to write something cheerful, the result somehow remains melancholic.

This suggests that style is deeper than conscious intention.

He can decide to change topic.

The underlying emotional grammar remains.

This may be one of the strongest arguments that the melancholy in Teoman's work is not simply a branding choice.

### Bruce Springsteen and Storytelling

Teoman also gives an important early influence.

At seventeen, during his Boğaziçi preparatory year, a teacher used Bruce Springsteen's *The River* in a listening-comprehension class.

Teoman was deeply affected by the song's storytelling.

He remembers thinking:

**I should write songs like this.**

This is an important addition to the archive.

It gives a concrete origin point for his narrative songwriting ambition.

### Story Rather Than Generic Love

Teoman contrasts storytelling with generic popular lyrics.

Instead of simply saying:

“I loved you and you did not love me,”

he wants events.

Characters.

Places.

Scenes.

Specific details.

This is exactly the songwriting philosophy we have observed since the late 1990s.

Here he identifies an early model for it.

### Kupa Kızı Sinek Valesi

He mentions *Kupa Kızı Sinek Valesi* as an example of how difficult this method can be.

Story songs require more architecture than generic emotional statements.

A convincing narrative has to balance:

information,

emotion,

character,

rhythm,

and economy.

The song must tell a story without becoming prose with background music.

### Cinema Cannot Replace Music

After leaving music, Teoman naturally considers other old ambitions.

Cinema is the obvious candidate.

He has a screenplay called *İstenmeyen Tüyler* that has existed for years.

But retirement reveals something unexpected.

Cinema was never really an independent alternative.

It had always existed beside music.

### The Other Dreams Were Satellites

This realization is central.

Teoman had often imagined multiple possible identities:

academic,

filmmaker,

writer,

musician.

After removing music, he discovers that the other ambitions do not have the same gravitational force.

They were complementary.

Music was the center.

### “I Am a Musician”

Eventually he reaches the clearest statement in the interview:

he is a musician and does not really want to become something else.

He considers returning to academic life.

No.

Sports.

No.

Yoga.

No.

Nothing fits.

This creates the paradox of retirement:

he left the only profession he actually wanted.

### Nothing Replaced Music

This is the meaning of the title.

Teoman expected that removing music might reveal another life.

Instead it created a void.

The problem had not been that he wanted another profession.

The problem was that the profession he wanted no longer gave him enough satisfaction.

That is much harder to solve.

### A Possible Return

For the first time, Teoman begins imagining a limited return.

Not the old system.

Not constant touring.

Perhaps ten concerts a year.

More careful arrangements.

Higher musical standards.

A larger and better-prepared team.

This is the beginning of the later Teoman model:

**reduce quantity in order to recover meaning.**

### Fewer Concerts, Better Concerts

The solution is elegant.

The old career structure demanded frequency.

Teoman's dissatisfaction demanded quality.

If he removes most concerts, the remaining ones can become special projects.

This principle will later spread to almost every part of his career.

Fewer interviews.

Fewer albums.

Selected books.

Selected performances.

More control.

### Producing for Others

Teoman even says that if he writes new songs, he may prefer writing them for other performers.

Again, this separates songwriting from the public identity of Teoman.

He may still enjoy production.

What exhausts him is the complete machine surrounding the Teoman product.

### The Magazin Figure

Another painful realization concerns fame.

Teoman had believed he did not care much about media coverage.

After leaving music, he realizes that he did care.

Worse, he fears that his cultural impact may have been more visual and tabloid than musical.

He wanted to influence people through songs.

Instead, perhaps the public mostly remembers the celebrity.

### Popular but Musically Invisible

This creates an extraordinary paradox.

Teoman is famous enough that almost everyone recognizes him.

Yet he worries that people are not really listening to the work.

Fame has become detached from the thing that originally justified fame.

The person is visible.

The songs feel invisible.

### The Nightmare of Celebrity

This may be the purest version of Teoman's long-running fame problem.

At first, fame threatens privacy.

Later, it threatens identity.

Finally, it threatens artistic meaning.

What if Teoman survives culturally, but the songs do not?

That would be the exact opposite of what he wants.

### Why This Interview Matters

This may be the strongest single interview explaining Teoman's 2011 retirement.

It contains nearly every factor:

- creative exhaustion,
- dissatisfaction with the music industry,
- disappointment with concert quality,
- perfectionism,
- failure to build a stable band,
- loneliness,
- anger toward collaborators,
- fear of becoming his own caricature,
- fear of aging inside a teenage rock identity,
- anxiety about whether his songs will become timeless,
- declining excitement about new material,
- recognition that cinema and other interests cannot replace music,
- and realization that fame may have overshadowed the music itself.

Most importantly, the interview shows that Teoman's problem was never simply:

**I don't want to make music anymore.**

The real problem was:

**I only want to make music, but I no longer want to make it this way.**

That distinction explains what happens next.

Teoman eventually returns.

But the career he rebuilds is different.

Less automatic.

Less frequent.

More selective.

And increasingly designed around the principle that would define his later life:

**if quantity destroys meaning, reduce the quantity.**
