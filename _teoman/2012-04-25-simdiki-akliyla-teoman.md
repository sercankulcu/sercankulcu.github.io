## Şimdiki Aklıyla Teoman

**GQ Türkiye — 25 April 2012**  
**Interview by Ebru Çapa**

This interview takes place after one of the most dramatic decisions in Teoman's career.

In September 2011, after roughly fifteen years of professional music and nine albums, he announced that he was leaving music.

The decision shocked much of his audience.

By the time Ebru Çapa meets him in 2012, almost nine months have passed.

The immediate drama has faded.

What remains is the more interesting question:

**Why did someone who had achieved almost everything he once wanted decide to stop?**

### After the Rock Star

The first image of Teoman in the interview is deliberately ordinary.

There is no stage.

No leather jacket.

No concert lighting.

No obvious rock-star performance.

He is sitting on a sofa in casual clothes, drinking tea.

The contrast matters.

Teoman had spent years explaining the difference between the public character and the private individual.

Now the public character has temporarily been removed.

What remains is the person who had been performing it.

### Nine Months Outside the Machine

Leaving music does not immediately produce another grand project.

Teoman spends months doing very little.

This period is almost deliberately empty.

He is no longer moving between concerts, interviews, recordings and promotional obligations.

The machinery surrounding Teoman has stopped.

That creates something he had complained about lacking for years:

time.

### Routine

Interestingly, the supposedly chaotic rock star describes himself as someone with rigid routines.

He likes finishing necessary tasks early.

If something is scheduled later in the day, he finds it difficult to enjoy the hours before it.

Even during his period away from music, he develops habits.

He wakes up.

He exercises.

He eats breakfast.

He visits people close to him.

He returns home.

The image contradicts the public mythology of the permanently disorganized musician.

### Reconstructing Daily Life

At first, Teoman also stops going out at night.

He tries to reorganize his habits.

Eventually he realizes that nightlife is something he genuinely enjoys.

So he begins returning to it gradually.

This is an important distinction.

Leaving the rock-star profession does not require deleting every behavior associated with the persona.

Teoman is trying to discover which parts belonged to work and which parts actually belong to him.

### Why Stop?

The decision to leave music was not caused by failure.

That makes it more psychologically interesting.

Teoman was still successful.

Concerts were available.

The audience remained.

His catalogue was strong.

The problem was not that the career had collapsed.

The problem was that continuing it had begun to feel increasingly meaningless.

### When Success Becomes Repetition

By this point, Teoman already knows the structure of the professional musician's life.

Make an album.

Promote it.

Give interviews.

Shoot videos.

Perform concerts.

Begin thinking about another album.

Repeat.

A career that once represented freedom can eventually become another routine.

This is one of the central paradoxes of Teoman's story.

He spent years fighting to become the person who could make his own music.

Then being that person became a job he wanted to escape.

### The Dream Has Already Been Completed

This connects directly with the Best FM interview from 2000.

More than a decade earlier, Teoman had already said that he had become the person his seventeen-year-old self wanted to become.

The problem was that the achievements imagined by the younger Teoman did not automatically produce lasting happiness.

By 2012, that problem has matured.

There is little left to prove professionally.

The old goals have been completed.

Repeating them with larger numbers no longer creates the same motivation.

### Burnout Without a Single Cause

Teoman's withdrawal should not be reduced to one dramatic event.

The interview suggests accumulation.

Fatigue.

Repetition.

Loss of curiosity.

Discomfort with the machinery around music.

A desire to see what happens when obligations disappear.

These factors combine gradually.

Burnout does not always arrive as collapse.

Sometimes it arrives as indifference.

### The Desire to Want Something Again

One of Teoman's recurring problems throughout the archive is not achieving what he wants.

It is finding something worth wanting.

As a young man, the objective was clear:

make music professionally.

Then:

make a good album.

Become successful.

Build an audience.

By 2012, these problems are largely solved.

The harder question remains:

**What comes after the dreams that organized your earlier life?**

### Retirement as Experiment

Seen this way, Teoman's retirement is not simply surrender.

It is an experiment.

Remove the career.

Remove the schedule.

Remove the expectation to be Teoman.

Then observe what remains.

Will another desire appear?

Will music return naturally?

Will cinema matter more?

Will writing become more important?

Or will ordinary life be enough?

### The Freedom of Not Producing

Popular culture treats constant productivity as evidence of vitality.

Teoman deliberately interrupts that logic.

For months, he does not need to produce another Teoman product.

This allows him to discover a kind of freedom that success had gradually removed:

the freedom not to turn every period of life into output.

### Identity After Profession

The deeper question is therefore not merely whether Teoman will return to music.

It is whether **Teoman exists without music**.

For fifteen years, profession and identity had become increasingly difficult to separate.

Music determined schedule.

Public image.

Social environment.

Income.

Media attention.

Even ordinary behavior could be interpreted through the Teoman persona.

Stopping creates the possibility of separation.

### Fame as Employment

Earlier interviews described fame as a psychological prison.

By 2012, that idea has become practical.

A famous musician is not merely known.

He has responsibilities created by being known.

Promoters expect concerts.

Record companies expect releases.

Media outlets expect access.

Audiences expect continuity.

A successful identity becomes an institution.

Retirement is partly an attempt to resign from that institution.

### The Strange Luxury of Boredom

The empty months also give Teoman something successful people rarely allow themselves:

boredom.

At first, boredom can look like failure.

But it also creates space.

Without constant external demands, attention can begin moving somewhere unexpected.

The difficulty is tolerating the period before something new becomes interesting.

### Age Forty and the End of Magic

This period belongs to a broader transformation in Teoman's relationship with age.

The early interviews already showed anxiety about aging.

At thirty-two, he called himself old.

At thirty-three, he discussed inevitable decline.

By his forties, the problem becomes deeper.

The emotional intensity associated with youth is becoming harder to reproduce.

The question is no longer whether the body is aging.

It is whether life still contains surprise.

### Leaving Before Becoming a Caricature

The decision can also be read beside his 2007 comments about celebrities becoming caricatures of themselves.

The successful public figure discovers a recognizable performance and repeats it because the audience expects it.

Eventually repetition replaces personality.

Teoman had already identified this danger.

Leaving music gives him one possible escape:

stop performing the character before the character completely replaces the person.

### What Retirement Does Not Mean

The interview does not present Teoman as someone who suddenly hates music.

Nor does it prove that the decision must be permanent.

The more accurate description is distance.

He needs to know what music feels like when it is no longer compulsory.

This is very different from simply announcing that creativity has disappeared.

### A Temporary Death of Teoman

Symbolically, the 2011 withdrawal resembles a small death.

The musician who spent years thinking about mortality removes his public identity while remaining physically present.

People continue listening to the songs.

The catalogue continues existing.

The artist steps away.

This unintentionally tests something he had said in 2007:

he wanted the songs to live independently of him.

For a period, they do exactly that.

### The Catalogue Without the Artist

Nothing happens to *Paramparça* when Teoman stops giving concerts.

Nothing happens to *Gönülçelen*.

The songs continue circulating.

New listeners discover them.

Old listeners continue attaching memories to them.

The retirement therefore demonstrates that Teoman's work has already acquired a life beyond his immediate professional activity.

### Future Plans

The interview does not close Teoman's future.

That is essential.

He is not presenting retirement as a perfectly designed second life.

He is allowing uncertainty.

Future projects remain possible.

Writing.

Cinema.

Perhaps music again.

The difference is that he does not want the next move to emerge automatically from the machinery of the previous career.

### Teoman 2.0

GQ's accompanying material describes this post-retirement version playfully as **“Teoman 2.0.”**

The phrase is useful.

This is not exactly the disappearance of Teoman.

It is an attempted revision.

The first version was built around becoming successful.

The second must decide what success is for.

### Why This Interview Matters

This is one of the most important transition documents in the entire archive.

For years, Teoman had been talking about problems that appeared individually:

- success does not guarantee happiness,
- fame creates a restrictive public identity,
- career repetition can reduce curiosity,
- aging changes emotional intensity,
- achieving old dreams leaves the problem of finding new ones,
- and an artist should know when public performance begins turning into caricature.

In 2011, those ideas stop being theoretical.

Teoman acts on them.

He leaves.

The 2012 GQ interview allows us to see what happens immediately afterward.

There is no dramatic revelation.

No instant second career.

No perfect answer.

There is simply space.

And perhaps that is the point.

The Teoman of 1996 wanted control over his music.

The Teoman of 2000 wanted success without being controlled by success.

The Teoman of 2007 wanted his songs to survive him.

The Teoman of 2012 tries something more radical:

**he removes himself from the career to discover whether there is still a person underneath it.**
