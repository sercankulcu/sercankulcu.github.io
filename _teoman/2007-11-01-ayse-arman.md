## Ayşe Arman ile Röportaj

**Hürriyet — November 2007**  
**Interview by Ayşe Arman**

This is one of the most biographically revealing interviews in the Teoman archive.

Ayşe Arman moves behind the familiar public image and asks about the structures that shaped Teoman long before fame: the death of his father, the women who raised him, childhood loneliness, books, fear, relationships and his complicated sense of masculinity.

The interview also contains one of Teoman's clearest statements about what he ultimately wants from music.

He does not primarily want to defeat his contemporaries.

He wants his songs to survive him.

### “Babamın Öldüğü Yaştayım”

The conversation begins from one of Teoman's best-known lines, from *Paramparça*:

> “Bir bar taburesi üstünde babamın öldüğü yaştayım.”

His father died when Teoman was only two and a half years old.

His father was thirty-three.

The line had already turned this biographical fact into one of the most recognizable moments in Teoman's songwriting.

But here Teoman explains the reality behind it in much greater depth.

### Two Memories of His Father

Teoman says he has only two clear visual memories of his father.

In one, he is crying and his father comes toward him, lifts him and makes him feel completely safe.

In the other, his father is ill in bed and coughing.

These two images are almost unbearably compressed.

Safety.

Illness.

Presence.

Disappearance.

They may help explain why the father figure in Teoman's emotional world is simultaneously comforting and connected with death.

### Growing Up with Injustice

Teoman describes losing his father as a major trauma.

As a child, he experienced it as an injustice.

Why him?

Why did other children have fathers while he did not?

Later, the direction of that anger changed.

Instead of thinking only that something unfair had happened to him, he began thinking that something unfair had happened to his father.

His father had died extremely young.

The loss therefore became not only deprivation but interrupted possibility.

### Creating the Father Himself

Because Teoman barely knew his father directly, much of his image had to be reconstructed.

Relatives described him.

Books remained behind.

Stories survived.

Teoman says, in effect, that he filled in the missing parts himself.

This is one of the most important biographical ideas in the interview.

His father is both real and imagined.

Teoman inherited fragments and built a person from them.

This process is strangely similar to the way he later creates characters in songs.

Reality provides pieces.

Imagination creates continuity.

### His Father's Books

Books became one of the ways Teoman could approach the father he had lost.

He describes him as intellectual, thoughtful and elegant.

His father's reading habits mattered.

Goethe is mentioned among the writers he liked.

For a child who could not grow up in conversation with his father, the books left behind became another kind of conversation.

This may partly explain the unusually strong place literature later occupied in Teoman's identity.

### His Mother’s Mourning

Teoman's mother was still very young when her husband died.

According to Teoman, her mourning lasted decades.

He describes the first years as particularly severe and says the loss also affected her health.

This means Teoman did not experience his father's death only as the disappearance of one parent.

He also grew up inside the continuing emotional consequences of that disappearance.

Death remained present in the household long after the funeral.

### Fear of Death

By 2007, Teoman is very open about his own relationship with death.

He says he cannot tolerate funerals, religious memorial rituals or even the thought of people close to him dying.

He worries about his mother's driving.

He worries when friends ride motorcycles.

The public image may suggest a reckless rock musician.

Teoman describes almost the opposite.

He sees himself as cautious.

### The Supposedly Dangerous Teoman

This difference between image and reality appears repeatedly.

From the outside, Teoman may look like someone living dangerously.

Alcohol.

Nightlife.

Bars.

Rock music.

Celebrity.

But he describes himself as someone who undergoes medical examinations and worries excessively about risk.

This is another example of the distance between **Teoman the media character** and the individual describing himself.

### Raised by Women

After his father's death, Teoman grew up primarily around his mother, aunt and grandmother.

He describes them as Republican women: elegant, Western-oriented and strongly concerned with manners and appearance.

The household had rules.

How to sit.

How to dress.

How loudly to speak.

What combinations of clothing were acceptable.

Teoman humorously calls his mother’s attitude something close to “style fascism.”

### The Dressing-Gown Child

One of the funniest images is Teoman describing himself as a child who wore a dressing gown at home.

Pajamas alone were considered inappropriate.

He later discovered that other children did not necessarily live this way.

The anecdote reveals a childhood somewhere between privilege, protection and unusual formality.

He describes himself as neither completely a street child nor completely sheltered.

He was, in his own phrase, something like a **salon child**.

### Masculinity Without a Father

This upbringing created an unusual structure.

Teoman learned many of his ideas about how a man should behave from women.

His mother, aunt and grandmother taught him what they considered proper masculinity:

restraint,

elegance,

good clothing,

controlled behavior,

and avoidance of vulgarity.

This is important because the public Teoman would later be represented as a rebellious or dissolute male figure.

His actual childhood training was surprisingly formal.

### Loneliness as the Dominant Theme

Asked whether he was a lonely child who lived in his imagination, Teoman answers immediately that loneliness was the dominant theme.

Books became one of his solutions.

Once he learned to read, he began reading constantly.

One of his greatest pleasures was closing the curtains, lying down and disappearing into a book.

This gives the loneliness of later Teoman songs a deeper chronology.

It did not begin with celebrity.

It predates music.

### Curiosity

Teoman also remembers himself as intellectually curious.

Before university, he was a strong student.

At Boğaziçi he became less disciplined academically, but he remained interested in the material itself.

He says he might read the textbooks before the academic year even started.

This distinction is useful.

He was not necessarily motivated by grades.

He was motivated by curiosity.

### Women

Because he grew up almost entirely around women, Teoman says he understands women's behavior more easily than men's.

Most of his close friends are women.

He believes women are more likely to investigate emotions, question motives and force deeper conversations.

Men, in comparison, can seem emotionally simpler to him.

This does not prevent Teoman from admitting that his romantic relationships are difficult.

### “Zor Adamım”

Teoman describes himself without much generosity.

He says he is difficult.

His pride is easily hurt.

He becomes frightened.

He retreats.

He does not call.

He disappears.

He says that his real problem is often himself.

This continues one of the strongest themes throughout the archive:

**self-awareness without automatic self-improvement.**

Teoman can diagnose his own behavior quite accurately.

Diagnosis does not necessarily cure it.

### A Bad Partner

He also admits that he may not be a particularly good boyfriend.

The other person can be hurt by his selfishness.

He feels guilty when that happens.

But the same patterns may continue.

Again, he does not present emotional intelligence as evidence of emotional competence.

Knowing why a relationship fails is not the same as knowing how to make it succeed.

### Shyness and Boldness

The opening idea of Ayşe Arman's portrait captures another contradiction.

Teoman cannot decide whether he is shy or shameless.

In some situations he seems completely unaffected by embarrassment.

In everyday emotional situations, however, he can be intensely shy.

He even says that without alcohol he would find it very difficult to make the first romantic move.

The apparent contradiction is useful.

Public boldness and private courage are not necessarily the same thing.

### Fame as Imprisonment

One of the most perceptive sections concerns celebrity.

Teoman says that ordinary people are allowed to be many different people.

A person can be cheerful one day, withdrawn the next, elegant somewhere and ridiculous somewhere else.

A celebrity is not given the same freedom.

The public expects consistency.

Once a public character has been created, the celebrity is expected to perform it repeatedly.

Eventually the person becomes a caricature of himself.

This is one of Teoman's clearest descriptions of the psychological problem of fame.

### You Have to Keep Playing Teoman

The paradox is brutal.

Becoming famous means becoming recognizable.

Being recognizable means developing a public identity.

But once that identity becomes fixed, variation begins to look like contradiction.

The audience wants **Teoman**.

The real person may want to be somebody else that afternoon.

Fame therefore transforms identity into a professional obligation.

### “Bana Rockçı Deme”

The interview also contains an important rejection of the “rockçı” label.

Teoman believes Turkish discussions of rock often give the genre an exaggerated moral prestige.

Rock becomes associated not merely with musical form but automatically with rebellion, left-wing politics, humanism and authenticity.

Teoman distrusts this mythology.

He does not feel a strong need to belong to the social image associated with the Turkish rock scene.

### Escaping Labels

This continues his earlier resistance to musical purity.

By 2007 the position is very clear:

he does not want labels to explain him.

Rock.

Pop.

Celebrity.

Intellectual.

Womanizer.

Lonely man.

Each may capture something.

None should become the whole person.

His resistance to categories mirrors his complaint about fame itself.

Categories turn complicated people into recognizable characters.

### His Real Competition

When Ayşe Arman asks what he wants to achieve in music, Teoman gives one of the strongest artistic statements in the entire archive.

He does not measure himself primarily against current Turkish chart musicians.

Instead, he asks whether he can write something as good as Leonard Cohen or perhaps someday approach Bob Dylan.

This is not modest ambition.

His reference group is international songwriting.

### Songs That Survive Their Author

More important than competition is the final objective.

Teoman wants his songs to become classics.

He wants them to exist independently after he is gone.

This changes the meaning of success completely.

Album sales matter now.

Fame matters now.

Concerts matter now.

But a classic song belongs to a different timescale.

Its real test begins when the singer is no longer necessary.

### Death Returns Through Art

This ambition is particularly interesting in an interview dominated by the death of Teoman's father.

His father died young but survived inside stories, books, memories and eventually songs.

Teoman wants his own work to survive him in a more deliberate way.

The connection need not be conscious to be meaningful.

A person preoccupied with disappearance is trying to make objects that remain.

### Why This Interview Matters

This is one of the central biographical documents in the Teoman archive.

It connects many elements that appeared separately in earlier conversations:

- the father's early death,
- fear of mortality,
- childhood loneliness,
- literature,
- being raised by women,
- unusual ideas about masculinity,
- difficulty in relationships,
- self-analysis,
- the difference between private identity and public persona,
- resistance to the “rock” label,
- and the ambition to make songs that outlive their creator.

Most importantly, it helps explain why death appears so persistently in Teoman's songs.

Death was not introduced into his imagination by rock music.

It was there almost from the beginning.

The child lost his father.

The household continued mourning.

The child learned to live alone with books.

The adult became famous.

And eventually the songwriter began asking another question:

**What can remain after the person disappears?**

His answer is increasingly clear.

The songs.
