## Bir Teoman Röportajı

**Sabah / Hafif Müzik — 12 April 2009**  
**Interview by Mehmet Tez**

This interview is one of the broadest and most provocative Teoman conversations from the late 2000s.

Mehmet Tez visits Teoman at his home in Cihangir shortly after the release of *İnsanlık Halleri*.

The conversation begins with the new album but quickly expands.

Politics.

The Kurdish question.

Ergenekon.

Sex.

Fidelity.

Prostitution.

Suicide.

Yavuz Çetin.

Cinema.

Poetry.

And Teoman's increasingly clear belief that his existing way of living may eventually have to change.

The result is a portrait of Teoman just before several major transitions in his life.

### At Home in Cihangir

The interview opens inside Teoman's apartment.

The setting matters.

This is not a backstage interview or a formal hotel conversation.

Teoman is at home, making an extremely simple meal and looking out over the Bosphorus.

Mehmet Tez portrays him almost as the ruler of a small independent republic.

The metaphor is humorous, but accurate in one important sense:

Teoman has constructed a life in which he is unusually self-sufficient.

### The Teoman Republic

By 2009, Teoman has enough professional and financial independence to organize much of life according to his own preferences.

This is an early version of something that becomes even more visible in his later interviews.

The ultimate reward of success is autonomy.

He can create his own routines.

His own social world.

His own rules.

The young musician who fought for control over his first album has gradually expanded that desire for control into everyday life.

### Fidelity

One of the first personal subjects is fidelity.

Teoman admits that faithfulness is not something he has fully internalized.

He does not present the statement as rebellion.

He simply describes it as something psychologically foreign to him.

This is remarkably consistent with what he would say fourteen years later in *Umarım Annem Dinlemez*.

The continuity is important.

His later comments on fidelity were not a new provocative pose.

The same attitude was already documented in 2009.

### Relationships Outside Conventional Rules

Teoman's approach to relationships repeatedly resists standardized expectations.

Love does not automatically imply conventional partnership.

Partnership does not automatically produce exclusivity.

Emotional attachment and sexual behavior are not necessarily identical.

Whether one agrees with this position or not, it appears remarkably stable across decades.

### Politics

The political sections are especially valuable because they capture Teoman's thinking during a period of considerable political change in Turkey.

He says he does not vote in local elections and is uncertain about whom he would support nationally.

Yet he is far from politically uninterested.

He follows developments closely.

His attitude toward the contemporary government is more complicated than later readers might expect.

### Ergenekon

Teoman says he is pleased that figures such as Veli Küçük and Kemal Kerinçsiz are being investigated and detained in the Ergenekon process.

From his 2009 perspective, this appears to represent the weakening of forces associated with aggressive nationalism and the old state establishment.

This is an important historical document because political interpretations of the Ergenekon trials would change dramatically in later years.

The interview preserves Teoman's position at that specific moment.

### The Importance of Historical Context

This is precisely why chronological interview archives matter.

A statement should not automatically be read through knowledge acquired years later.

In April 2009, Teoman is reacting to the information, political atmosphere and expectations available to him then.

The archive should preserve that temporal context rather than judge the statement as if it were made today.

### May Day

Teoman also comments positively on the announcement that 1 May would become an official holiday.

Again, his response is pragmatic.

Something that had produced years of political conflict could apparently be solved by a relatively straightforward government decision.

The observation reinforces his suspicion that Turkish politics often turns solvable questions into symbolic battles.

### “Kürdistan Dediği Anda Kurtulacağız”

The interview contains one of Teoman's most provocative political remarks.

He jokes about the intense controversy surrounding whether Abdullah Gül had or had not used the word “Kürdistan.”

Teoman's point is that enormous political anxiety has been attached to a word.

He suggests that perhaps simply saying it would remove some of its symbolic power.

### Words and Political Fear

This is an interesting linguistic argument.

A forbidden word can become more powerful because of the prohibition surrounding it.

Once spoken normally, it may become less threatening.

Teoman's position is not a complete political program for the Kurdish question.

It is an observation about taboo.

Fear can be preserved by language restrictions.

### Hope for the Future

Perhaps the biggest difference between the 2009 Teoman and the 2016 Teoman is optimism.

In this interview, Teoman genuinely believes several major problems might be moving toward resolution.

Kurdish issue.

Armenian issue.

Cyprus.

He sees Barack Obama's arrival in the United States as part of a broader international shift and imagines that Turkey may benefit from the same atmosphere.

He says:

**hope has come to him.**

### The Lost Optimism

This makes the interview particularly valuable when placed beside 2016.

Seven years later, Teoman would tell Işıl Cinmen that he had little hope Turkish people could learn to like one another.

The contrast is dramatic.

2009 Teoman:

**Maybe these problems can finally be solved.**

2016 Teoman:

**I am tired and pessimistic about this society.**

The archive allows us to see exactly how large that change was.

### Politics Without Party Identity

Even while discussing political events positively, Teoman avoids attaching himself firmly to a political party.

This is another long-term pattern.

He prefers evaluations of individual developments rather than stable partisan identity.

The same instinct would later become his language of standing on the side of “conscience” rather than a political camp.

### Fahişe

The conversation moves from politics to one of the songs on *İnsanlık Halleri*: *Fahişe*.

Teoman says prostitution has long interested him as a subject in art.

He is aware of the enormous literary and cultural history surrounding prostitutes as characters.

Again, the interest is not simply sexual.

It is narrative.

The prostitute occupies a particular symbolic position in literature and urban life.

### Outsiders

This fits Teoman's broader attraction to marginal characters.

People outside ordinary respectability often possess greater narrative intensity.

They reveal hypocrisies inside conventional morality.

They also occupy environments Teoman has repeatedly explored:

nightlife,

urban isolation,

transactional relationships,

and the border between intimacy and distance.

### Morality and Curiosity

The interview is typical in the way Teoman approaches taboo subjects.

He rarely begins from moral condemnation.

His first instinct is curiosity.

Why does this exist?

What kind of person lives inside this situation?

What does society say publicly?

What happens privately?

This sociological curiosity often produces statements that appear provocative when removed from context.

### Suicide

The conversation also reaches suicide.

This is one of the darker subjects in Teoman's history because of people he knew personally, especially Yavuz Çetin.

Teoman's comments should not be read as romanticizing suicide.

Rather, he is trying to understand how extreme suffering changes a person's relationship with death.

### Yavuz Çetin Again

Yavuz Çetin had already appeared in the 2001 *Gönülçelen* interview.

Teoman had said there that Çetin's death did not entirely surprise him because he knew how deeply his friend was suffering.

Eight years later, the subject remains emotionally present.

This reinforces the reliability of the earlier interview and shows how persistent the event was in Teoman's thinking.

### Death as a Permanent Subject

Death has now followed Teoman through several stages of the archive:

his father's death,

friends dying young,

Yavuz Çetin,

songs about mortality,

fear of funerals,

and reflections on suicide.

By 2009, death is no longer merely a lyrical theme.

It is part of the biographical structure through which Teoman interprets life.

### İnsanlık Halleri

The immediate musical context is *İnsanlık Halleri*.

The album title is appropriate for this interview.

Teoman talks about a wide range of human states rather than a single coherent philosophy.

Desire.

Fear.

Politics.

Sex.

Death.

Hope.

Isolation.

The album itself similarly moves across different conditions of ordinary human experience.

### Cinema

Cinema remains an important ambition.

Teoman had already directed *Balans ve Manevra* and continued thinking in cinematic terms.

His interest in storytelling has clearly expanded beyond songs.

Yet at this point, film still exists beside music rather than replacing it.

The 2012 retirement period would later test whether cinema could become the main activity.

The answer would be no.

### Poetry

The conversation also touches on poetry.

This is unsurprising considering Teoman's long-term obsession with language.

He repeatedly borrows from literary culture, works with poets and treats lyrics as texts worthy of attention independent of melody.

By this stage, the boundary between songwriter and literary reader is already extremely thin.

### A Lifestyle That Cannot Continue Forever

One of the most interesting underlying themes is Teoman's awareness that his lifestyle may eventually need to change.

He is forty-two.

Still single.

Still living within patterns strongly associated with the younger rock-star identity.

Nightlife.

Irregular hours.

Relationships.

Alcohol.

Freedom from conventional domestic structures.

But time is beginning to exert pressure.

### The Aging of a Lifestyle

This is slightly different from fear of physical aging.

The real issue is whether a lifestyle that works at thirty can continue working at fifty.

Even if the person remains physically capable, the emotional meaning changes.

The same behavior can gradually shift from freedom to repetition.

Teoman is beginning to sense this.

### Before the Crisis

This makes the 2009 interview historically important.

It takes place only about two years before Teoman leaves music.

The later crisis is not yet explicit.

He is still hopeful politically.

Still actively releasing albums.

Still functioning comfortably inside the rock-star life.

But several tensions are visible:

repetition,

mortality,

relationship structure,

identity,

and the question of whether life needs to change.

### The Last Optimistic Teoman?

Politically, this may be one of the last major interviews in which Teoman sounds genuinely optimistic about Turkey's future.

That alone gives the record unusual historical value.

Later interviews become much more pessimistic.

The transformation did not happen only inside Teoman.

The country around him changed too.

### Why This Interview Matters

This interview fills a major gap between the 2008 *Söz Müzik Teoman* period and the much more reflective 2010–2012 Teoman.

It records:

- Teoman's unusually hopeful political outlook in 2009,
- his contemporary interpretation of the Ergenekon process,
- his desire for progress on the Kurdish, Armenian and Cyprus questions,
- his resistance to political taboos,
- his long-standing unconventional attitude toward fidelity,
- his interest in prostitution as a literary and social subject,
- continuing reflections on suicide and Yavuz Çetin,
- his interest in cinema and poetry,
- and the first signs that the rock-star lifestyle may eventually need revision.

The interview is also a reminder that Teoman's political thought did not develop in a straight line.

The 2016 pessimist was once hopeful.

The 2024 advocate of social reconciliation once believed reconciliation might already be approaching.

The chronology therefore prevents us from flattening Teoman into one permanent political position.

People change.

Countries change.

The meaning of old optimism changes with them.
