## Teoman, Fatih Altaylı'nın Sorularını Yanıtladı

**Fatih Altaylı YouTube — 25 August 2023**  
**Interview by Fatih Altaylı**  
**Duration: approximately 1 hour 5 minutes**

This interview is one of the most important political and philosophical conversations of Teoman's later period.

The immediate context is musical.

Teoman has a new album, *Ben, Zargana, Deus Ex Machina*.

But the discussion quickly moves beyond music.

The more important questions concern Turkey, secularism, conservatism, social reconciliation, aging, artistic legacy and the permanent restlessness Teoman believes he carries inside himself.

### Is This the Last Album?

Fatih Altaylı begins with the new album and asks whether it might be Teoman's final one.

The question is natural.

Teoman has been talking about endings for more than two decades.

He once left music entirely.

He later returned.

He has repeatedly said that writing new songs interests him less than it once did.

The possibility of a final album therefore does not sound theatrical.

It belongs to a very long conversation Teoman has been having with himself about when an artist should stop.

### Leaving and Returning

Teoman again discusses the period when he quit music.

The decision now looks less dramatic from a distance.

He had become tired.

The profession had become repetitive.

The life he had once worked hard to achieve had begun to resemble a machine.

Eventually he returned because completely abandoning music did not solve everything either.

This is important.

Leaving was not the final answer.

Returning was not a return to innocence.

Both experiences became information.

### Changing Musical Style

Teoman also discusses why he changes musical approaches.

At this point in his career, repetition is one of the main dangers.

A musician with a recognizable style can easily continue reproducing it.

The audience may even reward that repetition.

But for Teoman, familiarity can become boredom.

Changing form is therefore partly a way of protecting his own interest.

### “Serseri Teoman” and “Şefkatli Teoman”

Fatih Altaylı frames one section through two apparently different versions of Teoman:

the rebellious or irresponsible **“serseri Teoman”**

and the increasingly compassionate **“şefkatli Teoman.”**

The distinction is useful, but Teoman does not present the later self as a complete replacement of the earlier one.

The public persona has changed because age changes emphasis.

Characteristics that were once hidden by the rock-star image can become more visible.

### The Persona Becomes Less Useful

When younger, rebelliousness helped create a recognizable public figure.

It distinguished Teoman.

At fifty-plus, performing the same rebellion would risk becoming exactly what he once feared:

a caricature.

The later Teoman therefore allows other qualities to become visible.

Compassion.

Concern about society.

Fatherhood.

Responsibility.

The character becomes wider.

### The Necip Fazıl Controversy

A major portion of the interview concerns Teoman's public reference to Necip Fazıl Kısakürek.

The choice surprised many people because Teoman's public identity has historically been associated with secular urban culture, while Necip Fazıl became one of the central intellectual symbols of Turkish political Islam.

Teoman explains that the point was not ideological conversion.

He was interested in reaching across a cultural division.

### “Kendi Vatanında Parya”

Teoman was particularly affected by Necip Fazıl's phrase about becoming a pariah in one's own country.

He does not endorse all of Necip Fazıl's politics.

On the contrary, he explicitly criticizes what he sees as the writer's harsh and vindictive ideological positions.

But one does not have to accept an entire political worldview in order to recognize emotional truth inside one sentence.

This distinction is central to Teoman's argument.

### Understanding the Other Side

The broader goal is social reconciliation.

Teoman believes secular and conservative groups in Turkey have spent too long viewing one another through simplified stereotypes.

He wants to understand how conservative people experienced earlier periods of the Republic.

This does not require agreeing with all conservative politics.

It requires recognizing that another group's historical sense of exclusion may contain real experiences.

### Boğaziçi and Empathy

Teoman connects this attitude partly with Boğaziçi University.

He says his education encouraged him to look at social questions from different perspectives.

This is consistent with his sociology background.

A sociological explanation is not the same thing as ideological approval.

Understanding why a group thinks in a particular way does not require becoming part of that group.

### The Irony of Boğaziçi

There is also a bitter irony in Teoman's discussion.

The institution he says taught him to understand conservative people later became itself a major site of conflict with conservative political power.

That contradiction troubles him.

The lesson was supposed to be pluralism.

The later political conflict appears to him as evidence that pluralism remains fragile.

### Secularism

Teoman's position is direct:

he wants Turkey to remain secular.

This is not presented as hostility toward religious people.

For him, secularism is precisely the framework that allows different ways of life to coexist.

The state should not force one cultural or religious model onto everyone.

### Not Anti-Conservative

At the same time, Teoman rejects the idea that supporting secularism requires contempt for conservatives.

This is one of the interview's central arguments.

A secular person who believes conservative citizens are socially inferior has already damaged the possibility of coexistence.

Political disagreement does not justify cultural humiliation.

### Class and the Secular Left

Teoman directs criticism toward parts of the secular and left-wing world as well.

He argues that some people who consider themselves progressive can still look down on conservative or poorer citizens.

This class dimension matters.

The cultural conflict in Turkey is not simply religious.

It is also connected with education, geography, lifestyle and class.

### The Need for Reconciliation

By 2023, Teoman sounds less interested in choosing one political camp than in reducing hostility between them.

He does not imagine himself as a professional political actor.

But he does think public figures can sometimes help create a language of reconciliation.

This is a development from the 2016 Tempo interview.

Then, he sounded deeply pessimistic about whether Turkish people could learn to like one another.

Seven years later, the pessimism remains.

But he is making a more explicit attempt to speak across the divide.

### Young Listeners

Teoman is aware that younger people listen to him.

This gives him a kind of cultural responsibility he did not necessarily seek.

He wonders what sort of influence an older artist should leave behind.

The question is no longer only musical.

Young listeners may also absorb attitudes.

How to disagree.

How to view people from another cultural group.

How to grow older without becoming completely rigid.

### Legacy

Fatih Altaylı asks directly about permanence.

This has been one of Teoman's central concerns since at least 2007, when he told Ayşe Arman that he wanted his songs to become classics and survive after him.

By 2023, the problem is even more concrete.

The catalogue exists.

Some songs have already survived decades.

Teoman can now begin seeing which parts of his ambition are actually happening.

### The Artist's Second Life

A song has one life when it is new.

It has another when younger people discover it decades later.

At that point, the original cultural context becomes less important.

The song must survive without the excitement surrounding its release.

This is the kind of permanence Teoman has long wanted.

### Aging and Religion

Altaylı also asks whether Teoman is becoming more religious as he gets older.

The question reflects a familiar cultural assumption:

people become more religious as death approaches.

Teoman's answer is more complicated.

Aging may increase interest in mortality and meaning.

But this does not automatically produce conventional religious belief.

### Mortality Without Conversion

Teoman has been thinking about death since childhood.

His father's death made mortality part of his emotional world long before old age.

So the subject itself is not new.

What changes with age is proximity.

Death becomes less theoretical.

But proximity does not necessarily resolve uncertainty.

### Istanbul

The question of whether Teoman might leave Istanbul also appears.

Again, the answer connects with decades of interviews.

Istanbul is not merely where he works.

It is the geography of his biography.

Childhood.

Beyoğlu.

Music.

Relationships.

Memory.

Songs.

Leaving the city permanently would therefore mean more than changing address.

### A City That Has Become Part of the Self

Teoman has criticized Istanbul many times.

Crowds.

Traffic.

Transformation.

Loss.

Yet he repeatedly returns to it.

This resembles his relationship with Turkey more generally.

Attachment survives criticism.

Home does not have to be ideal in order to remain home.

### “There Is a Restlessness in Me That Will Never Go Away”

Near the end of the interview, Teoman gives perhaps the most important statement of the entire conversation:

> “Benim içimde hiçbir zaman geçmeyecek bir huzursuzluk var.”

This sentence could serve as a summary of the complete archive.

Success did not remove the restlessness.

Love did not remove it.

Leaving music did not remove it.

Returning did not remove it.

Age did not remove it.

Projects can occupy it.

Relationships can soften it.

But Teoman increasingly believes the underlying condition is permanent.

### From Cure to Acceptance

This represents a major philosophical development.

The younger Teoman often seemed to search for something that might finally resolve dissatisfaction.

Success.

Love.

A different life.

A child.

Retirement.

A new project.

The older Teoman increasingly stops imagining complete cure.

Some internal structures may simply remain.

The task is not to eliminate them.

It is to build a life around them.

### The Importance of This Acceptance

Acceptance can sound pessimistic.

But it can also create freedom.

If permanent restlessness is part of the structure, then Teoman no longer has to interpret every recurrence as evidence that he chose the wrong life.

The feeling can exist without requiring another radical transformation.

This is a much calmer position than the one that produced the 2011 retirement.

### Love

Fatih Altaylı ends by asking about Teoman's romantic life.

The subject that once dominated tabloid coverage now occupies only a small part of a long conversation.

That change is itself revealing.

Relationships remain part of Teoman's life.

But they no longer define the public story to the same degree.

The hierarchy of subjects has changed.

### From Romance to Society

Compare the young Teoman interviews.

Journalists repeatedly asked about women.

Relationships.

Nightlife.

Celebrity.

In 2023, the central subjects are:

social reconciliation,

secularism,

cultural division,

legacy,

aging,

young people,

and mortality.

The individual has moved outward again.

### Why This Interview Matters

This interview is one of the clearest late-period statements of Teoman's social and philosophical position.

Several long-running themes converge:

- music is no longer an automatic obligation,
- repetition remains the enemy,
- the old rock-star persona is becoming less useful,
- Teoman wants Turkey to remain secular,
- secularism should not become contempt for conservative citizens,
- cultural and class arrogance damage social reconciliation,
- political understanding does not require ideological agreement,
- younger listeners create a sense of responsibility,
- artistic permanence matters more than immediate success,
- Istanbul remains part of Teoman's identity,
- and the internal restlessness he spent decades trying to solve may simply be permanent.

That final realization may be the most important.

The twenty-six-year-old Teoman imagined a future.

The thirty-three-year-old Teoman achieved much of it and discovered dissatisfaction.

The forty-four-year-old Teoman walked away.

The fifty-year-old Teoman began organizing the past.

The fifty-six-year-old Teoman reaches a quieter conclusion:

**perhaps the problem was never finding the right external life.**

Perhaps some of the dissatisfaction was always going to come along.

That does not make life meaningless.

It simply changes the task.

The goal is no longer to become a person without restlessness.

It is to become someone who knows what to do with it.
