## Best FM Interview

**Best FM — 2000**

This radio interview belongs to the period immediately after the release of *Onyedi*, when Teoman's popularity was increasing dramatically.

Unlike many newspaper profiles from the same period, this conversation allows Teoman to explain his own artistic logic in considerable detail.

He talks about the relationship between the songs on *Onyedi*, the unexpected success of the album, his attitude toward sales and music videos, the gap between achievement and happiness, and the role of irony in his melancholy.

It is one of the clearest early documents of how Teoman understood an album as a complete work rather than simply a collection of songs.

### One Album, One Story

One of the strongest ideas in the interview is Teoman's insistence that the songs on *Onyedi* should not be understood independently.

For him, *Paramparça*, *Onyedi*, *Rüzgar Gülü*, *Zamparanın Ölümü*, *Sürpriz* and the other songs belong to the same emotional world.

He says that he almost experiences them as a single song.

A phrase appearing in one track may answer an idea expressed somewhere else.

A character introduced in one song may help explain another.

This suggests that Teoman was already thinking in terms of **album architecture**.

The individual song matters, but the deeper meaning appears when the songs are placed next to one another.

### The Album as a Whole

This connects closely with something Teoman had said in another 2000 interview: creating an album is different from simply writing individual songs.

An album needs an atmosphere.

It needs internal consistency.

It should leave a particular emotional residue after all the songs have been heard.

In the Best FM conversation, this idea becomes especially visible.

The songs function almost like chapters in a book.

Different narrators, memories and emotional states belong to the same world.

That makes *Onyedi* particularly important in understanding Teoman's artistic development.

### Small Details

The interviewer points to the ordinary details inside Teoman's lyrics.

One example is the answering machine in *Paramparça*.

The image is extremely small compared with the song's larger themes of death, memory and loneliness.

Yet Teoman explains that these details come from ordinary observation.

People often become uncomfortable when leaving messages on answering machines.

They hesitate.

They do not know what to say.

Even Teoman admits that he experiences the same difficulty.

This gives us a useful principle for understanding his songwriting:

**emotional realism is often created through small ordinary details.**

The sadness becomes convincing because it exists beside something mundane.

### The Seventeen-Year-Old Teoman

One of the most revealing moments comes when the interviewer asks whether Teoman has become the person he imagined at seventeen.

His answer is essentially yes.

Professionally, he has achieved more than he expected.

But there is a problem.

The things that seventeen-year-old Teoman believed would make him happy do not necessarily make the adult Teoman happy.

This distinction is crucial:

**achievement and happiness are not the same thing.**

The younger Teoman had imagined happiness as something located behind future accomplishments.

Become a musician.

Make albums.

Become successful.

Be recognized.

The adult Teoman has achieved these things and discovered that the emotional equation does not work so simply.

### Success Without Happiness

This idea appears repeatedly throughout Teoman's later life, but here we see one of its earliest clear formulations.

Success can satisfy an old ambition.

It cannot automatically tell a person what to want next.

Teoman can look backward and feel satisfaction because he has fulfilled the dreams of his younger self.

But looking forward is more difficult.

He has to invent new reasons to remain interested in life.

This becomes one of the central existential problems of the interview.

### Looking for Something New

Teoman openly says that he is having difficulty finding new things to do.

Albums exist.

Concerts exist.

Success exists.

But these activities occupy only part of life.

A concert may be emotionally powerful, but it lasts only a few hours.

The remaining hours still have to be filled.

He wants something that can occupy his mind beyond the professional routine he has already mastered.

This is an important moment because it appears very early in his career.

Teoman has not yet reached the later stage when he would temporarily leave music.

But the psychological structure that would eventually make withdrawal imaginable is already visible.

### Melancholy and Irony

Another important idea concerns sadness.

Teoman does not simply describe himself as melancholic.

He describes a method for living with melancholy.

If life feels painful, he says, one can introduce irony.

You make jokes about the things that hurt.

You break the emotional weight by creating distance from it.

This is useful for understanding both Teoman's interviews and his songs.

His melancholy is rarely completely solemn.

There is often a joke nearby.

A cynical observation.

A contradiction.

A character making himself ridiculous.

Humor prevents sadness from becoming sentimental.

### Irony as Defense

This makes irony more than a stylistic preference.

It becomes a psychological defense.

The person experiencing pain can stand outside himself for a moment and observe the situation.

Once he becomes an observer, the pain changes shape.

It may still exist, but it becomes manageable.

This combination of melancholy and self-mockery would remain one of Teoman's most recognizable characteristics for decades.

### Album Sales

By the time of the interview, *Onyedi* was selling extremely well.

The interviewer describes the album as number one.

Teoman deliberately distances himself from these numbers.

He does not want sales statistics to become part of his self-definition.

This does not mean he dislikes success.

Instead, he worries about what success might do to his decisions.

If sales begin determining artistic choices, then the logic of the record company can gradually replace his own.

### “I Don't Want the Numbers to Change Me”

Teoman's position is surprisingly clear.

He has already made the album he wanted to make.

After that, concerts and video clips partly belong to the machinery surrounding the record.

He says that he would rather think of himself as someone making videos because the record company wants them and giving concerts because listeners want them.

The creative act remains separate.

This allows him to protect an internal space where commercial success does not determine what the next song must become.

This concern connects directly with his earliest 1996 interview, where he had already emphasized artistic control.

Four years later, that concern has become more difficult.

He now has something commercially valuable to protect himself from:

**his own success.**

### The Visual World of *Onyedi*

The same artistic logic extends to the album cover.

Teoman wanted the record to sound old.

Older guitars and amplifiers were deliberately used to create this atmosphere.

Once the music had that character, a bright and contemporary album cover would have felt inconsistent.

The visual design therefore follows the musical concept.

The black-and-white photography and shadowed face are not independent marketing choices.

They belong to the same aesthetic system as the songs.

This again reveals Teoman thinking about the album as a complete object.

### Commercial Logic Versus Artistic Logic

The record company preferred clearer photographs.

A recognizable artist should ideally look directly at the consumer.

The face should be large.

The image should be bright.

Teoman wants almost the opposite.

Part of his face disappears into shadow.

The image is less immediately readable.

Commercial communication wants instant recognition.

Teoman prefers ambiguity.

This small disagreement over a photograph captures a much larger conflict that would follow him throughout his career:

**the industry wants clarity; the artist wants atmosphere.**

### A Cycle Instead of an Ending

Near the end of the conversation, the interviewer notices a possible emotional movement across the album.

It begins with *Paramparça*.

Different memories, relationships and forms of pain follow.

Eventually the album reaches *Sürpriz*.

Perhaps the surprise suggests some kind of renewal.

Teoman responds humorously that after *Sürpriz*, everything simply returns to *Paramparça* again.

This joke is more meaningful than it first appears.

The album does not necessarily describe a journey toward resolution.

It may describe a cycle.

Pain.

Memory.

Irony.

Temporary relief.

And then fragmentation again.

### Why This Interview Matters

This radio conversation is one of the strongest early sources for understanding Teoman's artistic method.

Several ideas appear clearly:

- songs inside an album communicate with one another,
- albums should possess conceptual unity,
- ordinary details create emotional realism,
- success does not guarantee happiness,
- melancholy can be controlled through irony,
- commercial success should not determine artistic decisions,
- visual design and musical atmosphere should belong to the same world,
- and reaching childhood dreams does not automatically solve the problem of what to want next.

The most important development, however, may be psychological.

In 1996, Teoman wanted the freedom to make his own record.

By 2000, he had achieved that freedom and much more.

Now a different problem had appeared:

**what happens after you become the person you once wanted to be?**

This question would follow Teoman for much of his later career.
