## Işıl Cinmen ile Koyu Antoloji Röportajı

**Posta — 20 January 2018**  
**Interview by Işıl Cinmen**

This is one of the darkest and most self-analytical Teoman interviews in the archive.

At fifty, Teoman has spent more than two decades as a professional musician. Instead of concentrating on making a completely new record, he has returned to twenty-six older songs and reconstructed them for *Koyu Antoloji*.

At the same time, another team has been going through twenty-one years of his interviews and press archive.

Teoman is also writing his memories.

The result is unusual.

For months, he is surrounded by different versions of himself.

The young Teoman.

The famous Teoman.

The unhappy Teoman.

The successful Teoman.

The man who thought he would change.

And the fifty-year-old who now has to decide whether he actually did.

### A Dark Anthology

The title *Koyu Antoloji* is not accidental.

The arrangements are darker.

The selected songs are dark.

The atmosphere is deliberately heavy.

But Teoman argues that this darkness is not simply an artistic costume.

It represents a part of ordinary human psychology that people often conceal.

Social media shows happy lives.

Private conversations reveal something else.

When people stop performing happiness, Teoman says he often encounters disappointment, anxiety and deep unhappiness.

The darkness of the album therefore belongs not only to him.

It is something he believes people generally carry while trying not to display it.

### The Dark Teoman

Işıl Cinmen asks what exists inside Teoman's own darkness.

His answer contains several recurring elements:

restlessness,

lack of purpose,

fear about the direction of the world,

boredom,

and loneliness.

This is familiar territory.

What has changed is Teoman's relationship with it.

As a younger songwriter, melancholy often functioned as creative material.

At fifty, he sounds tired of having to live inside the perspective that produces it.

### Darkness Is Not a Virtue

Teoman makes an important distinction.

He does not romanticize being dark.

He does not claim that unhappiness makes him deeper or morally superior.

In fact, he says he actively tries to escape this perspective.

He knows that he tends to concentrate on problems and overlook good things.

Changing that tendency requires conscious effort.

The irony is obvious.

The perspective he would like to weaken is also the perspective that produced many of his songs.

### The Artist's Problem

This creates a difficult question.

What happens when a psychological characteristic is both harmful to the person and useful to the artist?

Teoman's negative perspective causes dissatisfaction.

But it also gives him material.

If he became completely content, would the songs change?

Would they disappear?

The interview never solves this tension.

It simply acknowledges it.

### Looking Back at Twenty-One Years

Preparing *Koyu Antoloji* forces Teoman to study his own catalogue.

At almost the same time, his old interviews are being collected and his memoir is being written.

He therefore experiences his life again through three archives:

his songs,

his public statements,

and his memories.

This produces an unusually concentrated form of self-examination.

### “I Haven't Made Much Progress”

What Teoman finds is not particularly comforting.

He recognizes development.

Age has changed him.

Experience has changed him.

But many of the problems he intended to solve are still there.

The basic personality has proven more durable than expected.

This brings him close to the old Turkish saying that a person at seventy remains, in important ways, the person he was at seven.

### Self-Knowledge Without Transformation

This is one of the longest-running themes in the Teoman archive.

In 1999, he could already diagnose his own problems in relationships.

In 2007, he described himself as difficult and selfish with considerable precision.

In 2018, the conclusion is even harsher:

decades of self-analysis have not necessarily produced decades of self-improvement.

This is a fundamental distinction.

**Understanding yourself is not the same as changing yourself.**

### The Archive as a Mirror

There is something almost cruel about reading twenty-one years of one's own interviews.

Ordinary memory allows people to revise themselves.

Old interviews do not.

They preserve previous ambitions, complaints and certainties.

Teoman can compare what the younger man said he would become with what actually happened.

The archive becomes evidence.

### Which Song Is Teoman?

Cinmen asks him to choose a song from the twenty-six tracks that best represents his life.

Teoman first points toward *İstasyon İnsanları* when thinking about childhood.

But for his life as a whole, he identifies *Hem Hayattan, Hem Ölümden Korkarak* as particularly representative.

The title alone is revealing.

Fear does not appear only on one side.

Life itself can be frightening.

Death can be frightening.

The person remains suspended between them.

### The Songs Are Autobiographical

Teoman says that much of his catalogue describes his own life.

His goal was generally not to write vague lyrics capable of fitting everyone equally.

He preferred concrete events, emotions and details.

He did not want to make himself look better than he was.

This is consistent with his songwriting philosophy from much earlier interviews.

Reality becomes convincing through specificity.

### Against Generic Songs

As a listener, Teoman had often disliked songs that seemed designed primarily to entertain without revealing anything particular.

He wanted another kind of writing.

A Teoman song should contain something that appears to have happened somewhere.

A room.

A street.

A mistake.

A person.

A remembered sentence.

A weakness.

The details create identity.

### Fifty

Age becomes one of the central subjects of the conversation.

Physically, Teoman says his medical results are surprisingly good.

Psychologically, however, he feels old.

The difference is important.

He does not define aging primarily through the body.

He defines it through diminishing meaning and pleasure.

Things that once produced excitement no longer have the same power.

### Old at Thirty-Two, Old at Fifty

This connects directly with the 1999 *Aklım Kendimde* interview.

At thirty-two, Teoman already contrasted the passionate child with the older self who had lost some of that intensity.

Almost nineteen years later, he is describing the same process more severely.

The problem was never simply wrinkles or physical decline.

For Teoman, aging means:

**the disappearance of fascination.**

### Living in the Past

Teoman recognizes that his current projects are oriented backward.

*Koyu Antoloji* revisits old songs.

The press archive revisits old interviews.

His book revisits old memories.

Rather than creating an entirely new future, he is organizing his past.

He explicitly connects this behavior with feeling psychologically old.

He is trying to carry the past into the future.

### An Artist Becoming His Own Archivist

This represents a major shift.

The young Teoman wanted to produce.

The middle-aged Teoman increasingly wants to preserve.

The distinction matters.

Creation asks:

**What should I make next?**

Archiving asks:

**What from what I already made deserves to survive?**

By 2018, the second question has become increasingly important.

### Koyu Antoloji as Legacy Project

The album should therefore not be understood merely as another greatest-hits collection.

Teoman deliberately chooses songs that show his songwriting style, including tracks that had been commercially overshadowed by larger hits.

He rearranges them so that they form a coherent dark sequence.

This makes *Koyu Antoloji* closer to a curated self-portrait.

It is Teoman interpreting Teoman.

### The Hits Are Not Necessarily the Important Songs

This is an important artistic distinction.

Commercial success determines which songs become famous.

It does not necessarily determine which songs the songwriter values most.

Some less popular tracks may contain more of what Teoman considers his real writing style.

The anthology allows him to correct the imbalance created by the market.

### No Desire for New Songs

One of the most striking statements concerns songwriting.

Teoman says he is not particularly interested in writing new songs.

After decades of work, the motivation has weakened.

This is not exactly the same as the dramatic retirement of 2011.

He has already returned to music.

The problem is now narrower.

He can continue being a musician while losing the desire to continuously expand the catalogue.

### The Burden of the Existing Catalogue

The larger the catalogue becomes, the harder it becomes to write another song.

Every new composition exists beside everything already written.

Repetition becomes easier.

Surprise becomes harder.

The songwriter is competing not only with other musicians but with his younger self.

That competition can be exhausting.

### The Future Is Frightening

Teoman's pessimism extends beyond his own career.

He is afraid of the future.

The condition of the world does not encourage optimism.

This produces another reason to look backward.

Memory appears safer than expectation.

The past has already happened.

The future can still go wrong.

### Memory as Shelter

This creates one of the central emotional structures of the 2018 Teoman.

Young Teoman looked toward future achievement.

Middle-aged Teoman increasingly looks backward toward memory.

The direction of desire has changed.

The future once contained possibility.

Now the past contains meaning.

### Love

The conversation also enters Teoman's attitude toward love.

He is increasingly skeptical about how easily the word is used.

Celebrity culture can describe a relationship of ten days as a new great love.

Teoman resists this inflation.

For him, love is difficult to identify.

He says only a small number of his relationships deserve the word.

### Pain as Evidence

His unusual test is what happens when the relationship ends.

A painful separation may reveal that something important existed.

If leaving is emotionally easy, perhaps the relationship was never what the participants imagined.

This is characteristically Teoman-like reasoning.

The reality of love is discovered through its wound.

### Relationships as Painkillers

The conversation becomes more interesting when Cinmen asks whether people sometimes move rapidly between relationships or sexual encounters to escape loneliness.

Teoman agrees that this can happen.

Another person can temporarily function like a painkiller.

Company postpones the feeling.

Sex provides contact.

Affection reduces loneliness for a while.

But if the underlying wound remains, repetition does not solve it.

### Intimacy Without Cure

This is a much older and less romantic Teoman discussing relationships.

Sex is not presented as scandalous freedom.

It is sometimes simply a strategy for regulating loneliness.

Human beings seek one another because isolation becomes difficult.

But another body cannot automatically repair the psychological problem that created the need.

The relief may be temporary.

### The Womanizer Grows Old

This section is particularly interesting when compared with Teoman's early media image.

In 1999 and 2001, newspapers were fascinated by the women around him.

The public persona became associated with romantic and sexual freedom.

By 2018, Teoman discusses the same behavior with almost clinical distance.

What once looked glamorous can also be repetitive avoidance.

### Childhood Still Wins

Again and again, the conversation returns indirectly to childhood.

Teoman increasingly believes that some of the basic emotional patterns established early in life are extremely difficult to escape.

This echoes the 2007 discussion of losing his father, growing up around mourning and experiencing loneliness as the dominant childhood theme.

At fifty, these are no longer simply biographical facts.

They have become explanatory models.

### The Danger of Explaining Everything

But there is another problem.

If childhood explains everything, adult life can begin to look predetermined.

Teoman seems aware of this danger.

He tries to control his thinking.

He tries not to focus only on problems.

He does not completely surrender to the idea that personality can never change.

The effort itself matters even when progress feels small.

### From Creation to Preservation

The deepest transformation visible in the interview concerns time.

In 1996, Teoman stood before his first album.

Everything pointed forward.

In 2000, success accelerated the future.

In 2012, he stopped because the future had become repetition.

In 2018, he is actively organizing the past.

The direction of artistic attention has reversed.

### Why This Interview Matters

This is one of the most important middle-period documents in the archive because Teoman is simultaneously examining his work and his life.

Several long-running themes reach a new stage:

- melancholy is no longer merely creative material but something he actively tries to escape,
- self-awareness has not produced all the personal change he once expected,
- aging is defined through diminishing fascination rather than physical decline,
- songwriting motivation has weakened,
- romantic behavior once associated with freedom can also function as avoidance,
- childhood remains psychologically powerful,
- the future increasingly produces fear,
- and preservation is becoming as important as creation.

*Koyu Antoloji* therefore represents more than a musical project.

It is part of a larger archival instinct.

Teoman is beginning to organize what he wants to leave behind.

The ambition expressed in 2007 — that his songs should survive him — is now becoming practical work.

He is selecting them.

Rearranging them.

Contextualizing them.

Writing his memories.

Collecting his interviews.

The songwriter is gradually becoming the archivist of his own life.

And perhaps the central question of the 2018 Teoman is no longer:

**What will I become?**

It is:

**What should remain?**
