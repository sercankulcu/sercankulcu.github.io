## Türk Rock Müziğinin Romantik ve Asi Çocuğu Teoman

**Aujourd’hui la Turquie — February 2010**  
**Interview by Ayça Yüksel**

This interview captures Teoman at an important transitional moment.

*İnsanlık Halleri* had been released recently. The music industry was changing rapidly because of digital distribution and falling physical sales. Teoman had already accumulated more than a decade of fame and was increasingly interested in cinema, literature and questions beyond conventional album promotion.

At the same time, the interview shows a musician who still thinks carefully about melody, language and the cultural sources of his work.

### France Without French

The interview begins with Teoman's affection for French culture.

He admits something slightly paradoxical:

he does not speak French.

Yet he feels strongly attracted to French music, cinema, literature and visual culture.

This is a useful example of cultural influence operating beyond direct linguistic understanding.

A musical language can affect someone through melody, mood, arrangement and aesthetics even when the words themselves are inaccessible.

### Chanson

Teoman specifically mentions French chanson.

He describes its melodic character as particularly close to his own taste.

This is significant because Teoman is usually discussed primarily through Anglo-American rock influences.

The interview reveals another layer.

His melodic sensibility is not formed only by rock.

French popular music also matters.

### Çoban Yıldızı

The discussion turns to *Çoban Yıldızı*, the first video from *İnsanlık Halleri*.

Teoman explains that the song's music belongs to French singer-songwriter Calogero's *Danser Encore*.

He liked the melody so much that he translated the emotional world of the piece into his own Turkish lyric rather than simply reproducing the original words.

This is an interesting example of adaptation rather than straightforward translation.

The melody travels.

The story changes.

### Translating Emotion, Not Words

Teoman had the French lyrics translated into English so that he could understand their meaning.

But he did not simply convert those lyrics into Turkish.

Instead, he wrote another story around the same musical structure.

This is important because it shows how he thinks about artistic borrowing.

The goal is not literal equivalence.

The goal is to discover what the music suggests to him.

### War and the Child

The story Teoman writes for *Çoban Yıldızı* concerns a child affected by war.

He says the image came partly from the First World War.

The song's narrator tries to describe how war destroys the life of a child.

This gives the song a political dimension, but Teoman is careful about how he describes that politics.

He does not primarily want to write ideological songs.

He wants to write about human consequences.

### Politics Through Individuals

Teoman says:

> “Ben politikanın müziğe hiç yakışmadığını düşünüyorum.”

This does not mean he has no political views.

In fact, the interview shows that he has very clear ones.

What he rejects is turning songs into ideological statements.

His preferred method is to approach political reality through individuals.

A child.

A death.

A damaged life.

A concrete human experience.

### The Kurdish Question

The most politically direct section concerns the Kurdish issue.

Teoman says that the Republic's nation-building project made serious mistakes and that Kurds were marginalized and assimilated.

He also speaks critically about the violence experienced in southeastern Turkey, especially after the 1980s.

Yet he deliberately shifts away from ideological camps.

His focus is the suffering produced by the conflict.

Young people die.

Families are damaged.

Entire communities live under pressure.

And, in his view, the damage eventually affects everyone in the country.

### Sociology Still Matters

This section makes clear that Teoman's sociology education never completely disappeared from his thinking.

He does not approach social problems only as emotional topics.

He thinks in terms of structures, historical projects and social consequences.

When asked why he did not continue professionally in sociology, however, his answer is simple.

He did not want an academic career.

Music remained the stronger desire.

### Women's Studies

The interview also confirms his graduate work in Women's Studies.

This is valuable because the subject had already appeared in 1999 interviews, but here it is independently documented again in a primary source more than ten years later.

It strengthens the biographical reliability of the earlier material.

Teoman's intellectual interests were not invented retrospectively.

They were part of his actual academic history.

### The Music Industry Is Changing

One of the most historically interesting sections concerns the collapse of the traditional music business.

Teoman describes the transition from cassette to CD and then toward MP3 and digital distribution.

Each technological change affects the economic structure of the industry.

Physical sales decline.

Musicians and record companies have to rethink how music reaches listeners.

This is not merely a technical issue.

It changes which artists can survive commercially.

### The End of the Old Model

Teoman recognizes that the old system cannot continue unchanged.

Record sales had once provided a major part of the industry's financial structure.

Digital copying and new distribution methods weaken that model.

This means artists increasingly depend on concerts and other forms of income.

Looking back, the interview is a useful snapshot of the Turkish music industry at the exact moment when streaming culture was beginning to replace physical media.

### A Loyal Audience

Teoman says he is relatively protected from some of these changes because he already has a strong audience.

A new musician faces a more difficult environment.

This is an important sign of how far his career has moved.

The unknown artist of 1996 is now an established musician whose name itself provides economic stability.

### Literature Inside Music

Near the end of the interview, Teoman returns to one of his longest-running themes.

He wants his songs to have literary content.

Melody matters.

But words must matter too.

He explicitly says that lyrics are just as important as melody.

This is entirely consistent with what he had been saying for more than a decade.

The emphasis on language is one of the most stable elements in Teoman's artistic identity.

### Turkish Rock

Teoman is also asked about his place in Turkish rock.

By 2010, he recognizes that he has become one of the important figures in the genre.

But he does not present himself as someone who invented Turkish rock.

He places himself within a longer tradition.

There were earlier musicians.

There were older masters.

There were foreign influences.

His own significance comes from finding a recognizable position inside that history.

### Influence Without Anxiety

This is another sign of artistic maturity.

The young artist often worries about originality.

The established artist can acknowledge influences without feeling threatened by them.

Teoman does not need to pretend that his music appeared from nowhere.

What matters is whether those influences have been transformed into a personal voice.

### Cinema

The interview also reminds us that by this point Teoman's artistic identity extends beyond music.

He had already written and directed *Balans ve Manevra* and appeared in several films.

Cinema gives him another way to tell stories that music cannot fully contain.

This continues the narrative ambitions visible since *Gönülçelen*.

Teoman is increasingly interested in characters, scenes and storytelling structures that exceed the limits of the three- or four-minute song.

### More Than a Rock Singer

The overall portrait is therefore broader than the title suggests.

Teoman may be introduced as the romantic and rebellious child of Turkish rock, but the interview itself reveals someone difficult to reduce to that label.

He is simultaneously:

- a songwriter,
- a reader,
- a sociologist by education,
- a filmmaker,
- a cultural observer,
- a musician shaped by both Anglo-American and French influences,
- and a public figure increasingly interested in social questions.

### Why This Interview Matters

This interview is valuable for several reasons.

First, it is one of the strongest primary-source documents in the archive.

Second, it records Teoman at the end of the 2000s, just before the period in which his dissatisfaction with music and fame would become much more dramatic.

Third, it brings together several threads that had appeared separately in earlier interviews:

- the importance of lyrics,
- French cultural influence,
- adaptation rather than imitation,
- political sensitivity without ideological songwriting,
- sociology,
- Women's Studies,
- the transformation of the music industry,
- cinema,
- and the search for a personal artistic position.

Most importantly, it shows Teoman becoming increasingly difficult to define through a single category.

He began as a rock musician.

By 2010, that description is no longer enough.

He is thinking simultaneously about music, literature, cinema, society and the changing conditions of cultural production.

The “rock star” identity still exists.

But it is beginning to look too small for the person inside it.
