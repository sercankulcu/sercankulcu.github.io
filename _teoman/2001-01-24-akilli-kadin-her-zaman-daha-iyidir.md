## Akıllı Kadın Her Zaman Daha İyidir

**Sabah Gazetesi — 24 January 2001**  
**Interview by Deniz Akkaya**

This interview captures Teoman at a moment when success has already changed his position in Turkish popular culture.

Deniz Akkaya had met him roughly two years earlier, before the full force of his fame had arrived. When they meet again, she describes essentially the same person, but with something new: the confidence produced by two highly successful years.

The conversation is playful, provocative and sometimes deliberately exaggerated.

But beneath that tone, several themes are important: Teoman's attitude toward pop culture, his discomfort with empty celebrity, his views on relationships and intelligence, and his awareness that success cannot continue forever.

### Success Has Changed His Confidence

One of the first observations belongs not to Teoman but to Deniz Akkaya.

She remembers an earlier Teoman as idealistic, funny and sincere.

Two years later, those qualities remain, but success has given him greater confidence.

This is a useful external observation.

The Teoman of 1996 often sounds like someone defending the right to make his own music.

The Teoman of 2001 no longer needs to imagine whether that music will find an audience.

It already has.

The uncertainty of the beginning has been replaced by the psychological consequences of achievement.

### A Career That Was Once Only a Hobby

Teoman again describes the uncertainty of his youth.

He imagined many possible professions.

Music was originally one possibility among several, almost a hobby.

This repeats a theme from earlier interviews: his career did not emerge from a perfectly organized childhood plan.

The musician who later developed an extremely recognizable artistic identity first spent years considering other lives.

That makes his later confidence more interesting.

It was constructed rather than inherited.

### Arabesque

The conversation moves into musical categories.

Teoman says arabesque is not especially close to the culture from which he comes, although he does not dismiss every musician associated with it.

This distinction matters.

He separates personal cultural identification from absolute aesthetic judgment.

A genre may not represent his world without being entirely without value.

His relationship with Turkish classical music is more skeptical.

He struggles to find its equivalent in ordinary contemporary life.

His criticism is essentially sociological: he is interested in whether a musical language corresponds to the real emotional and social conditions of the people singing it.

### Music and Social Reality

This gives us an interesting connection to Teoman's sociology background.

He does not discuss musical genres only according to melody or arrangement.

He asks what social world produces them.

Arabesque, for him, has a recognizable social origin and expresses particular forms of aspiration and pain.

His objection to some other musical traditions is that their emotional vocabulary can seem disconnected from the actual life of the modern urban listener.

Whether one agrees with this judgment or not, the method is revealing.

Teoman hears music partly as a social document.

### “I Make Pop Music”

One of the most useful statements in this interview concerns pop.

Teoman distinguishes between **pop music** and the cultural category of the **“popçu.”**

He openly says that he makes pop music.

But he does not consider himself a “popçu.”

The difference, for him, seems to involve craft, seriousness and musical legitimacy rather than simple popularity.

He criticizes people who enter the music industry primarily through connections, appearance, dancing or other advantages without having sufficient musical ability.

### The Rock–Pop Boundary Becomes Even Weaker

This develops the position seen in the December 2000 interview.

By now, Teoman is increasingly comfortable admitting that his work belongs to popular music.

The defensive need to insist on “rock” purity is fading.

This is significant.

Teoman can use guitars, write dark lyrics and belong culturally to Turkish rock while still recognizing that the songs themselves operate within popular music.

Genre no longer needs to function as a certificate of authenticity.

### Celebrity Culture

The interview repeatedly makes fun of Turkish celebrity culture.

Questions about magazines, nudity, television personalities and fashionable public debates are handled with irony.

Teoman rarely treats the celebrity world with solemnity.

But he is also undeniably part of it.

This creates one of his characteristic contradictions.

He mocks the cultural system that turns musicians into celebrities while benefiting from the enormous visibility that same system provides.

His solution is not escape.

It is irony.

### Intelligence in Relationships

The title of the interview comes from a deliberately simple question:

intelligent woman, beautiful woman or rich woman?

For a long-term relationship, Teoman chooses intelligence.

> “Akıllı kadın her zaman iyidir.”

The answer is partly humorous, but the qualification **long-term relationship** is important.

Physical attraction may matter immediately.

Money may make life easier.

But sustained companionship requires something more durable.

For Teoman, intelligence appears to be part of that durability.

### Beauty Is Not Enough

This fits a larger pattern in his interviews.

Teoman is frequently portrayed by entertainment media through women and physical attraction.

Yet when he speaks about relationships in more reflective contexts, he repeatedly introduces other criteria:

conversation,

intelligence,

psychological compatibility,

independence,

and the difficulty of living with another person.

This contrast between the media image of the womanizer and Teoman's more complicated descriptions of relationships will become increasingly important in later interviews.

### Humor as Distance

A great deal of the conversation is intentionally provocative.

Teoman makes jokes about politics, television, popular musicians and himself.

Taken literally, some statements can sound harsher than they function inside the conversation.

Their real role is often to produce distance.

This is the same mechanism Teoman described in the Best FM interview:

melancholy becomes easier when irony is introduced.

The principle extends beyond sadness.

Irony also allows him to participate in celebrity culture without fully surrendering to its seriousness.

### The Possibility of Decline

The most important statement may actually appear near the end.

Teoman is asked about the possibility that his star will eventually fade.

His response is calm.

He assumes that it will happen.

No one can continue rising forever.

This is striking because he is discussing decline while still very close to the beginning of his major success.

He does not imagine fame as permanent.

### “You Cannot Keep Going Up Forever”

This awareness becomes an important recurring theme in later Teoman.

Success contains its own ending.

Popularity will change.

Audiences will move.

The cultural center will shift.

A younger generation will arrive.

Teoman seems to understand this surprisingly early.

The question therefore becomes not how to avoid decline forever, but how to live with the knowledge that decline is structurally unavoidable.

### Dying at the Top

His humorous answer is that although one cannot rise forever, perhaps one can manage to die at the top.

The line is dramatic, but it reveals an anxiety that will return repeatedly throughout his career:

**how should an artist end?**

Should one continue indefinitely?

Should one leave before becoming irrelevant?

Should one deliberately withdraw?

More than ten years later, Teoman would actually announce that he was leaving music.

Seen retrospectively, the concern was not new.

The possibility of ending had already entered his thinking during the height of success.

### Why This Interview Matters

This interview is less philosophically concentrated than some of the 1999 conversations, but it is valuable as a document of Teoman becoming completely comfortable inside his public identity.

We see:

- confidence produced by success,
- a relaxed acceptance that he makes popular music,
- skepticism toward artificial distinctions between rock and pop,
- criticism of celebrity culture from someone living inside it,
- intelligence presented as a central quality in long-term relationships,
- irony functioning as a defense against seriousness,
- and an unexpectedly early awareness that fame must eventually decline.

Perhaps the most significant shift concerns popular culture.

The 1996 Teoman feared losing artistic intensity by trying to reach everyone.

The 2000 Teoman discovered that popularity itself was not the problem.

The early 2001 Teoman goes one step further:

**he can openly describe his music as pop without feeling that the word threatens his identity.**

At the same time, he is already thinking about what happens when the success ends.

The career is rising.

But Teoman has begun imagining the descent.
