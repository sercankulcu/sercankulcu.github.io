Move from HB to 4B and the pencil stops feeling like merely a darker version of the same instrument. Its behavior changes.

A 4B core deposits graphite quickly. Broad shadows appear with less pressure, and the side of the point becomes a useful drawing surface. Line width changes rapidly as the point wears. Instead of fighting that wear, artists can use it.

The darkness also reveals paper texture. Tooth catches the graphite, producing grain and variation that a harder pencil might barely show. Smooth paper, by contrast, can create dense metallic-looking areas when enough graphite accumulates.

For writing, 4B can be wonderfully expressive but impractical for very small notes. It blunts quickly and may smudge. A writer with large handwriting may enjoy it more than someone filling narrow margins.

As a collector, I like comparing 4B across brands because formulation differences become obvious. Some feel powdery, some waxy, some extremely smooth, and some retain surprising control despite their softness.

A 4B pencil is a reminder that the graphite scale is not just about darkness. Each region of the scale changes how the tool interacts with hand and paper.
