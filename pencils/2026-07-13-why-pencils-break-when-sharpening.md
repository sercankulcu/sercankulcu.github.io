Few pencil problems are more frustrating than a point that breaks every time it emerges from the sharpener. The visible break may happen at the blade, but the real damage can have occurred much earlier.

A pencil dropped onto a hard floor can develop internal fractures in the graphite core. The wood may look perfect while the core is broken at several points along the barrel. When sharpening finally reaches one of those fractures, the point falls away.

A dull or damaged sharpener can make the problem worse. More pressure is required, the pencil flexes, and the blade may scrape rather than cut. An off-center core also exposes one side too early and reduces support.

Before blaming the pencil, I test a second sharpener. If possible I use a sharp single-blade model and rotate the pencil gently without pushing inward. For an old or suspect pencil, careful knife sharpening can reveal whether the core is already fractured.

Good manufacturing helps. Proper bonding between core and wood can reduce the consequences of impacts, although no wooden pencil is indestructible.

Repeated breakage is therefore a useful diagnostic clue. It can tell you about shipping, storage, sharpening technique, blade condition, core centering, or manufacturing quality. The broken point is only the final symptom.
