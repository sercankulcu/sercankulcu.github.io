When a new pencil enters my collection, I try not to judge it from a single scribble. I use the same small routine so that comparisons remain meaningful.

First I inspect the unsharpened pencil. I look at paint coverage, printing, barrel straightness, end finish, core centering, and any visible damage. Then I sharpen it by hand. A clean, continuous shaving is a good sign; tearing, splintering, or an obviously off-center core deserves attention.

Next comes writing. I write normal sentences rather than only drawing dark swatches. This reveals how the point changes during real use. I note darkness, feedback, smoothness, grip comfort, and how frequently I feel the need to rotate the pencil.

Then I make controlled lines and shaded patches. I check how easily the pencil moves from light pressure to dark pressure, how much graphite smudges, and whether the core produces unexpected gritty spots.

Finally I sharpen it again. Repeated sharpening can reveal problems that the first point hides. A pencil should behave consistently along its length.

I keep the test simple because the purpose is not to turn every pencil into a laboratory experiment. The routine merely gives me a vocabulary. Instead of saying "I like this pencil," I can explain why—and that makes the collection more interesting.
