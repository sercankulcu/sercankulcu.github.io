Every sharpening shortens a wooden pencil. That makes it an accidental measuring instrument for work.

A new pencil begins with unused length and a perfect imprint. Weeks later the brand name may be partly gone, the barrel fits differently in the hand, and the balance has shifted. Eventually it becomes too short for comfortable use.

I like this because progress becomes physical. Pages of notes are not only stored in a notebook; some of them are also missing from the pencil.

Artists sometimes preserve stubs from completed projects. Writers keep the final centimeters of a favorite pencil. Those fragments are small records of time and attention.

Mechanical pencils hide this process by replacing the lead. Wooden pencils display it. Consumption is part of their design rather than a flaw.

Perhaps that is why finishing a pencil feels unexpectedly satisfying. Very few everyday objects invite us to use them completely. A pencil asks for exactly that.
