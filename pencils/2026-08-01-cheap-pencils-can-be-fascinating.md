A collection made only from expensive pencils would miss much of pencil history.

Cheap pencils reveal what manufacturers can produce under tight cost constraints. Wood choice, paint coverage, core diameter, ferrule design, printing, packaging, and quality control all become exercises in optimization.

Sometimes the results are poor: crooked cores, gritty graphite, splintering wood, weak erasers. But sometimes an inexpensive pencil writes far better than expected. Those discoveries are part of the fun.

Budget pencils are also culturally specific. A school pencil from Turkey, India, Mexico, Japan, or the United States can show different assumptions about color, erasers, grades, packaging, and classroom habits.

They are easier to use without anxiety. I can sharpen them repeatedly, test different knives, compare papers, and give them to other people. That practical freedom generates information that a sealed rare pencil cannot.

Collecting should not become a price competition. An unusual five-cent pencil can be more interesting than a luxury box if it fills a gap in the story you are building.
